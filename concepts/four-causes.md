## Gloss / Divergence map

**Aitia** (αἰτία/αἴτιον, pl. *aitiai*): Aristotle's four kinds of explanation. The Greek *aitia* is broader than the modern "cause"; the closest rendering is "explanatory factor" — whatever answers the question "why?" (*dia ti*) about a natural thing or change.

The **four causes** (Physics II.3; Metaphysics V.2):

| cause | Greek | question answered | bronze statue example |
|---|---|---|---|
| material cause | *hyle* | "what is it made of?" | the bronze |
| formal cause | *morphe* / *eidos* | "what is its form / essence?" | the shape of the statue |
| efficient cause | — | "what initiates the change?" | the sculptor's art |
| final cause | *telos* | "for what end/purpose?" | the finished statue / honoring the subject |

Together they give a complete explanation. For Aristotle, a natural explanation that mentions only material causes (as Democritean atomism does) is incomplete — it says *of what* but not *what, by what, for what*.

**The final cause (telos) is the distinctive commitment.** Aristotle grounds biological and physical explanation teleologically: the function that a thing serves is part of *what it is*. Importantly, this teleology does not require a conscious intender — sharp teeth exist *for* biting whether or not teeth "plan." This marks a fundamental difference from modern efficient-causal-only science and from all Indian causal theories: no Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Buddhist, or Jain account of causation has an Aristotelian telos.

**Physics II.3 note.** Aristotle says the four causes are "nearly as many as the ways in which 'why' is asked"; they often coincide (e.g., formal and final cause are the same in natural objects — the *what* and the *for-what* of a healthy human body are identical).
