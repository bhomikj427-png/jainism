## Gloss / Divergence map

The ***Tattvacintāmaṇi*** — "Jewel of Reflection on the Truth," also ***Pramāṇacintāmaṇi***, "Jewel of Reflection on the Means of Valid Knowledge" — is [[gangesha|Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya]]'s single work and the foundational text of [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]]. Four *khaṇḍa*s, one per Nyāya knowledge-source: **pratyakṣa** (perception), **anumāna** (inference), **upamāna** (analogy), **śabda** (testimony).

**Converged** — the sources agree on what the book is, how it is organised, what it is for, and what it did to the literature around it. They disagree only on its date, and that disagreement is fully inherited from its author's (see the strata analysis in `gangesha.md`); it is not a separate dispute about the book.

⚠ **Disambiguation, required by the filename.** *Tattvacintāmaṇi* is not a unique title. Wisdomlib's lexicon entry lists at least four works under it: this one; a **Tantric** *Tattvacintāmaṇi* by **Pūrṇānanda (1577)**; and two **jyotiṣa** works, by **Divākara** and by **Lakṣmīdāsa Miśra**. *Cintāmaṇi* ("wish-fulfilling jewel") is a stock title-element across Sanskrit genres. **This key denotes the Nyāya work only.**

### What kind of book it is

Three things about its construction are unusual and all three are load-bearing:

1. **It treats only the *pramāṇas*, and deliberately not the *prameyas*.** Hindupedia is explicit that Gaṅgeśa "dwelt with only the four *pramāṇas* or means of knowledge" and excluded the objects of knowledge. The [[nyaya-sutra|*Nyāyasūtra*]]'s sixteen categories open with *pramāṇa* and *prameya* as the first two; the *Tattvacintāmaṇi* takes the first and drops the second. **A book that declines half its inherited subject-matter is making a claim about what philosophy now is** — epistemology first, and ontology only as epistemology requires it. SEP's remark that God's existence and the self as substance are handled *inside* the inference chapter is the same fact from the other side, and the older Wikipedia stratum puts it bluntly: "other subjects, such as the proofs of God, are treated **incidentally**."
2. **Its plan is borrowed from the opposition.** Ganeri: the one-section-per-knowledge-source arrangement is "a pattern employed by the Buddhist logician [[dignaga-pramana|Dignāga]], and copied by Gaṅgeśa." The book that consolidated the school which spent centuries refuting Buddhist logicians is laid out on a Buddhist logician's plan.
3. **Its method is serial demolition.** On the definition of pervasion — the passage the whole later tradition lives in — Gaṅgeśa states candidate definitions, refutes them one after another, and only then gives his own. ⚠ **The count is disputed between the two best sources**: SEP says he rejects **twenty-four** attempts; Ganeri says **twenty-one** rejected (the first five being the celebrated *vyāpti-pañcaka*, two more the "Lion and Tiger" definitions of his Navya-Nyāya predecessors) and **seven** accepted. **Not reconciled here.**

### The argument at its centre

Ganeri reconstructs Gaṅgeśa's accepted definition (the ***siddhānta-lakṣaṇa***) and, in doing so, supplies the single most useful thing the corpus has for stating the Navya-Nyāya / modern-logic comparison **without inflating it**.

The problem: the natural definition of pervasion is *"there is no place where H is present and S absent."* This fails twice. It fails for **universally positive** properties (*kevalānvayin*) — if S is nameability, then "absence of nameability" is an uninstantiated term and the definition contains a non-referring expression. And it fails for **partially located** properties, where the presence-range and absence-range of one property overlap.

Gaṅgeśa's solution quantifies over *properties* instead: pervasion holds when **no property whose absence is co-located with H can be identical with S**, plus a co-location clause to rule out uninstantiated terms. Ganeri: "Gaṅgeśa's trick implicitly trades on the theorem A → B ≡ ¬(A & ¬B)" — the definition says what "there is no counter-example" says, "but it does so **without actually using the potentially non-referring phrase 'absence of S'**."

⚠⚠ **And then Ganeri asks the right question and gives the answer this corpus should record.** Why, if pervasion just means "all H are S," did the Naiyāyikas not simply use universal quantification?

> "The answer, perhaps, is that they were in fact **trying to define this notion**, and to do so only in terms of certain other notions which they took to be primitive, especially the notion of **co-location and absence**."

**This reframes the whole comparison.** The *vyāpti* definitions are not a laborious approach to a quantifier the Naiyāyikas lacked. They are an **analysis** of the quantifier into two primitives — being in the same place, and not being there — chosen because a Nyāya ontology has loci and absences in it and does not have variables and binders. Where a modern reading says "they were reaching for ∀ and kept missing," the accurate statement is: **they were asking what ∀ is made of, in a metaphysics where the answer had to be made of things.** Any parallel drawn from this file to formal logic must be drawn *this* way. (Ganeri notes the reconstruction is not finished: the implicit quantification over the property P still has to be eliminated — "cf. Goekoop 1967.")

### What it did to the literature: a three-tier pyramid

The measurable fact about this book is not what it says but what grew on it. Appendix C of a Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika historical study lists **thirty commentaries**, and the shape of the list is the finding — they come in **tiers**:

| tier | commenting on | examples named |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | the *Tattvacintāmaṇi* itself | *Dīdhiti* ([[raghunatha-siromani|Raghunātha Śiromaṇi]]); *Āloka* (Pakṣadhara Miśra = Jayadeva); *Prakāśa* (**Vardhamāna Upādhyāya**, Gaṅgeśa's own son); *Prakāśa* (Rucidatta); *Mayūkha* (Śaṅkara Miśra); *Maṇi-vyākhyā* (Kaṇāda Tarkavāgīśa); an *Anumāna-khaṇḍa* by **Vācaspati Miśra** |
| **2** | the ***Dīdhiti*** | Mathurānātha Tarkavāgīśa; Bhavānanda Siddhāntavāgīśa; Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa; Raghudeva Nyāyālaṅkāra; and [[gadadhara|Gadādhara]] |
| **3** | the ***Āloka*** | *Āloka-viveka* (Jayarāma Nyāyālaṅkāra), and others |

⚠ **The 30 total is sourced; the even 10/10/10 split is as reported by an unproofread OCR page and is not relied on.** What *is* relied on is the tier structure, which two further sources confirm independently: Deb records that "after Raghunātha, many Navyanaiyāyikas commented on the *Dīdhiti* rather than Jayadeva's *Āloka*" — i.e. **tier 2 displaced tier 3 as the live front** — and search-level aggregation names Mathurānātha and Jagadīśa as commenting on Raghunātha's commentary, with Jagadīśa and Gadādhara "the last two notable works of this school."

**The object of study migrates upward.** By the 17th century the text being argued over is not Gaṅgeśa's book but a commentary on a commentary on it — which is why Ganeri's endpoint follows: the idiom became so inaccessible that manuals had to be written to explain the school to beginners, the best of them nicknamed ***Bāla-gādādharī***, "a sort of 'Beginner's Guide to Gadādhara'." **A beginner's guide named after a third-tier commentator is the most precise available statement of what happened to this literature.**

⚠ **A corpus-internal confirmation, unplanned.** The tier-1 list includes ***an Anumāna-khaṇḍa commentary by "Vācaspati Miśra."*** `vacaspati-mishra.md` logged a **name collision** in Batch 45 — that SEP's list of Naiyāyikas answering Śrīharṣa includes a **Vācaspati Miśra II** of Mithilā working *after* Gaṅgeśa, five centuries after Vācaspati Miśra I. A commentary on Gaṅgeśa **cannot** be by Vācaspati I, who predates him by four hundred years. **This is independent confirmation that the collision is real**, reached from a commentary census rather than from a list of Śrīharṣa's opponents.

⚠ **One search-result conflation caught and rejected.** Search-level aggregation returned "the Commentary Ṭīkā of Bhaṭṭa Arcaṭa and the Sub-Commentary *Āloka* of Durveka Miśra" as if it belonged to this text's commentarial chain. Arcaṭa and Durveka Miśra are commentators on **Dharmakīrti's *Hetubindu***, a Buddhist work; the *Āloka* title has collided in the index. **Not carried into this file.**

### Editions and translations

Well published, and the corpus can say so from records it read rather than from memory:

- **Kāmākhyānātha Tarkavāgīśa's edition**, Calcutta, Baptist Mission Press, **1888–1901**, *with extracts from the commentaries of Mathurānātha Tarkavāgīśa and of Jayadeva Miśra* — i.e. tiers 1–2 printed alongside the text. Archive.org holds vol. 3 pt. 4 (646 pp.); **Raghunātha Śiromaṇi is listed among the item's creators.**
- Further Calcutta prints on archive.org: Jīvānanda Vidyāsāgara's *Upamānakhaṇḍa* (1872); a *Śabdakhaṇḍa* (1900); an *Anumānakhaṇḍa* (1905); *Dīdhiti-vivṛti* volumes (1910–11).
- **N. S. Ramanuja Tatacharya**'s editions (1972, 1982, 1999), per SEP's bibliography.
- **A complete English translation exists**: **S. Phillips, *Jewel of Reflection on the Truth about Epistemology*, 3 vols, Bloomsbury, 2020.** Also **V. P. Bhatta** on the perception chapter (2012) and the testimony chapter (2005), and **John Vattanky** on the *Īśvaravāda* section (1984).

⚠ **None of these was read.** The 1888–1901 scan's OCR was downloaded and inspected: it is Devanāgarī misrecognised as Latin characters and is unusable. Per §1 this corpus does not translate from unedited scans; the editions are recorded as **located**, not as consulted. **Phillips 2020 is the single highest-value unread item in the entire Navya-Nyāya layer** — it would move this file, `gangesha.md`, `avacchedaka.md` and `vyapti.md` from second-hand exposition to primary-source grounding in one step.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Jonardon Ganeri, *Navya-Nyāya: Analytical Philosophy in Early Modern India*.** The *siddhānta-lakṣaṇa* reconstruction with the formal renderings V1/V2/V3, the two problems it solves, the "A → B ≡ ¬(A & ¬B)" observation, the Goekoop caveat, and — the passage this file most depends on — the co-location-and-absence-as-primitives explanation. Also the Dignāga book-plan and the *Bāla-gādādharī* nickname.

**Signal 2 — SEP, "Gaṅgeśa."** Independent: the four chapters, the twenty-four rejected definitions, the "monkey-conjunction in a tree" case, God and self treated inside the inference chapter, the reception, and the modern editions/translations list.

**Signal 3 — Appendix C, "Detailed list of Commentaries on Tattva Chintamani," in a *Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika critical and historical study*** (Wisdomlib OCR). The thirty-commentary census and the tier structure. ⚠ Page marked **unproofread**; author names are visibly corrupted by OCR ("Pucidatta" for Rucidatta, "Tirkika-siromanî" for Tārkika-śiromaṇi, "Cangesa" for Gaṅgeśa). **Used for the shape of the list, not for orthography or exact counts per tier.**

**Signal 4 — Deb, *The Navya-Nyāya Theory of Pakṣatā***, independently: Yājñapati → Jayadeva (*Āloka*) → Rucidatta, and the *Dīdhiti*-displaces-*Āloka* observation.

**Signal 5 — Wisdomlib lexicon entry, "Tattvacintāmaṇi"**: the homonymous Tantric and jyotiṣa works; c. 1200 for this one (the stratum `gangesha.md` analyses).

**Signal 6 — archive.org metadata**, read directly, for the 1888–1901 edition and the other Calcutta prints.

**Independence.** Signals 1, 2, 3 and 4 are four different authors with four different purposes — an analytical exposition, an encyclopedia entry, a historical appendix, and a monograph on one technical term — and they **agree on the tier structure of the commentaries and on the book's plan** while disagreeing on the definition-count. Signal 3's confirmation of the Vācaspati Miśra collision is the strongest single check in the file, because that appendix has no interest in the collision and `vacaspati-mishra.md` was written from a completely different source.

**Why converged.** Every substantive question about the book — its title, its structure, its scope, its method, its reception, its commentarial shape — is answered the same way by sources that do not depend on each other. The one open number (21 vs 24) is a detail inside an agreed picture, and the date question belongs to the author, not the book. **Why medium, not high.** **Not one line of the text was read.** Everything here is exposition of exposition; Phillips's complete translation exists and was not consulted; the commentary census comes from an explicitly unproofread OCR page; and this file's central quotation is Ganeri explaining Gaṅgeśa, not Gaṅgeśa.
