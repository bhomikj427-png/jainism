## Gloss / Divergence map

The ***Vākyapadīya*** — "on sentence and word" — is [[bhartrhari|Bhartṛhari]]'s verse treatise, and **the text in which the grammatical tradition becomes a philosophy.** Everything the corpus attributes to Bhartṛhari as doctrine — [[sphota|sphoṭa]] generalised, [[sabdabrahman|śabdabrahman]], sentence-holism, *pratibhā* — is stated here.

Abhyankar's judgement on its opening section is worth having, both for the claim and for its modesty: the kāṇḍa "deals with Sphoṭa, and in a way it contains **in a nutshell the philosophy of Sanskrit Grammar**." ⚠ *The headword is lost at a column break in the scan; the description — sphoṭa plus the philosophy of grammar in miniature — fits the* **Brahmakāṇḍa***, and is reported here with that uncertainty rather than assigned confidently.*

### Structure

| kāṇḍa | names | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | ***Brahmakāṇḍa***, also *Āgamasamuccaya* | The metaphysics: Brahman as *śabda*, and sphoṭa. Opens "The *Brahman* is without beginning and end, whose essence is the Word, who is the cause of the manifested phonemes" |
| **II** | ***Vākyakāṇḍa*** | The sentence: its indivisibility, and the rejection of word-composition accounts of sentence-meaning |
| **III** | ***Padakāṇḍa***, also ***Prakīrṇaka*** ("miscellany") | The word, in **fourteen sections called *samuddeśa*** — *Jāti*, *Dravya*, *Sambandha*, *Bhūyodravya*, *Guṇa*, *Dik*, *Sādhana*, and others |

⚠ **Look at what kāṇḍa III is organised on.** *Jāti* (universal), *dravya* (substance), *guṇa* (quality), *dik* (direction), *kriyā* (action), *kāla* (time), *sambandha* (relation) — **these are not grammatical categories, they are the [[vaiseshika-sutra|Vaiśeṣika]] ontological ones**, several of which this corpus already holds as nodes ([[jati]], [[dravya-vaisheshika]], [[kala-dravya|kāla]]). A grammarian's treatise on *words* is structured as a treatise on *what there is*.

**This is the concrete form of the claim [[vyakarana]] records as contested** — that grammar is a *darśana*. Bhartṛhari does not merely assert the promotion; he **writes the book that earns it**, taking up the categories a philosophical school would be expected to handle and treating each as a problem about how language carves the world. **Whether that makes vyākaraṇa a darśana or makes Bhartṛhari a philosopher who happened to be a grammarian is precisely what the sources split on.**

### ⚠ A verse count that does not fit the structure

**IEP** and **Wikipedia** both give the work as "containing **about 635 verses**." ⚠ **The corpus flags this rather than repeating it**, on internal-consistency grounds (§4 signal 4): a work whose *third* section alone runs to **fourteen** sub-sections, each substantial enough to be separately edited and commented on, is unlikely to total 635 verses across all three. The figure has the look of a count for **kāṇḍas I–II only**.

⚠ **And the two sources are not independent on this point** — the identical round figure in both suggests one is reporting the other, or both a common source. **Recorded as a probable error with its likely explanation, not asserted either way**; settling it needs an edition, which was not obtained. Carried as a follow-up. *The corpus notes this because "about 635 verses" is exactly the kind of specific-sounding number that propagates unchecked* — the pathology [[katyayana]] and [[astadhyayi]] both documented in this batch.

### The commentaries, and a text that nearly did not survive

**Helārāja** (10th c.) wrote the ***Prakāśa*** on kāṇḍa III, and IEP calls him Bhartṛhari's "first major commentator." Modern editions of kāṇḍa III print the *Prakāśa* alongside Raghunātha Śarmā's *Ambākartrī*.

⚠ **Helārāja is load-bearing for this corpus specifically.** [[avacchedaka]] records **Matilal** citing *Vākyapadīya* III, "Adhikaraṇādhikāra," verses 2–3 **with Helārāja's commentary**, for the pervasive (*abhivyāpaka*) versus partitive (*aikadeśika*) locus distinction — **the grammatical ancestry of Navya-Nyāya's central technical device.** The corpus reaches this text through Helārāja, not through Bhartṛhari alone.

**Bhartṛhari's companion work fared far worse.** His ***Mahābhāṣyadīpikā*** — called ***Tripādī*** by early commentators, "indicating coverage only through the third *pāda*" — survives in **a single manuscript**, and that one "has the commentary **up to Pāṇini 1-1-55**." ⚠ **Kaiyaṭa had access to a fuller version and cites it in his *Pradīpa*.** So the tradition's greatest philosopher-grammarian commented on the [[mahabhashya|Mahābhāṣya]] at length, an 11th-century commentator still read it, and what reaches us is one manuscript covering fifty-five sūtras. **The corpus should hold its picture of Bhartṛhari loosely on this account**: a substantial part of his output is known only by report.

### What is not settled

- **Is the *Vṛtti* on kāṇḍas I–II by Bhartṛhari himself?** ⚠ **The corpus queued this and could not test it.** Neither IEP nor Wikipedia records any dispute — IEP mentions a *Vākyapadīyavṛtti* without comment. Given that a reference work's silence has proved worthless as evidence of agreement **four times in this batch** ([[katyayana]], [[patanjali-grammarian]], [[bhartrhari]], [[sphota]]), **silence is not taken here as settlement.** No claim made; follow-up.
- **The relation between kāṇḍa III and the rest.** *Prakīrṇaka*, "miscellany," is not a promising title for a unified argument, and the section is far longer and more technical than the first two. Whether it is one work with I–II was not investigated.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **IEP, "Bhartrihari"** (fetched): three kāṇḍas; ~635 verses (⚠ under challenge above); Helārāja as first major commentator; the opening verse on Brahman-as-Word; *pratibhā*; the *darśana* claim.
2. **Wikipedia, "Bhartṛhari"** (fetched, independent): the three kāṇḍa **names** — *Brahma-*, *Vākya-*, *Pada-kāṇḍa*; ~635 verses (the same figure, ⚠ hence probably not independent on this point); the *Mahābhāṣyadīpikā*/*Tripādī*.
3. **SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy"** (fetched, independent, scholarly): the doctrinal content — *kalpita*, the partless sentence understood "in a flash," *śabdabrahman* as *pariṇāma*, and that the seeds of *akhaṇḍapada-vāda* are traced to this text.
4. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand**): the kāṇḍa "deals with Sphoṭa, and in a way it contains in a nutshell the philosophy of Sanskrit Grammar"; the Vākyapadīya cited as his authority s.v. *sphoṭa*.
5. **Secondary sources on the text's structure** (wisdomlib essay, fetched; plus a search over published editions): kāṇḍa III as *Padakāṇḍa*/*Prakīrṇaka* in **fourteen samuddeśas** with several named; Helārāja's *Prakāśa*; the *Mahābhāṣyadīpikā*'s single manuscript to A 1.1.55 and Kaiyaṭa's fuller access. ⚠ **The samuddeśa list is partial and from search-summary level.**
6. **Corpus-internal, at first hand**: [[avacchedaka]]'s Matilal citation of VP III with Helārāja — the one place the corpus touches this text through a scholarly monograph rather than a reference work.

**CONTESTED**, and specifically on the text's own extent and composition — the verse count does not fit the described structure, the *Vṛtti*'s authorship is untested, and kāṇḍa III's relation to I–II is open. The **doctrine** is not in dispute across the sources. Confidence **medium**: four independent sources agree on structure and content, but **no verse of the text was read in edition or translation**, and the one specific number available is under active challenge.
