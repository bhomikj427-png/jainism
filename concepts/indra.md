## Gloss / Divergence map

**Indra** (epithets **Śakra** "the mighty one," attested as early as RV 5.34; **Vṛtrahan** "Vṛtra-slayer"; **Maghavan** "the bountiful") is the Vedic storm-and-war god who wields the **vajra** (forged by Tvaṣṭṛ) and slays the serpent **Vṛtra**, releasing the pent cosmic waters (RV 4.18, the fullest telling) — the single most-invoked deity of the Ṛgveda, sole dedicatee of roughly 250 hymns and co-praised in some 50 more, with RV 6.30.4 declaring him superior to every other god. He rules **Svarga** from his capital **Amarāvatī**, with consort **Indrāṇī/Śacī**, a court of **apsarās** (Menakā, Urvaśī, Rambhā, Tilottamā), and the mount [[airavata|Airāvata]].

**This file's central finding is not a single gloss but a documented diachronic reversal**, structurally the same kind of shift already logged for [[matsya|Matsya]] and [[varaha|Varāha]] (Vedic figure reattributed/eclipsed as Vaiṣṇavism rises) — except here the shift is not reattribution of a *role* to another god but an outright **decline in the god's own standing**:

| era/reading | portrait of Indra | source |
|---|---|---|
| **Ṛgvedic sovereign** | supreme among the devas; Vṛtrahan; wields the vajra; heaviest soma-association of any deity | Ṛgveda directly (RV 4.18, 6.30.4) |
| **onset of decline** | "Indra's reputation begins to decline in the Brāhmaṇas, about 900 BCE, where his supremacy is preempted by Prajāpati" | Wendy Doniger, *Encyclopedia of Religion* (1987) |
| **epic/Puranic demotion** | vulnerable, morally flawed, dependent on Śiva/Viṣṇu for restoration of power; his mythological functions migrate to sectarian gods | Doniger, ibid.; corroborated by specific narrative episodes below |

**The demotion narratives**, each independently attested and converging on a coherent "anxious, flawed king" portrait rather than a single borrowed motif:
- **Ahalyā**: Indra seduces sage Gautama's wife by disguise; Gautama curses him — earliest detailed in Rāmāyaṇa Bālakāṇḍa.
- **Govardhana** (Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 10): [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]] redirects Vraja's worship from Indra to Govardhana hill, arguing rain is merely Indra's *duty*, not grounds for worship; Indra, enraged, floods the land with the Samvartaka clouds; Kṛṣṇa shelters everyone by lifting the hill for seven days; Indra submits and apologizes.
- **Bali/Vāmana**: [[bali|Bali]] conquers Indra's three worlds; [[vamana|Vāmana]]'s three strides restore them specifically **to Indra** — already fully narrated in `bali.md`/`vamana.md`, not re-told here.
- **Anxious-monarch throughline**: Indra repeatedly dispatches apsarās to seduce ascetics whose tapas he fears will rival his throne (e.g. Menakā → Viśvāmitra, begetting Śakuntalā) — a recurring insecure-sovereign motif distinct from but thematically continuous with the demotion arc.

Because the Vedic-supremacy facts are independently well-attested while the demotion arc is a separate, later, and equally well-attested layer, this file is marked **`contested`** rather than resolved into a single gloss — the "who is Indra" answer genuinely differs by era, and flattening that into one paragraph would misrepresent both layers.

**Etymology — deliberately left unresolved.** "The etymological roots of Indra are unclear, and it has been a contested topic among scholars since the 19th century" (Wikipedia, citing the scholarly consensus). Competing proposals include *ind-u* ("spirit"), *indha* ("igniter"), a Proto-Indo-European *\*h₂nḗr-* ("manly/hero") derivation, and a possible non-Indo-European BMAC-substrate origin. No consensus exists; asserting one would violate the "etymology is a clue, not a verdict" rule.

**Indo-European dragon-slaying comparison (flagged, not drawn as an edge):** Vṛtra < Proto-Indo-Iranian *wr̥trás* < PIE *wer-* ("to cover/obstruct") is directly cognate with the Avestan **vərəθraγna**, the epithet behind the Zoroastrian god Verethragna — a genuine shared Indo-Iranian inheritance, not a loose analogy, per Calvert Watkins's comparative-philological reconstruction of a "hero-slays-serpent" formulaic root across Indo-European poetics. No dedicated node for Vṛtra, Verethragna, or this comparanda cluster exists yet in this repo; this is recorded here as a candidate for a future `structurally-parallel-to` edge once such a node is written, not asserted as a link now (Watkins consulted only via secondary summaries in this research pass — flagged for primary-source verification before any high-confidence claim is built on it).

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Ṛgveda directly** (RV 4.18, the Vṛtra-slaying; RV 6.30.4, Indra's declared supremacy; RV 5.34, early Śakra usage) — primary-text anchor for the Vedic-sovereign layer.
2. **Wendy Doniger, "Indra," *Encyclopedia of Religion* (1987)**, via Encyclopedia.com — a named academic scholar's direct statement of the Brāhmaṇa-era decline and the epic/Puranic demotion; this is the load-bearing citation for the central divergence, not a popular retelling.
3. **Wikipedia "Ahalya"** and **Rāmāyaṇa Bālakāṇḍa** — independent textual attestation of the adultery/curse episode.
4. **Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 10** (Govardhana episode) — the sourcing for this episode in the research pass leaned on Gaudiya-Vaiṣṇava/ISKCON-affiliated retellings (JKYog, Krishna.com, Hare Krishna Movement), which are doctrinally convergent but **not independent of one another** — flagged honestly rather than presented as triangulated confirmation; the episode's core beats (Kṛṣṇa's argument, the flood, the seven-day shelter, Indra's submission) are stable across them but should be checked against a primary Canto 10 translation before being raised to high confidence.
5. **Wikipedia "Svarga," "Apsara," "Menaka"** — court/cosmological detail, cross-checked across independent encyclopedia entries.
6. **Wikipedia "Indra"** and **"Vritra"** — etymological uncertainty and the Vṛtrahan/Verethragna cognate, used as a hard lexical constraint (signal 3) that stops at "cognate," not "identical."

Verdict: **contested**, confidence **medium** — the Vedic-supremacy layer is itself high-confidence/converged, but the file's honest answer to "who is Indra" spans two eras with materially different portraits, and one sub-episode (Govardhana) rests on weakly-independent sourcing pending a primary-text check.
