## Gloss / Divergence map

**Gauḍapāda** is the earliest Advaitin whose work survives, and **Śaṅkara's *paramaguru*** — grand-preceptor, through Śaṅkara's own teacher Govinda Bhagavatpāda. His one securely-attributed work is the ***Māṇḍūkya-kārikā*** (also *Gauḍapādīya-kārikā*, *Āgama-śāstra*): **215 metered verses in four chapters**, on which both signals agree exactly:

| # | chapter | verses | subject |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Āgama-prakaraṇa* | 29 | traditional doctrine; incorporates the 12-verse Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad |
| 2 | *Vaitathya-prakaraṇa* | 38 | unreality / illusion |
| 3 | *Advaita-prakaraṇa* | 48 | non-duality |
| 4 | *Alātaśānti-prakaraṇa* | 100 | "the peace of the firebrand" |

**Core doctrine — *ajātivāda*, "the doctrine of non-origination."** Nothing is ever born; creation is impossible, since "anything nonexistent in beginning and end is nonexistent in the middle." The phenomenal world is imagined by *māyā*, exactly as dream-objects are; only the unborn, unchanging Ātman-Brahman is real. This negates conventional causality outright, and is the sharpest formulation of non-dualism in the tradition before Śaṅkara softened it into a two-level scheme ([[vivartavada]], [[maya-advaita]]).

**The three states and *turīya*.** Reading the syllable OM phonetically: *a* = waking (*jāgrat*/Viśva), *u* = dream (Taijasa), *m* = deep sleep (*suṣupti*/Prājña), and the unmeasured fourth — ***turīya*** — "nondual, all pervading, unchanging and without sorrow," attributeless transcendental consciousness.

***Asparśa-yoga*** ("contact-less contemplation"): stripping the mind of desire and sensory contact, nullifying the mind's function, so that perceived duality ceases.

### Contested 1 — the dating, spread across three centuries

| claim | basis | source |
|---|---|---|
| **early 6th or 7th c. CE** | back-calculation from Śaṅkara via the Govinda lineage | Wikipedia |
| **c. 500 CE** | Bhāvaviveka, [[santaraksita|Śāntarakṣita]] and [[kamalasila|Kamalaśīla]] **cite** him; Bhāvaviveka is contemporary with Dharmapāla (mid-6th c.) | Wikipedia |
| **not later than 5th c.** | the same Buddhist citations, read as a firmer ceiling | IEP |
| **upper limit 4th c.** | he **cites [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]] and [[asanga|Asaṅga]]** — so he post-dates them | IEP |
| chapters composed **300–600 CE** in stages (ch. 1: 300–400; ch. 3: 400–500; ch. 4: 400–600) | stratigraphy | Nakamura, via Wikipedia |

Note that the two Buddhist arguments run in opposite directions — he is dated by **whom he cites** and by **who cites him** — and the corpus records the resulting spread rather than picking a midpoint. **Potter** further warns that Śaṅkara's "teacher's teacher" should not be read literally: *paramaguru* may mean "highest teacher," not a two-step lineage.

### Contested 2 — is chapter 4 his, and is it Buddhist?

Chapter 4 (*Alātaśānti*) is **the most explicitly Buddhist part of the text** and the focus of the whole controversy. The readings:

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| Vedāntic throughout: an "advaitic interpretation of Vedānta **in the light of** Mādhyamika and Yogācāra doctrines," but not Buddhist | **T. R. V. Murti** | one author, borrowing vocabulary for Vedāntic ends |
| ch. 4 is **a later text by some other author**, composed perhaps for a different audience | **Richard King** | the *Kārikā* is not one work |
| ch. 4 shows Buddhist influence and **may be composite** | **Vidhuśekhara Bhattacārya** | at minimum a distinct compositional layer |
| Gauḍapāda could have written it and still be fundamentally non-Buddhist | **Chandradhar Sarma** | one author; the Buddhism is idiom, not doctrine |
| Buddhist terminology used only because Buddhism was **prevalent**; he remains an Advaitin fundamentally opposed to Buddhist doctrine | **Swami Nikhilananda** | one author; the borrowing is incidental |
| Gauḍapāda (or his predecessors) may be **more "Buddhistically" influenced than Śaṅkara**, whom he credits with "re-vedantinizing" the text | **Sengaku Mayeda** | the tradition moved *away* from Buddhism after him |

**One piece of hard evidence both signals report:** later Vedāntins **quote only the first three chapters** — Murti and King both note that prominent Advaitins omit any reference to chapter 4. Against that, Śaṅkara's commentary on the Chāndogya Upaniṣad is cited as suggesting unified authorship.

**What he demonstrably borrowed:** the Yogācāra doctrine of ***vijñapti-mātra*** ([[vijnaptimatrata]]) and the four-cornered negation ([[catuskoti]]) — adapted, on the majority reading, to Vedāntic rather than Buddhist ends. This is the ancestry of the *pracchanna-bauddha* ("crypto-Buddhist") charge later levelled at [[shankara|Śaṅkara]], and it lands more squarely here than on Śaṅkara.

### Contested 3 — how many Gauḍapādas?

Commentaries on the *Nṛsiṃha-uttara-tāpinī Upaniṣad*, the *Uttaragītā*, tantric works (*Subhagodaya*, *Śrīvidyāratnasūtra*), and — the one that matters for this corpus — a ***bhāṣya* on Īśvarakṛṣṇa's [[samkhya-karika|Sāṃkhyakārikā]]** are all attributed to a "Gauḍapāda." **IEP is explicit that "some scholars maintain that there are multiple Gauḍapāda-s" across different periods, with only the *Māṇḍūkya-kārikā* definitively attributed to Śaṅkara's grand-preceptor.** The corpus therefore does **not** identify the Advaitin with the Sāṃkhya commentator; `samkhya-karika.md` lists a "Gauḍapāda-bhāṣya" among its commentaries, and the non-equivalence edge below exists so the map teaches that this may be a different man.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Gaudapada"** (fetched): the 215-verse/four-chapter structure with per-chapter counts and names; the Govinda/paramaguru lineage and Potter's caution; the Bhāvaviveka/Śāntarakṣita/Kamalaśīla citation argument for ~500 CE; Nakamura's per-chapter stratigraphy; ajātivāda with its "nonexistent in beginning and end" argument; the three states plus turīya; the Buddhist-influence positions of Murti, Mayeda, Nikhilananda, Sarma and King; the *vijñapti-mātra* and catuṣkoṭi borrowings; Comans on Śaṅkara's extensive reliance.
2. **Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Gauḍapāda"** (fetched, independently authored): the **same** four chapters, names and verse counts, independently given; a **different** dating argument (he cites Nāgārjuna and Asaṅga → 4th-c. upper limit; "not later than 5th c."); ajātivāda; *asparśa-yoga*; the OM/three-states/turīya scheme; Bhattacārya vs King on chapter 4, and King's observation that Advaitins omit it; and the explicit **"multiple Gauḍapāda-s"** statement covering the Sāṃkhyakārikā and other attributions.
3. **Search aggregation** (advaita-vedanta.org; wisdomlib; Britannica listing) — independently lists the same further attributions (*Sāṃkhyakārikā-bhāṣya*, *Uttaragītā-bhāṣya*, *Nṛsiṃhottaratāpanī-bhāṣya*, the two Śrīvidyā works) and reports the per-chapter multiple-authorship view, including that some treat the *Alātaśānti* as an independent work "written most probably by a Buddhist."

The two fetched signals are genuinely independent (a general encyclopedia and a peer-reviewed philosophy encyclopedia, differently authored) and **agree exactly on the text's structure, the core doctrines, the paramaguru relation, and the existence of the chapter-4 controversy**. They **disagree on the dating**, and both report the authorship questions as open. Status is therefore **contested** — three separate live disputes (date, chapter-4 authorship, how many Gauḍapādas), each of which *is* the finding — with **converged** content on the *Kārikā*'s structure and doctrine. Confidence **medium**: no verse of the *Kārikā* was fetched verbatim in a critical edition, the dating spans three centuries, and the scholarly positions are known through encyclopedia summary rather than from the monographs themselves.
