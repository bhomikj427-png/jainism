## Gloss / Divergence map

**Indu**, in all probability a Kashmiri, wrote the ***Śaśilekhā*** — a commentary in two versions, one on the *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha* and one on the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*. He completes the Āyurvedic commentator layer this corpus has been building since Batch 43.

He is the most interesting of the three commentators in this batch, for a reason that has nothing to do with medicine: **Indu did not remove the Buddhist material from [[vagbhata|Vāgbhaṭa]]'s text, and [[arunadatta|Aruṇadatta]] did.**

### The finding: a commentary that shows the censorship of another

Meulenbeld: "Indu is one of the few commentators who **accept Vāgbhaṭa's syncretistic attitude and do not cloak the Buddhist elements** in his works." The evidence is specific and checkable:

| passage | Indu | Aruṇadatta and many editors |
|---|---|---|
| *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* Ci. **19.98** | reads ***Jina*** and ***Jinasuta***, glossing Jina as **the Buddha** and Jinasuta as **Avalokiteśa** | the names are **suppressed** and **replaced by Śiva and Śivasuta** |
| **Tārā** | "recognised as a Buddhist deity" | "passed over in silence" |
| A.h. Ci. **1.177**, *daivatapūjā* | names as examples not only Hari and Hara but **Āryāvalokita, Āryatārā, Parṇaśabarī, Aparājitā** | — |
| A.s. U. **1.19** | argues **Ratnaketu** is a Buddhist *dhāraṇī*; also knows the *Māyūrī* | — |

That is not an interpretive disagreement. It is **one commentator preserving a reading and another replacing it with a sectarian substitute**, at a locatable verse, with the editors of the printed tradition following the substitution.

⚠ **Two cautions, and both matter.** First, Meulenbeld reports this as Indu *reading* Jina where others *replace* it; whether the original text read *Jina* is the natural inference but is not independently established here, and the direction could in principle be argued the other way. Second, **Indu's own religion is not settled**: Meulenbeld suggests it "may have been a mixture of Hinduism and Buddhism" — he names **both Manu and the Buddha** as founders of a *hitopadeśaśāstra* — "on the other hand … the *maṅgala*s of his commentaries are of a **Hindu type**." A commentator who preserves Buddhist names is not thereby a Buddhist; he may simply be the one not editing.

This bears directly on `arunadatta.md`, `vagbhata.md` and `ayurveda.md`, and it is the strongest evidence in the corpus that **the transmitted text of a medical classic was religiously shaped by its commentators**.

### ⚠ The Kerala tradition makes two claims about Indu. Meulenbeld rejects both.

**Claim 1 — that Indu was Vāgbhaṭa's direct pupil.** The tradition, "in particular current among the physicians of Kerala," has Vāgbhaṭa travelling to Kerala for the last part of his life accompanied by his favourite pupils **Indu and [[jejjata|Jejjaṭa]]**; a salutatory verse in **Nīlamegha's *Tantrayuktivicāra*** calls them Vāgbhaṭa's disciples; and Indu's own references to Vāgbhaṭa as *ācārya* have been read as confirming it.

Meulenbeld's counter-arguments, in ascending order of force:

1. *Ācārya* proves nothing — "it is common practice among commentators to mention well-known ancient authorities in this way."
2. **Indu cites Jejjaṭa**, "which excludes his being a contemporary" — a fellow-pupil is not a cited authority.
3. **The decisive one, and it comes out of Indu's own preface.** Indu says, in the introduction to *both* versions of the *Śaśilekhā*, that **Vāgbhaṭa's words had become obscured by bad commentaries**, and that this is why he wrote a new one. Meulenbeld: "**At least one generation of commentators must therefore have preceded Indu.**"

**That third argument is the best piece of reasoning in this batch's Āyurveda trio**, and worth stating as a method: *an author's complaint about his predecessors dates him.* You cannot be your master's direct pupil **and** the man cleaning up after a generation of bad commentators on your master. Indu refutes his own discipleship in the act of justifying his book.

**Claim 2 — that Indu knew Kerala practice.** N. S. Mooss, who edited and translated the 119 appended *paribhāṣā* verses, held that Indu "possessed a thorough knowledge of traditional practices prevalent in Kerala." Meulenbeld: "This view need not be endorsed, as Indu merely refers to practices adopted by **some physicians, without specifying a particular region**."

⚠ Both claims would place Indu in Kerala; Meulenbeld places him in Kashmir. **A regional tradition claiming a figure for its own lineage is a recognisable evidence-type, and this corpus should treat it as one** — compare `nathamuni.md`'s recovery-legend and `shrikantha.md`.

### Divergence — the date

| reading | who holds it | on what |
|---|---|---|
| **before the latter half of the 12th century** | **Meulenbeld** | *terminus post quem*: he quotes Jejjaṭa (7th–8th c.) and the *Dhanvantarīyanighaṇṭu* (A.D. 1000–1100). *terminus ante quem*: Niścala's *Ratnaprabhā* cites an *Indumatī* mostly traceable in the *Śaśilekhā* on the *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha* |
| **13th century** | **P. V. Sharma** | that Indu quotes the *Medinīkośa* (which Sharma dates to the 12th c.); that a quotation by Hemādri indicates Indu is **posterior to Aruṇadatta**; and that Maheśvara's *Viśvaprakāśa* (early 12th c.) influenced him |

⚠ **P. V. Sharma's second argument, if right, reverses the relation drawn above** — it would make Indu the *later* man, restoring Buddhist names to a text Aruṇadatta had already Śaivised, rather than preserving what Aruṇadatta removed. The suppression finding survives either way; **its direction does not.** Neither scholar is followed here.

⚠ **And a text-critical problem that undercuts both.** Meulenbeld: the available editions, "in particular those of the *Śaśilekhā* on the *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha*, present a large number of passages between brackets, which probably indicate **interpolations**" — and "other parts, **not** placed between brackets by the editors, should **also** be regarded as not belonging to the original text, since they derive from works by authors who are definitely posterior to Indu." **The printed Śaśilekhā is not a reliable witness to Indu**, which means every dating argument built on what it quotes is built on sand. Meulenbeld's own remedy is the right one: study the quotations *from* Indu in later commentaries.

### A second Indu

An **Indu who composed a *nighaṇṭu*** is known from quotations in Kṣīrasvāmin, Mallinātha, Bhānuji Dīkṣita, Nārāyaṇa Vidyāvinoda and others. **N. N. Das Gupta** suggested the lexicographer and the commentator are one man. Meulenbeld's evidence against: the verses cited from the *Indunighaṇṭu* **do not agree with** the quotations in Indu's commentaries, nor form part of the *Dhanvantarīya-* or *Aṣṭāṅganighaṇṭu*. Since the earliest quotations from the *Indunighaṇṭu* are in Kṣīrasvāmin's *Amarakośodghāṭana* (first half of the 12th c.), the *nighaṇṭu* "cannot be earlier than the second half of the eleventh century."

A further Das Gupta hypothesis — that Indu is **Indukara, father of the Mādhava of the *Mādhavanidāna*** — "is not based on any evidence, and was rightly rejected by D. Ch. Bhattacharyya, because Indukara and Mādhava were residents of **Bengal**, whereas Indu was a native of **Kashmir**."

**That is the fourth namesake-collapse this corpus has recorded** — after `arunadatta.md`'s three men, `vacaspati-mishra.md`'s two, and `haricandra.md`'s six.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — G. Jan Meulenbeld, *A History of Indian Medical Literature*, vol. IA, "Indu," read directly.** The standard critical reference and the sole substantive source: the two *Śaśilekhā*s, the Kashmiri origin, the Jina/Jinasuta and Tārā suppressions with verse references, the Buddhist deity lists, the Manu-and-Buddha remark and the Hindu *maṅgala*s, the three counter-arguments to the discipleship tradition, the two-sided dating with P. V. Sharma's dissent, the interpolation problem, the *Indunighaṇṭu* identity question, and the Indukara rejection. **Signal 2 — the scholars and witnesses Meulenbeld reports and weighs**, who are the evidence and are not him: N. N. Das Gupta, D. Ch. Bhattacharyya, P. V. Sharma, N. S. Mooss, Nīlamegha's *Tantrayuktivicāra*, Niścala's *Ratnaprabhā*, Kṣīrasvāmin. **Signal 3 — Indu himself**, at one remove: the preface to both *Śaśilekhā*s complaining of bad commentaries, which is the load-bearing item and is an author's statement about his own book. **Signal 4 — corpus-internal**: `arunadatta.md`, `vagbhata.md`, `jejjata.md`, `hemadri.md` (Batch 44) and `haricandra.md`, `candrata.md` (this batch).

⚠ **Single-scholar dependence, as with `candrata.md` and `haricandra.md`.** One author, one work, for the third node running. The mitigation is the same and is no stronger: Meulenbeld attributes each claim to a named witness or scholar, and where scholars disagree he prints both. **No claim here has been confirmed against a second modern scholar**, and the most consequential one — the direction of the Jina/Śiva substitution — depends on a dating Meulenbeld and P. V. Sharma dispute.

**Why contested.** The date splits by roughly a century between two named scholars; the discipleship is a live tradition Meulenbeld argues against rather than a settled error; the lexicographer's identity is unresolved; and the printed text is judged unreliable. **Why medium, not high.** Neither *Śaśilekhā* was read; the Jina/Jinasuta verse was not checked in any edition of the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*; P. V. Sharma is known only through Meulenbeld's report of him; and the suppression finding's **direction** is left open.
