## Gloss / Divergence map

Anattā (Pali) / anātman (Sanskrit) is the Buddhist teaching of **non-self** — the denial that there is a permanent, unchanging self or soul underlying personal experience. It is one of the three marks of existence (*ti-lakkhaṇa*) alongside impermanence (*anicca*) and suffering (*dukkha*).

**The Anattalakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59)** — "The Not-Self Characteristic Discourse" — is traditionally the second discourse the Buddha delivered (to the first five disciples, at Isipatana/Benares). **The mūla is now triangulated in two genuinely independent translations, both fetched directly (2026-06-10):**

- **Thanissaro Bhikkhu** (Access to Insight): "Form, monks, is not self. If form were the self, this form would not lend itself to dis-ease." Questioning sequence: "Is form constant or inconstant?" → "Inconstant" → "stressful" → not fit to regard as "This is mine. This is my self. This is what I am." Uses hyphenated "not-self" throughout.
- **Ñāṇamoli Thera** (Access to Insight): "Were form self, then this form would not lead to affliction, and one could have it of form: 'Let my form be thus, let my form be not thus.'" Formula: "This is not mine, this is not I, this is not myself." Avoids "soul."

The two translators agree on the argument structure: each of the five khandhas is (a) impermanent, (b) suffering, and (c) not subject to the command "let it be thus" — therefore none is fit to be identified as "self," and there is nothing outside the khandhas to be self either.

**What anattā denies:** a permanent, unchanging, autonomous substance that underlies experience, persists through change, and constitutes personal identity. This targets both the Brahmanical *ātman* (eternal unchanging soul = Brahman) and any equivalent claim.

**What anattā does NOT straightforwardly deny:** that there is a conventional, functional sense of person-hood for practical/ethical purposes (a monk still observes vows; a person can be held responsible; rebirth occurs). The tension between conventional and ultimate levels is a major source of Buddhist philosophical development.

**Lexicon note (2026-06-10):** Buddhadatta's *Concise Pali-English Dictionary* gives anattā a dual classification — adjective "soul-less" and noun "non-ego." The grammar itself underdetermines the interpretive choice below: the adjectival use ("X is not-self," a characteristic of phenomena) fits the methodological reading; the nominal use ("the absence of self," a fact about reality) fits the ontological reading. Etymology is a clue, not a verdict.

## Contested readings of anattā

| reading | who holds it | what it commits to |
|---|---|---|
| **Ontological no-self**: no self exists at any level | Theravāda Abhidharma (most explicit); the five khandhas and their dharmas are the only real things | eliminativism about personal identity; difficult to explain rebirth without a transmigrating subject |
| **Methodological not-self**: the teaching is a *practice strategy* — identify nothing as self; not a metaphysical thesis | **Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "No-self or Not-self?" (fetched directly 2026-06-10):** "the anatta teaching is not a doctrine of no-self, but a not-self strategy for shedding suffering by letting go of its cause"; on the Buddha's silence (the Vacchagotta exchange): "to hold either that there is a self or that there is no self is to fall into extreme forms of wrong view that make the path of Buddhist practice impossible." Bhikkhu Bodhi also distinguishes "no-self" vs "not-self" (attribution via aggregators) | Avoids the rebirth problem; but may seem to dodge the ontological question; makes SN 22.59 a pedagogical instrument rather than a metaphysical argument |
| **Conventional self only**: a self exists conventionally but not ultimately | Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna, ~2nd c. CE) — applies śūnyatā; the self is empty of inherent existence but not simply nothing | Consistent with both rebirth and anattā; requires the ultimate/conventional two-truths framework |

This is a live debate among both scholars and practitioners. The reading shapes how one understands Buddhist ethics, rebirth, and nirvāṇa. Note that SN 22.59 itself — in both fetched translations — only ever predicates "not self" *of the khandhas*; the move from "none of the khandhas is self" to "there is no self" is an inference the ontological reading makes and the methodological reading refuses. That textual gap is precisely where the dispute lives.

## Anattā vs. the Jain jīva — the sharpest contrast in the corpus

| dimension | Buddhist anattā | Jain jīva |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a permanent individual soul? | no — anattā denies it (on the ontological reading; the methodological reading declines the question) | yes — jīva is an eternal individual substance |
| Is liberation of a "self"? | liberation = cessation of the arising of khandhas; no individual persists | liberation = the individual jīva at lokāgra, omniscient, eternally distinct |
| Is change compatible with identity? | impermanence is part of what makes "self" untenable | yes — jīva changes via paryāya (modes) while its dravya (substance) persists |
| What rebirth transmigrates? | contested (see above); conventionally there is continuity; ultimately no substance transmigrates | the jīva itself transmigrates from body to body |

A precise formulation from search-result scholarship: "Jainism incorporates an eternal but *changing* jīva; Buddhism teaches anattā (no eternal self)." — the emphasis on *changing* is key: Jain jīva is not the Brahmanical unchanging ātman; it does change; but it IS a persisting individual substance. Buddhism denies even that (or, methodologically, refuses to affirm it).
