## Gloss / Divergence map

Anattā (Pali) / anātman (Sanskrit) is the Buddhist teaching of **non-self** — the denial that there is a permanent, unchanging self or soul underlying personal experience. It is one of the three marks of existence (*ti-lakkhaṇa*) alongside impermanence (*anicca*) and suffering (*dukkha*).

**The Anattalakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59)** — "The Not-Self Characteristic Discourse" — is traditionally the second discourse the Buddha delivered (to the first five disciples, at Isipatana/Benares). The argument:

*"Form is not self. If form were self, form would not lead to affliction... Feeling is not self... Perception is not self... Mental formations are not self... Consciousness is not self."*

Each of the five khandhas is shown to be (a) impermanent, (b) subject to suffering, and (c) incapable of responding to the command "let this be thus" — therefore none is fit to be identified as "self." There is nothing outside the five khandhas to be self either. Therefore: no self.

**What anattā denies:** a permanent, unchanging, autonomous substance that underlies experience, persists through change, and constitutes personal identity. This targets both the Brahmanical *ātman* (eternal unchanging soul = Brahman) and any equivalent claim.

**What anattā does NOT straightforwardly deny:** that there is a conventional, functional sense of person-hood for practical/ethical purposes (a monk still observes vows; a person can still be held responsible; rebirth occurs). The tension between the conventional and ultimate levels is a major source of Buddhist philosophical development.

## Contested readings of anattā

| reading | who holds it | what it commits to |
|---|---|---|
| **Ontological no-self**: no self exists at any level | Theravāda Abhidharma (most explicit); the five khandhas and their dharmas are the only real things | eliminativism about personal identity; difficult to explain rebirth without a transmigrating subject |
| **Methodological not-self**: the teaching is a *practice* — identify nothing as self, not a metaphysical thesis | Some interpreters (Bhikkhu Bodhi distinguishes "no-self" vs "not-self"); fits the Buddha's silence on metaphysical questions | Avoids the rebirth problem; but may seem to dodge the ontological question |
| **Conventional self only**: a self exists conventionally but not ultimately | Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna, ~2nd c. CE) — applies śūnyatā; the self is empty of inherent existence but not simply nothing | Consistent with both rebirth and anattā; requires the ultimate/conventional two-truths framework |

This is a live scholarly debate. The reading shapes how one understands Buddhist ethics, rebirth, and nirvāṇa.

## Anattā vs. the Jain jīva — the sharpest contrast in the corpus

| dimension | Buddhist anattā | Jain jīva |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a permanent individual soul? | no — anattā denies it | yes — jīva is an eternal individual substance |
| Is liberation of a "self"? | liberation = cessation of the arising of khandhas; no individual persists | liberation = the individual jīva at lokāgra, omniscient, eternally distinct |
| Is change compatible with identity? | impermanence is part of what makes "self" untenable | yes — jīva changes via paryāya (modes) while its dravya (substance) persists |
| What rebirth transmigrates? | contested (see above); conventionally there is continuity; ultimately no substance transmigrates | the jīva itself transmigrates from body to body |

A precise formulation from search-result scholarship: "Jainism incorporates an eternal but *changing* jīva; Buddhism teaches anattā (no eternal self)." — the emphasis on *changing* is key: Jain jīva is not the Brahmanical unchanging ātman; it does change; but it IS a persisting individual substance. Buddhism denies even that.
