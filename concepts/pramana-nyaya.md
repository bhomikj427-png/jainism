## Gloss

The Nyāya school (founded on the *Nyāyasūtra* attributed to Gautama/Akṣapāda, ~200 CE) admits exactly four valid sources of knowledge (*pramāṇa*): **pratyakṣa** (perception), **anumāna** (inference), **upamāna** (comparison/analogy), and **śabda** (verbal testimony). Each is a distinct pathway to reliable cognition; Nyāya treats them as exhaustive for the purposes of philosophical inquiry. This four-pramāṇa scheme is the defining epistemological claim of the school and shapes its logical, physical, and theological positions.

## The four pramāṇas

| Pramāṇa | Sanskrit | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perception** | *pratyakṣa* | Direct sensory contact between organ and object; non-verbal, non-deviating | Seeing smoke on the hill |
| **Inference** | *anumāna* | Cognition following from invariable concomitance (*vyāpti*) | Inferring fire from smoke (5-member syllogism) |
| **Comparison** | *upamāna* | Knowing an unfamiliar object by its similarity to a known one | Learning "a wild cow resembles a domestic cow" |
| **Testimony** | *śabda* | Reliable assertion by a qualified, trustworthy speaker | Scripture; direct witness |

**The five-member Nyāya syllogism (*pañcāvayava*):** *pratijñā* (thesis), *hetu* (reason/sign), *udāharaṇa* (example), *upanaya* (application), *nigamana* (conclusion). Requires *vyāpti* — a proven invariable connection between sign and target.

## Contrast with Jain pramāṇa theory

| Feature | Nyāya (4 pramāṇas) | Jain (2 pramāṇas) |
|---|---|---|
| Number | 4 | 2 |
| Perception (*pratyakṣa*) | Sensory only | Sensory + super-sensory (kevala-jñāna = direct omniscience) |
| Inference | Separate pramāṇa | Subsumed under *parokṣa* (indirect knowledge) |
| Testimony | Separate pramāṇa | Subsumed under *parokṣa* |
| Analogy | Separate pramāṇa | Not recognised as separate |
| Self (*ātman/jīva*) | Permanent; defended by inference | Permanent jīva; defended ontologically |
| God | Accepted (inferred from cosmic order) | Rejected (no creator/sustainer) |

The most consequential divergence: Jain perception (*pratyakṣa*) includes kevala-jñāna (omniscience) — a form of direct knowledge far beyond the senses, which Nyāya's sensory-contact definition cannot accommodate.

## Nyāya and the Buddhist challenge

Dignāga (~5th c. CE) argued perception should be purely non-conceptual (no words, no inference), and rejected both upamāna and śabda as independent pramāṇas. The Nyāya-Buddhist debate over the number and nature of pramāṇas is one of the longest-running disputes in classical Indian philosophy. Nyāya defends conceptual perception and the permanent ātman; Buddhism rejects both. Jain philosophers also contested Nyāya's argument for God while sharing its realism about substances.
