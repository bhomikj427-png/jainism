## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Pramāṇavārttika** ("Commentary on Valid Cognition") of **Dharmakīrti** (c. 600–660 CE, of Nālandā) is "Buddhism's most influential epistemological text" — the magnum opus of the thinker who stands as the greatest Buddhist epistemologist after Dignāga. It is framed as a **commentary on Dignāga's [[pramana-samuccaya]]**, but vastly elaborates and defends it. It is the primary-text anchor behind [[hetu-vidya]] (the Buddhist-logic tradition), [[apoha]], and the commentator [[dharmottara]]. The work runs to ~2000 verse-stanzas in **four chapters**: *pramāṇasiddhi* (the establishment of valid cognition — and of the Buddha as an authority), *pratyakṣa* (perception), *svārthānumāna* (inference-for-oneself, with Dharmakīrti's own auto-commentary), and *parārthānumāna* (inference-for-others).

**The two pramāṇas, causally re-grounded.** Like Dignāga, Dharmakīrti admits only **perception and inference** ("based on but not reducible to perception"). His decisive addition is the criterion of the real: **arthakriyā** — *causal efficacy*. "Whatever has causal powers really exists"; only the momentary **svalakṣaṇa** (unique particular) has arthakriyā and is therefore real, while universals are "convenient fictions." From this he argues **momentariness (kṣaṇikatva)**: things must be momentary because "nothing causes new effects while itself remaining the same."

**Inference grounded in real relations.** The valid reason satisfies the **trairūpya** — the reason is a property of the subject (*pakṣadharmatva*), pervades the similar cases (*anvaya-vyāpti*), and is absent from the dissimilar (*vyatireka-vyāpti*). Crucially, Dharmakīrti grounds the pervasion (vyāpti) in **real relations — causation or essential identity** — not in mere repeated co-observation, answering the Cārvāka challenge that induction can never secure a universal. He likewise **re-grounds Dignāga's apoha in causality**: "blue things cause the judgment 'this is blue'" because blue particulars, not red, initiate the relevant perceptual-conceptual chain.

**Two metaphysical registers.** Dharmakīrti argues on a sliding scale: most arguments accept external objects (**Sautrāntika** realism), but by the perception chapter he holds "everything cognizable is internal" (**Yogācāra** idealism) — a shift that leaves the epistemology, nominalism, and causal theory "largely unaffected." His apoha was also weaponised against Brahmanical universals (*jāti*, the word that also names caste). Later commentators include **Dharmottara**, Devendrabuddhi, and Prajñākaragupta; in Tibet the text became central via Sakya Paṇḍita and Tsongkhapa.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1** (Wikipedia, "Pramāṇavārttika"): Dharmakīrti (6th–7th c.); the work as Buddhism's most influential epistemological text and a commentary on Dignāga's Pramāṇasamuccaya; ~2000 verses in four chapters (named); two pramāṇas ("perception and inference, based on but not reducible to perception"); apoha; centrality in the Tibetan scholastic tradition (Sakya Paṇḍita, Tsongkhapa). **Signal 2** (SEP, "Dharmakīrti"): dates c. 600–660 CE; Nālandā; most influential epistemologist after Dignāga; the Pramāṇavārttika as his (unfinished) magnum opus commenting on the Pramāṇasamuccaya; arthakriyā/causal efficacy as the criterion of the real ("whatever has causal powers... really exists"); svalakṣaṇa as the causally efficient particular; momentariness via the sattvānumāna; the trairūpya (pakṣadharmatva + anvaya + vyatireka); apoha grounded in causality; the Sautrāntika→Yogācāra two-level structure; the anti-jāti polemic.

The two are independent (an encyclopedia and a peer-reviewed philosophy encyclopedia) and agree on author, dating, the commentary-relation to Dignāga, structure, the two pramāṇas, apoha, and the metaphysical levels. Medium, not high: the Pramāṇavārttika verses were **not fetched verbatim**; the four-chapter *ordering* differs across manuscripts/traditions (the Wikipedia list and SEP's discussion present them in different sequences — the *content* is stable, the canonical chapter order is itself a scholarly question), and that variance is left unresolved here.
