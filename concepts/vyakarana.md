## Gloss / Divergence map

***Vyākaraṇa*** is the **Sanskrit grammatical tradition** — the discipline that analyses the language into its elements and states the rules by which they combine. Etymologically it is not "grammar" but **"separation, distinction, discrimination, analysis, explanation"** (*vi-ā-√kṛ*, "to take apart"): the name states the method, not the subject.

Its **institutional** position is not in dispute: it is one of the **six vedāṅgas**, the ancillary sciences of the Veda, alongside **śikṣā** (phonetics), **chandas** (metre), **nirukta** (etymology), **kalpa** (ritual procedure) and **jyotiṣa** (astronomy/calendar). Its stated motive is conservative rather than descriptive — to "prevent sloppy usage and transmission of the Vedic knowledge." **Grammar exists here because a text had to be recited correctly**, and only afterwards became a theory of meaning.

### The divergence: is vyākaraṇa a *darśana*?

This is the node's live question, and the corpus must not settle it by default. Vyākaraṇa is *never* one of the six āstika **darśanas** ([[nyaya-sutra|Nyāya]], Vaiśeṣika, [[samkhya-karika|Sāṃkhya]], [[yoga-darshana|Yoga]], [[mimamsa-sutra|Mīmāṃsā]], [[brahma-sutra|Vedānta]]) — yet it produced a metaphysics ([[sabdabrahman]]), a semantics ([[sphota]]), and a body of philosophical polemic that the darśanas answered at length.

That it had to be argued is itself evidence it was not standardly granted |
| Neither — the grammarians simply were not doing ontology | SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy": grammarians were "more interested in language and communication than in ontology," and themselves "do not develop a pramāṇa theory" | The philosophical reading is supplied by *readers* of the grammarians (Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya, Vedānta), not asserted by them |

⚠ **The honest statement is the third plus the second: vyākaraṇa is a vedāṅga that argued its way toward darśana status, and the argument was made by [[bhartrhari|Bhartṛhari]], not by [[panini|Pāṇini]].** The corpus therefore files it as a **śāstra**, not as a seventh darśana — and records that the promotion was claimed rather than granted.

### The *munitraya*, and an inverted hierarchy of authority

The tradition's own canon is the ***munitraya***, the "triad of sages" — verified at first hand in Abhyankar's *Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*:

> "the famous three ancient grammarians Pāṇini (the author of the Sūtras), Kātyāyana (the author of the Vārttikas), and Patañjali (the author of the Mahābhāṣya)"

Abhyankar's **second** sense is the more interesting one: *munitraya* also names **Pāṇini's grammar itself**, "being the contribution of the reputed triad of Grammarians." **The text is identified with its commentators.** The [[astadhyayi|Aṣṭādhyāyī]] as actually studied is not Pāṇini's book but the three-author composite.

⚠ **And the tradition drew the consequence explicitly.** A maxim attributed to **Kaiyaṭa** (c. 11th c.), *yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam*, holds that **among the three sages the *later* has the greater authority** — so [[patanjali-grammarian|Patañjali]] outranks [[katyayana|Kātyāyana]], who outranks Pāṇini. This inverts the usual assumption that a commentary is subordinate to its text. **It is the same movement the corpus recorded independently in [[tattvacintamani]]** — where by the 17th century the live object of study is a commentary on a commentary — but there it was a drift, and here it is a **stated rule**.

✅ **Provenance caveat DISCHARGED, Batch 48.** Batch 47 flagged this maxim as "secondary report only, unverified at first hand" after the primary scholarship returned HTTP 403 and the formula proved absent from Abhyankar under *yathottara*. **It is now verified**: Deshpande's 26th J. Gonda Lecture (2018), read at first hand, gives the wording exactly as recorded above and locates it — ***Pradīpa* on *Mahābhāṣya* on P. 1.1.29; Vol. I: 217**. The attribution to Kaiyaṭa is correct. See [[kaiyata]], which also holds the two things that remain contested (his date, and *why* he ranked the sages at all).

⚠ **But the scope was wrong, and the correction matters here too.** Deshpande's next clause bounds the rule: authority "increases successively, and **it effectively peaks in Patañjali**." So the maxim ranks **the three sages and stops** — it does **not** generalise into "a later commentator outranks an earlier one." The contrast with [[tattvacintamani]] drawn just above therefore needs care: the *munitraya* ranking is a stated rule **covering three men**, while the climb to tiers 4 and 5 ([[kaiyata]]'s *Pradīpa*, Nāgeśa's *Uddyota*) is as unlicensed as the Navya-Nyāya drift. Corrected in [[mahabhashya]] this batch, which had leaned on the wider reading.

### What preceded Pāṇini, and how little of it survives

Pāṇini did not begin the discipline. Abhyankar, reading Pāṇini's own text, counts **ten** earlier grammarians whom Pāṇini names: **Āpiśali, Śākaṭāyana, Gārgya, Śākalya, Kāśyapa, Bharadvāja, Gālava, Cākravarmaṇa, Senaka and [[sphota|Sphoṭāyana]]**. "The works of most these authors are lost." ⚠ Wikipedia gives **the same ten names** — which is corroboration of the *reading* but **not an independent witness**: both are reporting one list that Pāṇini's own text supplies, so agreement here is expected and carries no extra weight (§4 signal 1). **The tradition's founder is the earliest grammarian whose book survives, not the earliest grammarian** — a distinction this corpus has had to draw before ([[kapila]], [[kanada]]).

⚠ And the blank left by that loss was filled the usual way: tradition names **Indra** "the first grammarian of the Sanskrit language," while Abhyankar judges "it is very likely that Pāṇini had no grammar work of Indra before him." A founder supplied where the record has none.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Vyākaraṇa"** (fetched): vedāṅga classification and the other five; etymology; the Vedic-preservation motive; the pre-Pāṇinian list (independently naming **Sphoṭāyana**); the post-Pāṇinian line down to Nāgeśa. Does **not** call it a darśana.
2. **IEP, "Bhartrihari"** (fetched, independent): the darśana claim — and, decisively, that it is a claim Bhartṛhari **argues for**.
3. **SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy"** (fetched, independent, scholarly): the grammarians as non-ontologists who develop no *pramāṇa* theory; Pāṇini c. 400 BCE, Kātyāyana and Patañjali 200–100 BCE.
4. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand** from a full scan): *munitraya* in both senses; the ten pre-Pāṇinian names; the Indra judgement. This is the batch's **lexical hard constraint** (§4 signal 3), and it is the grammarians' own reference work rather than a general encyclopedia.
5. **Internal consistency**: 20 files in `concepts/` invoked "grammar / Pāṇini / vyākaraṇa" before this node existed; [[avacchedaka]] recorded the absence as a gap rather than papering it over with a loose edge.

Signals 1–3 **split on the darśana question**, and that split is the finding — hence **contested**. They *agree* on the vedāṅga classification, the motive, and the three-author canon. Confidence **medium**: three of the four signals are encyclopedia-level, and the authority-maxim is single-sourced and second-hand (flagged above).
