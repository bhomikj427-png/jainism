## Gloss

**Fallacious reason** — something that *appears* to be a valid logical reason (*hetu*) but is defective and prevents a valid inference (*anumāna*) from being drawn. The Nyāyasūtra defines five types at NS I.2.4: *savyabhicāra-viruddha-prakaraṇasama-sādhyasama-kālātītā hetvābhāsaḥ*.

## The five Nyāya hetvābhāsas (NS I.2.4)

| Sanskrit | name | defect | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| savyabhicāra | inconclusive / erratic | the middle term is present in both positive and negative cases of the major term — vyāpti fails to hold universally | "Sound is permanent because it is imperceptible" — imperceptibility applies to permanent AND impermanent things |
| viruddha | contradictory | the reason actually proves the OPPOSITE of the conclusion | "Sound is permanent because it is produced" — productedness proves *im*permanence |
| prakaraṇasama | counterbalanced | an equally strong reason can be adduced for the opposite conclusion, leaving the question open | used when two sides each have a hetu of comparable force |
| sādhyasama | unproven / as-yet-undetermined | the reason itself requires the same proof as the conclusion | "Sound is eternal because it has qualities, and having qualities is eternal" — the premise needs the same proof |
| kālātīta / bādhita | contradicted | the reason is overridden by another reliable source of knowledge | inferred conclusion directly contradicted by perception or scripture |

**Why hetvābhāsa is important**: it defines the boundary of valid *anumāna*. Each fallacy type maps onto a failure of vyāpti: savyabhicāra breaks universal co-presence; viruddha reverses the concomitance direction; etc.

**Buddhist parallel**: Dignāga's trairūpya (three-condition) schema for a valid hetu implies three corresponding fallacies — *asiddha* (the reason is not established in the subject), *viruddha* (the reason contradicts), *anaikāntika* (the reason is not universally co-present with the conclusion). These map approximately onto Nyāya's five with different grouping.
