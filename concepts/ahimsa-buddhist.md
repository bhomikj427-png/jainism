## Gloss

Buddhist *ahiṃsā* (Pāli: *avihiṃsā*) is the non-harming principle grounded in the first of the five precepts (*pañcasīla*): *pāṇātipātā veramaṇī* — abstaining from taking life. It is not an absolute ontological rule as in Jainism but an *intention*-based ethical norm: harm caused without any harmful intention (e.g., unknowing treading on an insect) does not bind the karma of the actor. The positive expression of Buddhist non-harm is the *brahma-vihāra* (divine abiding) framework — especially *mettā* (loving-kindness) and *karuṇā* (compassion) — which is the psychologically generative ground from which non-harm naturally follows.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Dhammapada 10.129 (Arihanta Institute article, citing Pāli canon):** "All tremble at violence; all fear death. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should neither slay nor cause to slay." This is the canonical textual anchor for Buddhist ahiṃsā: the basis is empathic identification, not an ontological claim about the harm done to a jīva.

**Signal 2 — Wikipedia "Karuṇā" and "Ahimsa" (citing Pāli sources):** Confirms the four brahma-vihāras (mettā, karuṇā, muditā, upekkhā) are the standard positive formulation of Buddhist ethics; Theravāda texts identify these as divine abodes for both monastics and householders.

**Signal 3 — Arihanta Institute comparative article:** Independently establishes: Jain ahiṃsā = absolute, covering unintentional harm, indirect harm, and approved harm (kṛta/kārita/anumata); Buddhist ahiṃsā = intentional, relational, motivation-based. These are structurally different despite the shared name.

## Buddhist vs. Jain ahiṃsā: structural comparison

| Feature | Buddhist ahiṃsā | Jain ahiṃsā |
|---|---|---|
| **Grounding** | Intention / mental state | Ontological (every being has a jīva; harm = karma-matter) |
| **Scope** | Sentient beings (conscious experience) | All life-forms with jīva, including 1-sensed beings |
| **Unintentional harm** | Does not bind karma if no harmful intent | Does bind karma (even unintentional) — hence extreme care |
| **Meat-eating** | Allowed for monastics if three-pure (not seen/heard/suspected killed for them) | Prohibited; Jain monks also avoid root vegetables |
| **Positive formulation** | Brahma-vihāras: mettā + karuṇā | Compassion implicit; focus on structural non-harm observances |
| **Mahāyāna extension** | Bodhisattva compassion may override precepts in extreme cases | No override — observances are absolute |
| **Karma mechanism** | Mental formations (cetanā); not physical particles | Fine physical matter accreting to jīva |

## The four brahma-vihāras

The positive basis for Buddhist non-harm:
1. **Mettā** (loving-kindness) — goodwill universally extended; "true friendliness" to all beings
2. **Karuṇā** (compassion) — empathy with the suffering of others; desire to alleviate it
3. **Muditā** (sympathetic joy) — rejoicing in others' well-being
4. **Upekkhā** (equanimity) — non-reactive balance; not pulling or pushing

These are the brahma-vihāras ("divine abidings") recommended for both monastics and householders in the Pāli canon. Karuṇā and mettā together constitute the positive Buddhist ethic of non-harm.

## The intentionality gap

The critical philosophical difference: Buddhism locates karma in *cetanā* (intention/volition); Jainism locates karma in the result regardless of intention. This means:
- A Theravāda monk who unknowingly steps on an insect accrues no karma
- A Jain ascetic who unknowingly steps on an insect accrues karma — hence the practice of sweeping the path

This is a genuine ontological disagreement, not merely a difference of emphasis. Both traditions developed extreme ascetic practices, but the Jain ontological commitment to jīva-protection explains the greater rigour of Jain observances.
