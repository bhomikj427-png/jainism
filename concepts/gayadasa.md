## Gloss / Divergence map

**Gayadāsa** (**Gayadāsācārya**), **11th century**, is the author of the ***Nyāyacandrikā Pañjikā*** on the *Suśrutasaṃhitā* — the middle layer of that text's three-storey commentarial tradition, standing between [[jejjata|Jejjaṭa]]'s lost earliest commentary and [[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]]'s complete *Nibandhasaṅgraha* a century later.

He is, in this corpus, the clearest case of a **partially surviving** text, and the arithmetic of that survival is worth setting out:

| what | condition |
|---|---|
| the *Nyāyacandrikā* on the **Nidānasthāna** | extant — "known in **one manuscript**," and edited |
| the *Nyāyacandrikā* on **everything else** | **lost as a text** |
| but: "numerous quotations from Gayadāsa's work are found throughout **all sections** of the *Suśrutasaṃhitā*, especially in Ḍalhaṇa's commentary" | **proving that it covered the whole of that text** |

So the commentary is known to have been complete **because a later commentator quoted it everywhere**, while what physically survives of it is one section in one manuscript. The evidence for its extent and the evidence for its text come from entirely different places.

That is the [[jejjata]] pattern one notch better off, and together the two make the point sharply: **[[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]]'s *Nibandhasaṅgraha* is not merely a commentary on Suśruta — it is the archive of Suśruta's commentators.** [[dalhana]] recorded as an open hole that "no source consulted names *which* earlier commentators the *Nibandhasaṅgraha* preserves." With this node and [[jejjata]], two are named, and the *Nibandhasaṅgraha*'s compilatory habit stops looking like a lack of originality and starts looking like the reason a century of Suśruta scholarship is recoverable at all.

### The printed evidence

The relationship is visible in the standard edition's **title**, which names both commentaries and marks exactly how much of the second there is:

> *The Suśrutasaṃhitā of Suśruta with the Nibandhasaṅgrahā commentary of Śrī Dalhaṇāchārya **and the Nyāyacandrikā Pañjikā of Śrī Gayadāschārya on Nidānasthāna***

— ed. Vaidya Jādavji Trikamji Āchārya and Nārāyaṇ Rām Āchārya, Nirṇayasāgara Press, Bombay, **1938**. The qualifier "*on Nidānasthāna*" is doing bibliographic work: it is the edition telling you, on its own title page, that the second commentary is a fragment.

Aufrecht's *Catalogus Catalogorum* adds a separate line of evidence for his standing: "**a medical writer. Quoted by Bhāvamiśra, by Vaidyavācaspati.**" So Gayadāsa was still being cited in the 16th century, by the author of the *Bhāvaprakāśa* — his reception outlasted his text.

### What is not established here

- **The date rests on one report.** "Gayadāsa's *Nyāyacandrikā* is from the 11th century AD, while Ḍalhaṇa's *Nibandhasaṅgraha* is from the 12th" comes through search-level summary of the Āyurvedic literature, not from a source read directly. It is consistent with everything else here — Ḍalhaṇa quotes him, so he precedes Ḍalhaṇa, whose own bracket [[hemadri|this batch loosened]] — but it is **not independently confirmed**.
- **His region, teacher and dates within the century are unknown** to every source consulted.
- **G. J. Meulenbeld's *A History of Indian Medical Literature*** (Groningen Oriental Studies 15; Egbert Forsten, 1999–2002) is the standard reference work for exactly this question and treats Gayadāsa directly. It is **freely available** as full text — Internet Archive item **`Meulenbeld-HIML`**, volumes 1A/1B/2A/2B/3 — and was **not obtained this pass**: two download attempts failed because the item's filenames contain literal spaces (`HIML 1A _djvu.txt`), which neither URL-encoding attempted resolved. This is the single highest-value unread item for the **whole** Āyurveda cluster in this corpus, not only for this node, and it is recorded here with its item ID so the next attempt starts from a known address.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Three+ sources, tested for independence** — **Aufrecht's *Catalogus Catalogorum*** (manuscript catalogue, via Wisdomlib); the **1938 Nirṇayasāgara critical edition's bibliographic record** (Internet Archive, Wellcome copy), which is *primary evidence about what survives* rather than a report of it; and **search-level summaries** of the Āyurvedic secondary literature carrying the date and the "covered the whole text" argument. The first two are independent of the third and of each other.
2. **Commentarial tradition** — the anchor, and this node is a fact *about* it: three named layers on one root text, each in a different state of survival.
3. **Lexicon + morphology** — Devanagari गयादास; *nyāya-candrikā*, "moonlight of reasoning," and *pañjikā*, the technical term for a word-by-word gloss — so the title announces the genre as well as the manner.
4. **Internal consistency** — swept `concepts/`: [[dalhana]] carried an explicitly recorded hole about *which* commentators the *Nibandhasaṅgraha* preserves. This node and [[jejjata]] close it from two directions, and the closing changes how Ḍalhaṇa should be read.
5. **Provenance** — the edition record is good and specific; Aufrecht is good; the dating is the weak link and is labelled as such. The reference work that would settle everything was located and not obtained.

**CONVERGED** on identity, work, the surviving portion, the printing, and the relation to Ḍalhaṇa — no source contradicts another. Confidence **medium**, held down by three named things: the 11th-century dating is single-sourced at search level; nothing is known of the man; and Meulenbeld, the one work that would fix both, was located and failed to download.
