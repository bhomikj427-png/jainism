## Gloss

**Sthitaprajña** (Sanskrit *sthita* "established, steady" + *prajñā* "wisdom, insight") is the Bhagavad Gītā's portrait of the realized sage — "one of steady/established wisdom." The term frames a defining passage: Arjuna asks (BG 2.54) *sthitaprajñasya kā bhāṣā* — "what is the mark of the person of steady wisdom, how does he speak, sit, move?" — and Kṛṣṇa's answer runs to the end of Chapter 2 (BG 2.55–72).

Krishna's defining verse: *prajahāti yadā kāmān sarvān pārtha mano-gatān / ātmany evātmanā tuṣṭaḥ sthitaprajñas tadocyate* (BG 2.55) — "when one casts off all the desires of the mind and is satisfied in the Self by the Self alone, then one is called *sthitaprajña*." The portrait that follows: untroubled in sorrow, free of craving in pleasure, without attachment, fear, or anger (2.56); withdrawing the senses as a tortoise draws in its limbs (2.58); abiding in peace having abandoned all desire and the sense of "I" and "mine" (2.71); established in *brāhmī sthiti*, the state of Brahman, unshaken even at the hour of death (2.72).

The sthitaprajña is thus the **exemplar in whom samatva (equanimity) is fully realized** — the steadiness of BG 2.48 made a settled character. It is the Gītā's living-liberation ideal: wisdom expressed not as withdrawal from action but as unshakable inner balance amid it.

**Cross-tradition note (parallel, not identity).** The sthitaprajña anticipates the Advaita **jīvanmukta** (one liberated while still living) and stands structurally near the Buddhist arhat's equanimity and the Stoic sage — but each is embedded in a different metaphysics of the self (eternal ātman / no-self / rational cosmos) and should not be flattened into one figure.
