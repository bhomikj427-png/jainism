## Gloss / Divergence map

**Atri** is one of the **Saptarṣi** of the present age — with Agastya, Bharadvāja, Gautama, Jamadagni, Vasiṣṭha and Viśvāmitra — and a *mānasaputra*, a **mind-born son of Brahmā**. The *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* associates him with **the tongue**.

His standing rests on a **book**: **Maṇḍala 5 of the Ṛgveda is the *Atri Maṇḍala***, comprising **87 hymns** attributed to him and his descendants, addressed principally to [[agni|Agni]] and [[indra|Indra]]. The family's compositions are noted for exploiting Sanskrit's flexibility — "lexical, syntactic, morphological and verb play" — and **RV 5.44** is judged by scholars "**the most difficult riddle *śloka* in all of the Ṛgveda**."

He is also a **household**: married to **Anasūyā**, counted among the seven female *pativratā*s; father of three sons distributed one per god — **Candra** (moon) as an *aṃśa* of Brahmā, **Dattātreya** as an *aṃśa* of Viṣṇu, **Durvāsas** as an *aṃśa* of Śiva. In the *Rāmāyaṇa* he and Anasūyā receive and counsel [[rama|Rāma]] and Sītā at their hermitage at Citrakūṭa. A fragmentary ***Atri Saṃhitā*** transmits ethical precepts on self-restraint, charity and compassion within the Vaikhānasa Vaiṣṇava tradition of South India.

### "Atri's Eclipse" — a case the charter exists for

**RV 5.40** is a nine-verse hymn. "After four verses in praise of Indra, the fifth verse informs us that a demon named **[[svarbhanu|Svarbhānu]]** pierced the sun and plunged a bewildered world into darkness. The next four verses are in praise of Atri, who is said to have discovered the sun concealed in gloom and, with the power of his prayers, restored it back in the sky."

It is widely repeated that this is "the **earliest reference to a total solar eclipse** in any historical astronomy of the world," and that the eclipse can be **dated**. The corpus's position:

**What is well-founded.** RV 5.40.5–9 describes the sun being obscured and restored, attributes the obscuring to Svarbhānu, and is old. That the Vedic poets registered and cared about solar obscuration is not in doubt, and Svarbhānu is the figure who later splits into [[rahu|Rāhu]] and [[ketu|Ketu]] as the eclipse-causing nodes — so this hymn is a genuine early node in a long tradition of eclipse-thinking. That much the corpus asserts.

**What is not.** The dating is not a retrodiction from the hymn. It is a chain, and the chain visibly fails to converge:

| proposed date | proposer | basis |
|---|---|---|
| **22 October 4202 BCE** | Mayank Vahia (TIFR) and Mitsuru Soma (NAOJ), building on **B. G. Tilak's** interpretation | not the hymn itself but references in the ***Śāṅkhāyana Brāhmaṇa*** to "the location of the rising Sun during the spring equinox" — whether in Orion or the Pleiades |
| **19 October 3811 BCE** | the **same** two authors | the same method |
| **25 July 3928 BCE** | a separate academia.edu treatment | a different reconstruction |

**Three dates, spanning 391 years, from one hymn** — and two of them from the same pair of authors, meaning the method does not select a unique solution even in its own proponents' hands. The hymn contains **no date**, **no year**, and **no location**; the numbers come from a *different text's* equinox description filtered through a **19th-century** interpretation. Wikipedia's own article on the episode is flagged for "unreliable citations," and concedes that "the language of the Ṛgveda is very symbolic having hidden meaning" while astronomers have "**interpreted**" the disappearance as an observed eclipse.

**The §0 statement.** A hymn in which a demon swallows the sun and a sage restores it *by the power of his prayers* is, on its face, **a ritual and mythological text**. Reading it as an observation log is a decision, not a finding. The corpus therefore records: *the Ṛgveda contains an early and vivid account of solar obscuration attributed to Svarbhānu* — and **declines** the claims that it is a datable observation, that it is demonstrably the world's earliest such record, or that it can date the Ṛgveda. Those are **not refuted here**; they are **not established by anything the corpus has seen**, and the disagreement among their own proponents is the reason.

This is a **variant** of the failure the corpus already records at [[rahu]] and in Ch 21 §4. There the error is reading the swallowing-demon myth *as* an eclipse theory, when the relation between the myth and India's real computational astronomy is "**naming and correlation, not identity**." Here the error runs one step further: the myth is first read as an observation, and the observation is then used to **date the text**. Both mistakes have the same root — a genuine astronomical tradition existed *alongside* the myth, and later readers infer that the myth *was* the astronomy in code. And the dating version has an additional weakness of its own: **a retrodiction method that yields three answers for one hymn is powerful enough to produce an answer for any input, and therefore weak evidence for any particular one.**

**CONTESTED** — not about Atri's biography, which the sources agree on, but about **what RV 5.40 is**. Confidence **medium**: signal 1 is a single encyclopedia article and no Ṛgvedic hymn was read in translation (Jamison & Brereton for Maṇḍala 5 is the obvious first fetch, and would let the eclipse verses be assessed directly rather than through paraphrase); the JAHH paper could not be read; and the three-date table is assembled from encyclopedia and preprint summaries rather than from the papers themselves. **The negative claim in the §0 section is deliberately weaker than the positive claims it declines** — the corpus says the datings are not established by what it has seen, not that they are false.
