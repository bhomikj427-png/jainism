## Gloss

Dignāga (~480–540 CE), student of Vasubandhu and founder of the Buddhist *pramāṇa-vāda* (epistemological) tradition, admits exactly two valid sources of knowledge: **pratyakṣa** (perception) and **anumāna** (inference). Each maps onto a different ontological object: perception reaches *svalakṣaṇa* (unique particulars); inference reaches *sāmānya-lakṣaṇa* (general/universal characteristics). Upamāna and śabda (accepted by Nyāya) are rejected as independent pramāṇas — they reduce to inference. This 2-pramāṇa scheme is the Buddhist-logician answer to the same question Nyāya and Jainism address differently.

## Dignāga's two pramāṇas

| Pramāṇa | Object | Nature | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pratyakṣa** (perception) | *Svalakṣaṇa* — unique momentary particular | Pre-verbal, pre-conceptual, unstructured sensation | Infallible in itself; error enters only with conceptual overlay |
| **Anumāna** (inference) | *Sāmānya-lakṣaṇa* — general characteristics | Conceptual, linguistic, communicable | Operates via *vyāpti* (concomitance); subsumed upamāna and śabda |

**Key move — perception is non-conceptual:** Dignāga defines genuine perception as sensation *devoid of structure* (*kalpanā-apoḍha*) — no names, no universals, no classification attached. Once the mind adds conceptual framing ("this is fire"), the act is no longer pure perception. This directly blocks Nyāya's claim that conceptual determinate perception (*savikalpaka-pratyakṣa*) is still perception.

**Apoha theory (in inference):** Words and universals mean by *exclusion*, not by denoting real properties: "cow" means "not-non-cow." Universals are mental constructs imposed on perceived particulars, not real features of objects. This is Buddhist nominalism applied to language and inference.

## Three-tradition epistemology comparison

| Feature | Jain (2 pramāṇas) | Buddhist/Dignāga (2 pramāṇas) | Nyāya (4 pramāṇas) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number** | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| **Perception** | Includes non-sensory direct knowledge (kevala-jñāna) | Non-conceptual sensation only; no supernatural knowledge | Sensory + conceptual; two sub-types |
| **Inference** | Subsumed under parokṣa (indirect) | Independent pramāṇa; covers universals | Independent pramāṇa; 5-member syllogism |
| **Universals** | Real (realism) | Nominal constructs (nominalism) | Real substances with real universals |
| **Permanent self** | Yes (jīva persists) | No (anattā) | Yes (ātman persists) |
| **God** | Rejected | Not relevant | Accepted (inferred) |

**The deepest split:** What is perception? Dignāga says it is pre-conceptual (non-verbal, infallible sensation). Nyāya says it can be conceptual and still valid. Jainism says direct knowledge includes omniscient non-sensory awareness. These are three incompatible answers to one question.

## Historical impact

Dignāga's framework inaugurated the Buddhist logic tradition (*pramāṇa-vāda*) that continued through Dharmakīrti (~7th c. CE) and profoundly influenced Tibetan Buddhist epistemology. His critique of Nyāya realism drove a centuries-long debate; Jain logicians (notably Akalaṅka, ~8th c.) also responded to the Buddhist 2-pramāṇa framework.
