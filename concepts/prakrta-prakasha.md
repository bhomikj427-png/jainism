## Reasoning across signals

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The one-word proof, and why the corpus had the right fact and the wrong finding

Batch 49 recorded the 8→12 expansion as its "most interesting claim and its least well sourced," and read it as the third instance of a **terminal-module** pattern — an architectural habit shared with [[candra-vyakarana]] and [[hemacandra]]. **The fact is confirmed. The reading was too small.**

Ollett's evidence is a single word:

> "What had earlier been 'Prakrit' was reconfigured… as the species **'Māhārāṣṭrī': crucially, the word appears in the expanded version of Vararuci's *Light*, but not the older version.**"

⭐⭐⭐ **The core text never names its own subject, because it had no need to.** It was simply *Prakrit*. The label **Māhārāṣṭrī appears only once there is something for it to be contrasted with** — and that is exactly when the three dialect chapters arrive. The accretion is therefore detectable **from the vocabulary of the text itself**, independently of any manuscript count. ⚠ **This is the kind of argument the corpus should hold up as a standard**: it is falsifiable, it rests on the text rather than on a colophon, and it explains *why* the addition happened rather than merely dating it.

And what it explains is not bookkeeping. Ollett:

> "Previously, knowledge of Prakrit meant knowledge of the grammar, lexicon, and metrical forms of Prakrit **literature**… The redactors of *Light on Prakrit* clearly considered it a **genus**. What had earlier been 'Prakrit' was reconfigured… as the **species** 'Māhārāṣṭrī'… Pluralization meant that Prakrit, now Māhārāṣṭrī, no longer stood **above** the other languages, but **alongside** them."

⭐⭐⭐ **Adding three chapters demoted the book's own subject from a genus to a species.** The text did not merely grow; **its object changed category.** ⚠ And Ollett draws the consequence that makes it philosophy rather than book-history: "**genera do not have specific characteristics, and in this case, they do not have grammars.**" To write grammars of the Prakrits *plural* is to concede that "Prakrit" has become a container with nothing of its own to describe.

⭐⭐ **This is the corpus's terminal-module finding, corrected upward.** The three fates stand — but the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*'s "accretion" is now the only one of the three whose consequence was **conceptual**:

| work | terminal module | fate | ⭐**[B50]** what the fate *did* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cāndra** ([[candragomin]]) | Vedic (7), svara (8), 86 [[paribhasha\|paribhāṣās]] | **lost** (the Vedic pair) | nothing conceptual — a transmission accident, as [[candra-vyakarana]] established |
| **Siddhahemacandra** ([[hemacandra]]) | book 8, Prākṛt | **detached** | made the Prākṛt grammar independently famous |
| ***Prākṛtaprakāśa*** | 3 dialect sections | **accreted** | ⭐⭐⭐ **demoted its own core's subject from genus to species** |

⚠ **And the corpus must record that its earlier reading was not wrong so much as under-powered**: it had the shape of the event and none of its content, because it had read *about* the text and not *in* it. **That is drift D1, stated as a worked example rather than a metric.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The text at first hand: what it actually is

Counted directly from Cowell's translation, section by section (⭐ **the corpus's first structural description of this text from the text**):

| § | subject | sūtras | | § | subject | sūtras |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | vowels (`ādeḥ ataḥ`) | 44 | | VII | conjugation | 34 |
| II | single consonants | 82 | | VIII | verbal roots | 71 |
| III | conjunct consonants | 66 | | IX | particles (*nipāta*) | 18 |
| IV | "mixed" rules | 33 | | **X** | **Paiśācī** | **13** |
| V | declension | 47 | | **XI** | **Māgadhī** | **17** |
| VI | pronouns | 64 | | **XII** | **Śaurasenī** | **32** |

⭐ **Core 459 sūtras; the three dialect modules 62 — 11.9% of the work.** The whole grammar of three languages is shorter than the chapter on single consonants.

⭐⭐ **And the modules are *diffs*, which is why they were appendable.** Sections X and XI each open with the same second sūtra — **`prakṛtiḥ śaurasenī`**, glossed by Cowell as "the original of this dialect [i.e., **that which forms the base on which its peculiarities are engrafted**] is the Śaurasenī." Each module declares a base and lists only departures from it. Hemacandra does the identical thing and closes his Śaurasenī chapter with **`śeṣaṃ prākṛtavat`** — "the rest is as in Prākṛt" (8.4.286, printed by Cowell as Appendix C).

⚠⚠ **A correction the corpus makes to itself, inside this batch, before committing it.** On first reading Cowell it looked as though the *Prākṛtaprakāśa* had an elegant two-level derivation tree — core → Śaurasenī → {Paiśācī, Māgadhī} — and that this was **Vararuci's** design. ⭐ **Ollett shows it cannot be**: the base text had no dialect sections at all. **The derivation tree is the redactors' architecture, not the author's**, and attributing it to Vararuci would have been precisely the §0 error of reading a later state of a text as an original intention — the same error [[candra-vyakarana]] caught Abhyankar making. **Caught by holding the second source open before writing, not after.**

⭐⭐ **The order is wrong, and the wrongness is evidence.** Paiśācī (X) and Māgadhī (XI) both declare **Śaurasenī** their base — and Śaurasenī is printed **last**, as XII, so that Cowell must forward-reference ("cf. xii. 1"). ⭐ **Two modules stand before the module they depend on.** That is not how an author arranges a book; it is how modules accumulate.

### ⭐⭐⭐ What the node said it did not know, now answered

Batch 49 closed this node by naming its own hole: it knew the text's "shape, attribution, dialect coverage and editorial history" and "**nothing about its contents** — not one rule, not its method, not how it states the relation between Prākṛt and Sanskrit, **which is the question a reader would most want answered**." All four are now answered.

**The relation to Sanskrit is a single sūtra: `śeṣaṃ saṃskṛtāt`** — "the rest, from Sanskrit" (Ollett n. 72, citing *Light* 8.23). ⭐⭐⭐ **The entire grammar is a diff against Sanskrit.** It describes only what changes; everything not mentioned is imported wholesale. That is why a 521-sūtra book can constitute a language — it never has to say what Prākṛt *is*, only how it differs. ⚠ And it is why "Prakrit" is definitionally derivative: [[hemacandra]] states the doctrine outright, in Cowell's quotation — "*prakṛtiḥ saṃskṛtam, tatra-bhavaṃ tata āgataṃ vā prākṛtam*": the *prakṛti* (source, base) is Sanskrit; what arises from it or comes from it is *prākṛta*. ⚠ **A folk-etymological definition doing structural work, and the corpus flags it as such rather than endorsing it as linguistics.**

**The method is Pāṇini's, and this is T4 confirmed from outside the Sanskrit grammars.** Read at first hand in Cowell: sūtra **I.1 `ādeḥ ataḥ` is an *adhikāra*** ("supposed to exercise an authority, by virtue of which it is implied in the succeeding sūtras"); **II.1 likewise**; **IV.1 turns on a *pratyāhāra*** ("*ach* is here of course the *pratyāhāra*"); and Cowell must gloss I.1's *t* by citing **[[panini|Pāṇini]] I.1.70**. Ollett states the general point: "**His use of Sanskrit as a metalanguage, of concise transformational rules, and of technical terms and abbreviations sets *Light* far apart from the general descriptions of Prakrit**" that preceded it.

⭐⭐⭐ **This is the strongest form of Batch 49's T4 yet.** T4 established that *paribhāṣā* was shared across nine grammatical systems and across śāstra generally. **Here the shared metalanguage crosses the language boundary**: a grammar of a *different language* is written in Pāṇini's technical apparatus and is unreadable without it. ⚠ The corpus states this as an observation about **technique**, not about descent — Ollett's own framing is that these projects sought "to **liberate** these techniques… from the tradition of the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*."

### ⭐⭐⭐ The Vararuci attribution has a *mechanism*, and a specialist names it

The corpus has treated the Vararuci tangle as a **collision** — an accident of homonymy to be distrusted. Ollett gives it a function:

> "Perhaps because of what many perceived to be his critical attitude toward Pāṇini in his *vārttikas*, **Vararuci-Kātyāyana was the go-to sage for authorizing additions and interventions in these new non-Pāṇinian systems.**"

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That is a seventh collision generator, and it is different in kind from the six Batch 49 catalogued.** Those six — personal name, place-name, doctrine-name, short form, genre label, title in another śāstra — are all **mechanisms of confusion**: two things accidentally share a label. This one is a **mechanism of authorization**: the name is attached *on purpose*, because a text needs a warrant to depart from Pāṇini and Vararuci is the sage who is on record departing from Pāṇini. ⚠ **The attributions are not errors. They are claims.** And Ollett generalises the point in a sentence worth keeping: "**The fragile originary connection between a man and his work, moving forward through time, collides against the will to remember otherwise — to reach back into the past and overwrite it, to reassign identities, to constantly reauthorize the text.**"

⚠ **The corpus does not thereby drop its distrust**; it now distinguishes two questions that it had been running together — *is this the same man?* (usually no) and *why was he named?* (usually a reason). See [[vararuci]], where the four-way split is recorded, and note that **nine** Kātyāyanas were catalogued in 1862.

### ⭐⭐⭐ The Jain hypothesis — and why it matters here more than anywhere else

Ollett's positive evidence: certain forms **taught in *Light*** are attested **only in early Jain texts** — the past tense in *-ia* (*Light* 6.23, first spotted in literature by Alsdorf in 1936, in the *Vasudevahiṇḍi*) and the first-person locative singular *mae* (5.92, spotted by Esposito in 2011, again in the *Vasudevahiṇḍi*). He then states the evidential asymmetry precisely — ⭐ **and it is a rule worth the corpus keeping**:

> "Forms taught by Vararuci that occur in Jain literature **and nowhere else** have greater weight in regard to the question of the grammar's target language than forms occurring in Jain literature and nowhere else **that are not taught by Vararuci**."

Hence: "It may even be possible that *Light on Prakrit* was **composed by a Jain author in a Jain literary milieu**, and… non-Jain authors found it necessary to **reattribute** the text to Vararuci-Kātyāyana."

⚠ **Ollett hedges twice ("may even be possible") and so does this node.** But the consequence for *this* corpus is specific and large: the anchor text of the whole repository is the **Tattvārtha Sūtra**, the Jain material is its deepest layer, and **its first Prākṛt-grammar node may belong to that layer under a Brahminical name.** ⭐ **Recorded as a live hypothesis with named evidence, not as a finding** — and note that it is the *same* mechanism as the paragraph above, running the other way: the name did not collide onto the text, it was **applied** to it.

### ⭐⭐⭐ Two scholars, one text, incompatible sūtra numbers — the warning and its vindication, 163 years apart

Cowell, 1854, on his six manuscripts:

> "The most important variation is in **the number of the Sūtras**, some MSS. containing many which are not found in the others; and as **no record appears to have been preserved of the original number**, it is a question of some difficulty to decide in particular instances."

Ollett, 2017, cites *Light* at **5.92** and **8.23**. ⭐⭐ **Neither resolves in Cowell.** Cowell's Section V ends at **47**; his **VIII.23 is `jño jāṇamuṇau`** (the substitutes *jāṇa*/*muṇa* for the root *jñā*), not `śeṣaṃ saṃskṛtāt`. ⭐⭐⭐ **The accretion Cowell warned was unrecoverable is measurable in a modern citation that will not resolve in his edition — nearly double the sūtras in section V.** Ollett's own note 33 explains why: "the conspectus edition of Baladeva Upadhyaya (1972)… **prints the recensions of Vasantarāja (and the anonymous *Cluster of Blossoms*) and [[bhamaha|Bhāmaha]] separately.**"

⭐⭐⭐ **There is no "the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*." There are recensions, and each is named by its commentary.** Ollett's bibliography lists **five editions of this one text**, four of them distinguished by which commentary they carry. ⚠ **This is Ch 45 §1.2 pushed one step further.** Ch 45 (from Ajotikar, Ajotikar & Scharf) established that commentaries are the **older and better evidence**, because manuscripts do not last. Here the commentary is not better evidence about the text — **it is what individuates the text.** And [[bhamaha]] records the endpoint of that logic: where the commentary is *lost* (Section XII), Cowell had the printed sūtras in front of him and **declined to translate them**.

### ⭐⭐ Two corrections to the corpus's own edges, and one to Cowell

**(a) The [[katantra]] parallel is real but narrower than recorded.** Ollett: "*Light on Prakrit*'s **debts to the tradition of *Kātantra* have been overlooked, perhaps because they are obvious**" — shared topical (rather than element-wise) arrangement; shared technical vocabulary (Renou 1938: *āmantraṇa-* "vocative," *bhūta-* "past," *bhaviṣyat-* "future"); and *Light* puts nominal suffixes at the end of a "miscellaneous" chapter just as the Kātantra's nominal-suffix section "is believed to be a secondary addition **by none other than Vararuci-Kātyāyana**." ⚠ **But Ollett's own note 27 blocks the stronger claim**: "**The list of topics, however, is very different**: the *Kātantra* deals with sandhi, nouns, and verbs; the *Light* with… vowels, single consonants, conjunct consonants, then a 'mixed' set of rules, and then nominal morphology, verbal morphology, verbal roots, and indeclinables." ⭐ **That list is exactly the nine sections counted at first hand above — an independent confirmation of the corpus's own reading.** So: **the parallel is one of method, not of content**, and the edge note is corrected accordingly.

**(b) ⚠ Grierson's "two schools," and a warning that reaches back into Batch 49.** Ollett demolishes the standard Eastern/Western division of Prākṛt grammarians in three moves: there were no "**schools**" (no core doctrines, no self-identification — at best "**traditions**," meaning authors who rely on common sources); the **regionality is a false equivalence** between where an author lived and what language he described; and the figure of two schools falsely implies parallel isolated development, when "all of the 'western' grammarians… relied directly or indirectly upon the 'eastern' *Light*, and 'eastern' writers like Mārkaṇḍeya relied heavily on the 'western' Hemacandra." ⭐⭐ **Batch 49 finding 6 concluded, from Abhyankar, that "Pāṇinian grammar had regions" — Eastern ([[jinendrabuddhi]]), Southern ([[haradatta]]), Benares.** ⚠ Ollett does **not** refute that; Abhyankar's regions are lineages of *commentarial preference* ("Benares follows the *Padamañjarī* more than the *Nyāsa*"), which is Ollett's rehabilitated sense — **traditions, not schools**. ⭐ **But he supplies the word and the three inferences not to draw**, and Batch 49 had refused only one of them (the theological one). **Recorded here and flagged to [[haradatta]] and [[jinendrabuddhi]].**

**(c) ⚠ Cowell is wrong about Hemacandra's century.** Cowell 1854: "Hemacandra was a grammarian of the Jaina sect, who flourished in the **thirteenth century**." Ollett: he "compiled his grammar of the 'six languages' around **the middle of the twelfth century**," a Śvetāmbara monk at the Cālukya court of Aṇahilavāḍa under Jayasiṃha and then Kumārapāla. ⭐ **The corpus's own [[hemacandra]] node already carries 12th-century dates, so Cowell is corrected by the corpus as well as by Ollett.** ⚠ This is the **standing Batch 47 caution, fourth instance**: an editor is authoritative on what his discipline knows internally — Cowell's manuscript stemma, his collation, his reading of Bhāmaha — and **out of date on anything that depends on outside chronology.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Cowell's Kaccāyana inference, and the corpus's rule against it — vindicated

Batch 49 carried as follow-up #12: "⚠ **Is the Kaccāyana/Dravidian influence claim true?** Single-sourced, and if it holds, T1's correction is much larger than stated." The Kaccāyana half is now answerable, and the answer is instructive because **the two authorities split and the corpus's own rule picks the winner in advance.**

**Cowell 1854** argues that the Prākṛt and Pāli grammars are one tradition, and his ground is purely onomastic: "**Kachchāyano is simply the Prakrit form of Kātyāyana**, there can be little doubt that the Prakrit grammar of the one and the Pali grammar of the other, are only the **Brahmanical and Buddhist versions of the same tradition**." He is equally frank that the whole Vararuci=Kātyāyana identification rests on "**the universal popular belief in India**, and the direct testimony of **Somadeva**" — i.e. the *Kathāsaritsāgara*, a 12th-century story collection.

**Ollett** treats the identical fact as evidence of a shared *attribution practice*: the Pāli grammar is "likewise attributed to Kātyāyana (Kaccāyana in Pali)," and "**centuries after** the historical Kātyāyana composed his *vārttikas*, his name… **was attached to** projects that sought to apply the principles and techniques of Sanskrit grammar to Middle Indic languages."

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Batch 49's rule — "distrust identifications that rest on the name *alone*" — was formulated from grammar-internal evidence and, applied here, picks Ollett over Cowell without knowing either.** ⚠ And note what Cowell's own grounds are: **popular belief plus the *Kathāsaritsāgara*** — which is **exactly the evidential circle [[sarvavarman]] identified as this corpus's most striking finding of Batch 49**, and Cowell states it openly as his warrant rather than concealing it. ⭐ **Cowell is not caught out; he is transparent, and 1854 transparency is what makes 2026 correction possible.** ⚠ The **Dravidian** half of follow-up #12 remains **untested** — nothing read this batch bears on it.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** ⭐ **Three first-hand sources, no editorial descent between them, 163 and 56 years apart.** **Cowell 1854** — a critical edition from six collated MSS with an English translation: the section structure, sūtra counts, `prakṛtiḥ śaurasenī`, the *adhikāra*/*pratyāhāra* apparatus, Bhāmaha's maṅgala, the lost Section XII, the sūtra-count warning, the Sañjīvanī's duplicated-colophon miscount, and the Hemacandra dating error. **Ollett 2017** — a peer-reviewed open-access monograph: the Māhārāṣṭrī one-word proof, the genus/species reading, Nitti-Dolci's grammar-of-a-text, the Jain hypothesis with its two forms, the authorization mechanism, the Kātantra debt with its limit, the Grierson critique, `śeṣaṃ saṃskṛtāt`, and the recension situation. **Abhyankar 1961** — the Vararuci (2) split, Caṇḍa, Hemacandra's eight books. ⚠ **They disagree**, and the disagreements are the node's best content: Cowell vs Ollett on Kaccāyana, on Hemacandra's century, and implicitly on whether "the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*" is one book.

**Status: CONTESTED, confidence medium.** ⚠ **Confidence is deliberately NOT raised**, although far more is now known: the **shape, method, architecture, textual history and conceptual stakes are secure**, but the batch's reading made the **authorship *less* settled, not more** — Ollett's Jain hypothesis and Westergaard's nine Kātyāyanas both widen what Abhyankar's four-way split had narrowed. ⭐ **A node can learn a great deal and honestly stay at medium.**
