## Gloss / Divergence map

The ***Prākṛtaprakāśa*** is "probably the **earliest surviving Prakrit grammar**," attributed to **[[vararuci|Vararuci]]** — specifically to Abhyankar's **Vararuci (2)**, "who also was **Kātyāyana by Gotra name**," "a grammarian **later than [[patanjali|Patañjali]]**," and the author of the fourth adhyāya of the [[katantra|Kātantra]]. ⭐ **It is this corpus's first node for Prākṛt grammar of any kind** — a gap [[vararuci]] named in Batch 48 and this batch was opened to close.

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐⭐ The architectural finding, now with three instances and three different outcomes.** [[candra-vyakarana]] established, against Abhyankar, that the Cāndra grammar had **terminal** chapters — Vedic (7) and svara (8) — "placed at the end of his work rather than following Pāṇini's practice of distributing them throughout," and that they were **lost**. This node and Abhyankar's Hemacandra entry show that terminal modularity is not a peculiarity of that book but **the normal architecture of the non-Pāṇinian grammars**:

| work | core | terminal module(s) | what happened to the module |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cāndra** ([[candragomin]]) | 6 adhyāyas, classical Sanskrit | ch. 7 **Vedic**, ch. 8 **svara**; and **86 [[paribhasha|paribhāṣās]] "given at the end"** | ⭐ the Vedic pair **lost**; the paribhāṣā appendix **survived** |
| **Siddhahemacandra** ([[hemacandra]]) | 7 books, Sanskrit, "based practically upon Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī" | **book 8 = Prākṛt**, "a Grammar of **all** the Prakrit dialects" | ⭐ **detached and became the famous part** |
| ***Prākṛtaprakāśa*** | 8 (or 9) chapters, **Māhārāṣṭrī** | **3 dialect chapters** — Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, Paiśācī | ⭐ **accreted**: the text grew from 8 to 12 |

⭐⭐ **Same design, three fates: lost, detached, accreted.** ⚠ **And this retroactively strengthens the [[candra-vyakarana]] correction rather than merely repeating it**: if terminal modules are the norm, then "chapters 7 and 8 stood at the end and are missing" is the *ordinary* thing to expect of such a book, and Abhyankar's reading of the gap as an authorial choice was not a freak error but a **systematic** one — the same misreading is available for every work in this table. ⚠ **The corpus does not extend the correction to Hemacandra or the Kātantra on this basis**; it records that the *possibility* is now open for both, where before it had not occurred to anyone here.

**⭐ Pāṇini interleaves; the rivals modularise. And that is the same fact as the pedagogy.** [[katantra]] found the rival systems arranged **topic-wise** on the pre-Pāṇinian model rather than element-wise on Pāṇini's. A topic-wise book has separable topics. ⭐ **The organisation that makes a grammar teachable is the organisation that makes its parts detachable** — which is why this literature's Vedic chapters go missing, its Prakrit book becomes a separate classic, and its dialect sections multiply. ⚠ **No source consulted states this**; it is the corpus's synthesis across four first-hand entries and two independent reports, and it is offered as a pattern with three instances, not a law.

**⭐⭐ A resonance the corpus must state *with the split intact*, because it is not evidence of anything.** [[sarvavarman]] established, from the *Kathāsaritsāgara* and the *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia*, that **Guṇāḍhya lost the wager over the [[katantra|Kātantra]], abandoned Sanskrit and Prākṛt, and composed the *Bṛhatkathā* in Paiśācī** — and that **Vararuci is a character in that same story.** And the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*, attributed to a Vararuci, is one of the very few texts that **codifies Paiśācī**.

⚠ **This is a coincidence of names and the corpus says so plainly.** Abhyankar's whole point at [[vararuci]] is that **four different men** bear that name, and the Prakrit grammarian is **#2 — "later than Patañjali"** — while the *Kathāsaritsāgara*'s Vararuci belongs to the legendary layer. ⭐ **The apparent resonance — "the man in the Paiśācī origin-legend wrote the Paiśācī grammar" — is manufactured entirely by the name collision, and it is exactly the kind of pleasing pattern §0 exists to stop the corpus from asserting.** It is recorded because **noticing it and refusing it is the finding**: this is what the collision *does* to a reader, and Batch 48 showed the tradition itself succumbing to it.

**⚠ What is genuinely uncertain here, and the corpus does not tidy it.** Abhyankar's own cross-reference problem recurs: elsewhere he says of a work attributed to Kātyāyana that "**this work possibly was not a grammar work and its author also was not the same as the Vārttikakāra Kātyāyana**," and that its name "was given **possibly** to Kātyāyana's Prākṛt Grammar, the author of which was **Vararuci surnamed Kātyāyana**" (citing *Mahābhāṣya* Vol. VII p. 395). ⭐ **Three hedges in two sentences, on the attribution of the corpus's oldest Prakrit grammar.** [[vararuci]] rates the four-way split as first-rate reasoning on thin corroboration; **this node inherits exactly that rating and does not improve it.**

**⭐ The scholarly apparatus exists and is old.** **E. B. Cowell**, *The Prākṛta-prakāśa: or, the Prākṛit Grammar of Vararuchi, with the Commentary (Manoramā) of Bhāmaha* — **Hertford, S. Austin, 1854**, and it is **an edition with an English translation**. ⭐ **That makes this node's primary text reachable under §1 without any from-scratch translation, and it is on archive.org.** Caṇḍa's rival *Prākṛtalakṣaṇa* was edited by **A. F. R. Hoernle**, Calcutta 1880. ⚠ **Neither has been read.** ⭐ Note the pattern this batch keeps producing: [[katantra]] and [[candra-vyakarana]] were both put into modern circulation by **Bruno Liebich**; the two oldest Prakrit grammars by **Cowell** and **Hoernle**. **The whole non-Pāṇinian shelf reaches the modern reader through a handful of nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century European editors** — and Abhyankar, notably, gives both **Liebich** and **Pischel** ("a famous European Grammarian… who wrote a work entitled *Prakrit Grammar*") headwords among the ācāryas.

**⚠ And the corpus should be clear about what it has *not* got.** This node knows the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*'s **shape, attribution, dialect coverage and editorial history**. It knows **nothing about its contents** — not one rule, not its method, not how it states the relation between Prākṛt and Sanskrit, which is the question a reader would most want answered. ⭐ **That question has a named modern authority — Andrew Ollett, *Language of the Snakes* (an open-access monograph) and "Ghosts from the past: India's undead languages" (2014)** — and both were located and **neither could be retrieved** (Project MUSE serves chapters no longer individually; the author's own page returned 403). Carried as the node's top follow-up.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Abhyankar** at first hand — Vararuci (2) as the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*'s author and a Kātantra co-author; the hedged Kātyāyana-attribution note with its *Mahābhāṣya* Vol. VII p. 395 citation; **Caṇḍa** and the Candragomin identification "by some"; **Hemacandra's** eight books with the eighth on Prākṛt; and the **Pischel** and **Liebich** headwords. **Independent search-level reports** — the *Prākṛtaprakāśa* as "probably the earliest surviving Prakrit grammar"; 8 chapters expanded to 12; the four dialects and their distribution; **Cowell 1854** with Bhāmaha's *Manoramā*; **Hoernle 1880**; and Caṇḍa's "very controversial" date. ⚠ **These agree on everything checkable and diverge from Abhyankar on nothing** — but they are search summaries, and ⭐ **the one thing they add that Abhyankar lacks (the 8→12 expansion) is the node's most interesting claim and its least well sourced.** **CONTESTED**, confidence **medium** — contested on the oldest-grammar question, on the chapter count, and on Caṇḍa's identity; ⚠ **the text has not been read, and a free English translation of it has existed since 1854.**
