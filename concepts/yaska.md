## Gloss / Divergence map

**Yāska** is the author of the [[nirukta|*Nirukta*]], and this node exists because the corpus needed the **person** apart from the **discipline and the text** — the split [[panini]]/[[astadhyayi]] already models. He is the earliest Indian author whose analysis of language survives whole, and he is the one who **answered Kautsa**.

⭐ **Batch 47 left the corpus holding "the Vedic mantras are meaningless" without a reply.** Follow-up #3 recorded the gap exactly: "The corpus has the objection and **not Yāska's answer**," the relevant paper being paywalled. That gap is now closed, and closed **at the primary level** — Lakshman Sarup's critical edition and English translation (Oxford, 1920–21) is public domain and was read directly.

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐ Kautsa's six arguments and Yāska's six replies, read at first hand.** *Nirukta* 1.15 states the objection — "If (the object of the science) is to ascertain the meaning of Vedic stanzas, **it is useless**, says Kautsa, **for the Vedic stanzas have no meaning**" — and supports it six ways. 1.16 answers each in order.

| # | Kautsa (1.15) | Yāska (1.16) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Propositions **have their words fixed, their order too immutably fixed** — formula, not speech | "it is **the same with regard to the everyday speech of the world**, as 'Indra and Agni', 'father and son'" |
| 2 | The **Brāhmaṇa**, not the mantra, enjoins the act — "Spread it wide," and so he spreads | "a **mere reiteration** of what has been said already" — dismissed as not a new argument |
| 3 | Their meaning is **impossible** — "Save him, O plant!"; while striking, "Do not injure him, O Axe!" | "no injury is to be inflicted, so it must be understood by the authority of the Vedic passage" |
| 4 | Their meaning is **contradictory** — "There was but one Rudra and no second" vs. "Rudras… thousands without number" | "the same objection is applicable to the everyday speech of the world, as 'this Brahmana has no rival', 'this king has no enemies'" |
| 5 | They **enjoin someone already acquainted** — "Address the hymn to Agni which is being kindled" | "in salutation a person announces his name to one who is already acquainted with it" |
| 6 | Their meaning is **obscure** — *āmyak*, *yādṛśmin*, *jārayāyi*, *kāṇukā* | ⚠ "it is **not the fault of the post if the blind man does not see it**; it is the fault of the man himself" |

**⭐ The structure of the defence is the finding, and it is visible only when the replies are laid against the objections.** **Four of the six replies work by exhibiting the same feature in ordinary language.** Fixed collocations? Ordinary speech has them. Contradiction? Ordinary speech has it. Redundant address? Ordinary speech has it. Over-broad identification ("Aditi is everything")? "all fluids reside in water." And the whole answer opens on that principle: "Vedic stanzas are significant, **because their words are identical with those of the spoken language**."

⭐ **Yāska defends the Veda's meaningfulness by assimilating it to ordinary speech, not by exalting it above ordinary speech.** The revealed text is saved by being shown to behave like everyone's language. That is a **linguistic** defence of scripture, and it is the reason this passage matters to a history of linguistics rather than only to a history of theology.

⚠ **One qualification the corpus adds to its own source.** Sarup judges that Yāska "is altogether **free from fanaticism, bigotry, and intolerance** when he meets Kautsa's adverse criticism… but gives **rational answers** to the various points of objection." That is right for replies 1, 3, 4 and 5. **Reply 6 is not an argument** — it is an insult, and it answers the objection that is hardest to answer: Sarup's own footnotes record that *āmyak*, *yādṛśmin*, *jārayāyi* and *kāṇukā* each "occurs **once only** in the RV." ⭐ **Kautsa's sixth argument is precisely about hapax legomena — words with no comparative evidence at all — and there Yāska changes the subject to the reader's eyesight.** ⚠ Recorded as this corpus's reading of the passage Sarup translated, not as a correction of Sarup's overall verdict, which concerns Yāska's conduct across the exchange and is otherwise well supported.

**⭐ Sarup's meta-observation, which is the strongest thing in his Introduction.** "It is **inconceivable** that the learned theologians would reproduce, in their orthodox books, a controversy which challenges the most fundamental beliefs of their religion" — and yet they transmitted it. He draws the consequence both ways: it shows "it was possible to carry on such discussions **with tolerance** at that period," and it implies "**Kautsa was an eminent scholar**… whose thought could not be ignored." ⚠ **The survival of the objection is itself the evidence.** Sarup further guesses the movement's origin lies in **sectarianism** — Atharvaveda partisans needing to demonstrate their Veda's superiority — and flags this as conjecture.

**⚠ The objections are not only Yāska's problem.** Sarup's apparatus cross-references Kautsa's arguments to the **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā Sūtra i.2.31–38** at five separate points. The same six-part attack on mantra-meaning stands in the Mīmāṃsā *pūrvapakṣa*. ⭐ **Two disciplines preserved the same sceptical argument and each answered it in its own idiom** — Yāska by appeal to ordinary language, Mīmāṃsā (see [[mimamsa-sutra]]) within its own theory of injunction. ⚠ The corpus has **not** read PM i.2.31–38 and does not here claim priority in either direction; the parallel is Sarup's, and it is recorded as a lead.

**The four word-classes.** Yāska divides words into **nāma** (noun), **ākhyāta** (verb), **upasarga** (preverb/prefix) and **nipāta** (particle) — "the first two established by **definition**, the remaining two by **enumeration**." ⭐ **A classification that is half definitional and half a list** — and it is the earliest surviving parts-of-speech scheme in the tradition. ⚠ Reported from secondary sources; **not** located in Sarup at first hand this batch.

**Predecessors, and the pattern the corpus keeps meeting.** Yāska names earlier authorities — **Śākaṭāyana**, **Gārgya** and others — whom [[panini]] also names, and [[nirukta]] records Abhyankar's finding that the *Nighaṇṭu* Yāska explains was itself inherited from collections "very general and numerous." **The earliest surviving author is again working on material already old.** This is [[vyakarana]]'s ten-lost-predecessors finding in a second discipline.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Sarup 1920–21** (critical edition + translation + introduction, **read at first hand** — the whole Kautsa exchange, the historicity argument, the apparatus), **Abhyankar** via [[nirukta]] (the *Nighaṇṭu*'s priority), and the **secondary literature** reported in search (the four word-classes; the Śākaṭāyana/Gārgya dispute; the 2021 *J. Indian Philos.* paper's thesis, from its title and abstract only). Independent. Confidence **medium** rather than high: the word-classes and the Gārgya dispute were **not** verified in Sarup, the date is inherited rather than re-derived, and no Sanskrit was read — Sarup's English is the object here.
