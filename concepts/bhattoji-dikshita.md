## Gloss

**Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita** (c. 1550–1630; settled in Varanasi before 1600) is the grammarian who **changed the order in which Pāṇini is read** — and thereby changed what "learning Pāṇini" means. His ***Siddhāntakaumudī*** (fully *Vaiyākaraṇasiddhāntakaumudī*) "re-arranges the sūtras of Pāṇini **under appropriate heads** and offers exposition that is orderly and easy to follow." It became the standard teaching text of the tradition's last three centuries, and it is the book [[nagesha-bhatta|Nāgeśa]] commented on.

He was a Brahmin, reported from Maharashtra or Andhra; his teacher was **Śeṣa Kṛṣṇa**, himself the commentator (*Prakāśa*) on Rāmacandra's *Prakriyākaumudī*; his patrons were the **Keladi/Ikkeri** rulers **Veṅkaṭappa Nāyaka I** and **Vīrabhadra**. His works are the **Siddhāntakaumudī**, the **Śabdakaustubha** (on the [[mahabhashya|Mahābhāṣya]]), the **Vaiyākaraṇasiddhāntakārikā**, and the ***Prauḍhamanoramā*** — ⭐ **a commentary he wrote on his own book**, "written by the author himself to explain fully in a scholarly manner the popular grammar written by him" (Abhyankar). His grandson **Haridīkṣita** taught Nāgeśa; his disciple **Varadarāja** abridged the Siddhāntakaumudī into the *Laghusiddhāntakaumudī*.

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐ The finding: what the re-ordering cost, stated by the sources rather than inferred.** Pāṇini's sequence groups rules by **operation**; a single derivation therefore requires rules from all over the book. Wikipedia's *Siddhāntakaumudī* article puts the pedagogical problem plainly — in the Aṣṭādhyāyī's own order "formulas of different chapters had to be used to do the same" derivation, whereas the Siddhāntakaumudī collects them "under appropriate headings… so **all sūtras necessary for the achievement of a particular** [derivation] **are available at one place**."

⚠ **The corpus states the consequence and stops short of the inference it is tempted to draw.** A *prakriyā* re-ordering makes derivations learnable and dissolves the sequence in which Pāṇini's rules were designed to interact. Whether that made the *paribhāṣā* literature more necessary — [[nagesha-bhatta|Nāgeśa's]] *Paribhāṣenduśekhara* is the tradition's monument to exactly those metarules, a century later — is **a plausible connection that no source consulted here makes**, and it is therefore recorded as a **question**, not a result. ⚠ It is precisely the kind of tidy causal story this project exists to resist.

**⭐ He is one of the two authorities that fix the received size of the Aṣṭādhyāyī.** Abhyankar, at first hand: "The total number of Sūtras **as commented upon by the writers of the Kāśikā and the Siddhāntakaumudī is 3983**." [[astadhyayi]] already records the resulting spread of counts (3,983 / 3,996 / 3,981 / 3,959) and the reason for it — "the text is stable and its boundary is not." **This node supplies half of the pairing that defines the commonest figure**: [[kasika|the Kāśikā]] (7th c.) and the Siddhāntakaumudī (early 17th) are a millennium apart and are cited together as one canon-fixing authority.

⭐ **And the same operation that fixes the count also shrinks it.** Abhyankar continues: of those 3,983, "**nine sūtras… are described as Vārttikas and two as Gaṇasūtras by Patañjali**." **Reclassifying a line's genre changes the size of the text** — and that is the identical move [[nagesha-bhatta]] made from the other end, where a two-class definition of *Vārttika* cut [[katyayana|Kātyāyana's]] received output from ~5,000 to ~1,400. **The tradition's first great commentator and its last both resize a canon by reclassification rather than by discovery**, and the corpus can now show the operation running on both texts. ⚠ `astadhyayi.md` flags an unreconciled arithmetic in the scan at this point (3,983 − 9 − 2 = 3,972, against a printed 3,979, possibly an OCR digit error); that caveat stands and is not repeated as fact here.

**A student's-eye detail worth keeping.** Abhyankar on the *Prauḍhamanoramā*: "On account of the difficult nature of it, it is usual to read the [Manoramā] **upto the end of the Kāraka-prakaraṇa**." ⭐ **The author's own commentary on his own textbook is, in practice, read partway and abandoned** — and the stopping point is the [[karaka]] section. The tradition tells you where its students give up.

**⚠ A dating error in a general source, flagged not adopted.** The Wikipedia *Siddhāntakaumudī* article states that "the author lived during 1700–1800" and gives a date of 1870 — the first is off by roughly 150 years against the c. 1550–1630 that the same encyclopedia's *Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita* article and every other source support, and the second is a **print edition** mistaken for a composition date. ⭐ **This is the batch's fourth chronology failure in a general reference source**, alongside Kaiyaṭa's unargued century, the Kāśikā's unevidenced one, and Nāgeśa's misparsed genealogy. Batch 47's rule named chronology as the weak point; four independent instances in one batch is no longer anecdote.

**Name-collision, carried from Batch 47 and now attached to a person.** Bhaṭṭoji's teacher **Śeṣa Kṛṣṇa** belongs to the **Śeṣa family of grammarians** — which Batch 47 flagged pre-emptively against Patañjali-as-**Ādiśeṣa** and against **Cakrapāṇi-Śeṣa** (17th-c. grammarian) vs the corpus's **Cakrapāṇidatta** (11th-c. Āyurvedic commentator). The family is real, it is a grammarians' lineage, and it has nothing to do with the serpent. ⚠ Recorded so the collision stays flagged now that a member of the family has entered the corpus by name.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** Abhyankar (first hand: the 3,983 pairing, the *Prauḍhamanoramā* as self-commentary, the Kāraka-prakaraṇa stopping point, the contrast with Vāsudeva Dīkṣita's *Bālamanoramā*); Wikipedia's two articles (biography, teacher, patrons, works, the re-ordering rationale — **and one bad date**); LinguIndic via [[nagesha-bhatta]] (Haridīkṣita as grandson, the teaching lineage). They converge on dates, teacher, patrons and works. **CONVERGED**; confidence **medium**, because no work of Bhaṭṭoji's was read in the original or in translation and the biography rests on general reference sources.
