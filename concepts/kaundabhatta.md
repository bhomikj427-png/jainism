## Gloss

**Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa** (Abhyankar spells him **Koṇḍabhaṭṭa**; "lived in the beginning of the 17th century") is the **son of Raṅgojī and nephew of [[bhattoji-dikshita|Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita]]** — and the tradition's major philosopher of grammar between [[bhartrhari|Bhartṛhari]] and [[nagesha-bhatta|Nāgeśa]]. His ***Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇa*** is an extensive gloss on Bhaṭṭoji's verse text, and the ***Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇasāra*** is "in a way an **abridgment of the Bhūṣaṇa**." He also wrote two independent works, the *Vaiyākaraṇasiddhāntadīpikā* and a ***Sphoṭavāda***.

⭐ **Abhyankar's valuation of him is unusually direct for a dictionary**, and it is the reason this node exists: Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa "was **one of the few writers on the *Arthavicāra* in the Vyākaraṇaśāstra**" — on **meaning**, within a discipline mostly about form — "and his ***Bhūṣaṇasāra* ranks next to the [[vakyapadiya|*Vākyapadīya*]] of Bhartṛhari**." That places a 17th-century work second in a philosophical literature whose first is a 5th-century one, with nothing named between them.

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐ The finding: the direction of borrowing reverses, and the corpus can now show both halves.** Batch 46 established — and Batch 47 confirmed at first hand through Matilal — that [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya's]] signature device, the [[avacchedaka|*avacchedaka*]], has **grammatical** ancestry: Bhartṛhari's pervasive/partitive locus distinction, read through [[helaraja|Helārāja]]. That is traffic running **grammar → logic**, across the 5th to 13th centuries.

By the 17th century it runs the other way. **Bhaṭṭoji and Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa "respond to the views expressed by Naiyāyikas and Mīmāṃsakas by reformulating the association of speech forms with semantic conditions… in terms of *cognition*"** — that is, they restate the grammarians' own semantics in **Navya-Nyāya's** analytic idiom. ⭐ **The discipline that supplied Navya-Nyāya's central instrument ends up re-describing itself in Navya-Nyāya's vocabulary.** ⚠ This is *not* a contradiction and must not be drawn as one: they are separate transfers, five centuries apart, in opposite directions, and each is separately sourced. The corpus records both and asserts no net "influence."

**⭐ The tradition's last philosophical school was a household.** Assembling this batch's five biographical nodes yields one family and one pupil:

> **Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita** (c. 1550–1630) — brother **Raṅgojī** → son **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa** (early 17th c.); and Bhaṭṭoji's grandson **Haridīkṣita** (c. 1650) → pupil **Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa** (fl. c. 1700).

SEP records that **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa and Nāgeśabhaṭṭa** both **rejected Bhartṛhari's unitary sentence-meaning model** — already noted in [[bhartrhari]] as evidence "the tradition did not simply absorb him." ⭐ **The two names SEP pairs as the counter-position to Bhartṛhari are an uncle's son and a grandson's pupil in one Varanasi family.** ⚠ **What this does and does not license:** it explains the *transmission* of a shared position and says nothing about whether the position is right. Nor does the corpus claim the rejection was *caused* by the kinship — Bhaṭṭoji's own *Vaiyākaraṇamatonmajjana*/*Siddhāntakārikā* is the text both men work from, so a common root text is a sufficient explanation. **Recorded as a striking fact about how a doctrine travelled, not as an argument.**

**⚠ A title question left open rather than resolved by inference.** Abhyankar says the *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇa* glosses Bhaṭṭoji's ***Vaiyākaraṇasiddhāntakārikā***; the modern publishing and scholarly descriptions say it is a commentary on Bhaṭṭoji's ***Vaiyākaraṇamatonmajjana***. These are **very likely two names for the same verse text** — it is the obvious reading, and Bhaṭṭoji's kārikā-text is known under more than one title — but no source consulted here states the identification, so **the corpus does not assert it.** Carried as a follow-up. ⚠ This is deliberately the *opposite* of the Batch 47 failure mode: the tempting move is to smooth two titles into one and never mention it.

**⚠ Two *Sphoṭavāda*s — an eighth flagged collision, and the first that is a title rather than a person.** Abhyankar attributes a *Sphoṭavāda* to **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa**; LinguIndic lists a *Sphoṭavāda* among **Nāgeśa's** works. Uncle's son and grandson's pupil, roughly a century apart, in one lineage, on one doctrine. They are **probably two distinct works with the same natural title**, but the corpus has verified neither and **has read neither**. Flagged now, before it can be made. (The earlier seven are listed in the Batch 47 run-log and in [[kasika]].) ⭐ Note the pattern this batch has produced: collisions arise from **personal names** (Vararuci, Patañjali, Kātyāyana), from a **place-name** ([[kasika|Kāśikā]]), and now from a **doctrine-name** — three different generators, one failure.

**On the *Bhūṣaṇasāra* being an abridgement of the *Bhūṣaṇa*.** ⭐ The work Abhyankar ranks "next to the *Vākyapadīya*" is the **shorter** one, and it is the *Bhūṣaṇasāra* that the modern editions, translations and analytical studies overwhelmingly treat. **The tradition's second-ranked philosophical grammar is read in its author's own abridgement** — the same shape as [[bhattoji-dikshita]]'s *Prauḍhamanoramā*, read only "upto the end of the Kāraka-prakaraṇa," and as Varadarāja's *Laghusiddhāntakaumudī* displacing the full *Siddhāntakaumudī*. ⚠ **Three abridgements in one family's output**; whether that is a fact about this school or about Sanskrit pedagogy generally is not established here.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Abhyankar** (first hand: dates, parentage, the four works, the *Arthavicāra* judgement, the Bhartṛhari ranking) and the **modern editorial/scholarly literature** reported in search (family and dates independently; the semantics significance; the Navya-Nyāya reformulation; the *Vaiyākaraṇamatonmajjana* title), plus **SEP** on the Bhartṛhari rejection. They agree on parentage, relation and century without overlap in content. **CONVERGED**; confidence **medium** — ⚠ **no work of Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa's has been read**, in original or translation, and the corpus is reporting a philosopher's importance on a lexicographer's say-so.
