## Gloss

*Jina* (Sanskrit root *ji*, "to conquer"; suffix *-na* → "one who has conquered") denotes a being who has conquered the four inner enemies — the kaṣāyas (anger, pride, deceit, greed) — and thereby attained omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*) and liberation (*mokṣa*). The tradition named after the jinas is *Jainism* (followers of the Jina). The jina is not a god, creator, or intercessor — a liberated soul beyond karma-bondage has no power to intervene in the world.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Uttaradhyāyana Sūtra 9.34 (cited via multiple Jain sources):** "Victory over thousands of external enemies in the battlefield is insignificant compared to the victory over one's inner enemies; vanquishing one's passions is an unparalleled conquest." This verse anchors the meaning of *jina* as inner conqueror, not military victor.

**Signal 2 — WisdomLib etymological dictionary (traditional sources):** Confirms *ji* = "to conquer or excel" as root; Jina = victorious. Notes the term is used across both Jainism and Buddhism (in Buddhist usage, "Jina" occasionally refers to the Buddha — cross-tradition overlap without identity).

**Signal 3 — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Jaina Philosophy" (Ganeri, academic):** Treats jina and tīrthaṅkara as used synonymously in the tradition's self-description, while acknowledging the technical sub-distinction. At every cosmic half-cycle, 24 omniscient jinas are also called tīrthaṅkaras.

**Signal 4 — Jainworld, "Aspects of Jain Religion" (Jain institutional):** Explicitly distinguishes sāmānya-kevali from tīrthaṅkara-kevali: both are jinas, but only the tīrthaṅkara founds the four-fold community (*caturvidha saṃgha*).

**Independence:** Signals 1–4 are independent (canonical text, lexicon, academic encyclopedia, Jain institutional). All agree on the core meaning. Confidence medium because TS does not define "jina" as a technical term in the way it defines dravyas or tattvās — the concept is embedded throughout rather than concentrated in one sūtra.

## Jina-tirthankara-siddha taxonomy

| Term | Technical meaning | Number | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jina** | Conqueror of inner passions; omniscient | Many (indefinitely many over cosmic time) | Any being who achieves liberation |
| **Tīrthaṅkara** | A jina who founds the teaching-ford (*tirtha*) | 24 per cosmic half-cycle | Specific sub-type with teaching mission |
| **Arihant** | Embodied jina: 4 ghāti karmas destroyed, body remains | As many as jinas | Stage before siddhahood |
| **Siddha** | Liberated soul at universe-summit: all 8 karmas shed | Countless (accumulated over all cycles) | Final state |

The conceptual chain: kaṣāya → bandha → gunasthāna stages 1–13 → at stage 13/14, all 4 ghāti karmas destroyed → arihant / kevala-jñāna → body sheds, 4 aghāti karmas gone → siddha.

## The five supreme beings (pañca-parameṣṭhī)

The five revered categories in Jain devotion: Arihant, Siddha, Ācārya, Upādhyāya, Sādhu. The Namaskar Mantra (*Namo Arihantāṇam, Namo Siddhāṇam…*) venerates these five, not gods. Jinas fall in the first two. The mantra does not address any being that can respond or grant wishes — it is aspiration toward a state, not petition to a deity.

## Not a creator; not a savior

A liberated jina has no awareness of the particular world of souls still in bondage (their consciousness is infinite but non-particular). This is a sharp contrast to Vedantic brahman (in which the divine is immanent) and to theistic traditions where God intervenes. Jain worship (*pūjā*) of the jina images is consistently interpreted as inspirational, not intercessory. This is a substantive philosophical commitment, not a peripheral detail.

## Cross-tradition name overlap

The term *jina* appears in Buddhist texts occasionally as an epithet for the Buddha ("Jina/Victor" as one of many Buddhistic titles). This is shared vocabulary without identity — the Buddhist usage carries no implication of Jain ontology (no karma-as-matter, no ṣaḍ-dravya cosmology). Filed as `shares-vocabulary-with`, not equivalence.
