## Gloss

**Mettā** (Pāli; Sanskrit **maitrī**) is **loving-kindness** — boundless, impartial goodwill toward all beings. Etymologically it derives from *mitra* ("friend"): mettā is universalised friendliness, the wish *"may all beings be happy."* It is the **first of the four *brahmavihāra*** ("divine abidings") and the first of the four ***appamaññā*** ("immeasurables") — so called because they are radiated without limit or partiality. In Theravāda it is also the first of the ten *pāramī* (perfections).

**Canonical anchor.** The *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* (Sn 1.8) gives the defining simile: *"Just as a mother would protect her only child at the risk of her own life, even so let one cultivate a boundless heart toward all beings."* The practice — ***mettā-bhāvanā*** — repeats well-wishing phrases ("may you be happy / free from suffering") radiated in widening circles: oneself → loved ones → neutral persons → difficult persons → all beings in all directions.

**Near and far enemy** (the *Visuddhimagga* diagnostic that defines each brahmavihāra by what it is *not*):
- **Far enemy:** ill-will / hatred (*vyāpāda*, *dosa*) — its outright opposite.
- **Near enemy:** selfish or preferential affection (*rāga*, greed) — the *counterfeit* that resembles mettā but is partial and possessive.

This near-enemy structure is the crucial point: mettā is explicitly **not** romantic love, attachment, or fondness for those who please us. *"Love, without desire to possess… this is the highest love"* (Nyanaponika). Affection that clings is the near enemy precisely because it masquerades as the virtue.

**Cross-tradition note (parallel, not identity).** The *Yoga Sūtra* of Patañjali (1.33) lists the same four attitudes — *maitrī, karuṇā, muditā, upekṣā* — and Jainism has a structurally similar set of four reflections (*maitrī, pramoda, kāruṇya, mādhyasthya*). These are genuine parallels in vocabulary and structure, but each is embedded in a different soteriology and is not the same doctrine.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Wikipedia, "Maitrī" / "Brahmavihara":** etymology from *mitra*; the *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* (Sn 1.8 / Khp 9) with the mother-and-only-child simile; mettā as first brahmavihāra / first immeasurable (*appamaññā*) and first Theravāda *pāramī*; *mettā-bhāvanā* radiating practice; far enemy *vyāpāda/dosa* and near enemy *rāga*; the *Tevijja Sutta* and *Visuddhimagga* (Buddhaghosa, 5th c.) as canonical/commentarial sources; the Yoga-Sūtra 1.33 parallel.

**Signal 2 — Nyanaponika Thera, "The Four Sublime States" (Access to Insight):** independently defines mettā as boundless impartial love "without desire to possess," names its near enemy (selfish/preferential affection) and far enemy (hatred/aversion), and places it as the first of the four immeasurables radiated to all beings.

**Independence:** an encyclopedia article and a Theravāda monastic essay converge on the definition, the canonical source, the radiating practice, and the near/far-enemy pair. Confidence medium: the *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* Pāli and the *Visuddhimagga* were not directly fetched; the simile and near/far enemies are quoted from secondary translation/exposition.
