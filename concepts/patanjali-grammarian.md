## Gloss

**Patañjali the grammarian** — the ***bhāṣyakāra*** — is the author of the [[mahabhashya|Mahābhāṣya]], the "Great Commentary" on [[panini|Pāṇini]]'s sūtras and [[katyayana|Kātyāyana]]'s Vārttikas, and the third and (by Kaiyaṭa's maxim) the **highest** member of the *munitraya* ([[vyakarana]]).

⚠ **This node exists because [[patanjali]] — the Yoga-sūtra compiler — recorded a debt it could not pay:** *"No node exists for the grammarian; the caution is recorded here in prose since a typed edge needs a target."* The target now exists, and the two are linked by an explicit `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edge. **They are different men.**

### ⚠ The best-dated figure in this batch — and he is the commentator

**Three genuinely independent sources agree**, which is rare enough in this corpus to state plainly:

| source | date | basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Abhyankar** (read at first hand) | "Patañjali, the exponent of Kātyāyana who lived in the **second century B.C.**" | Specialist lexicon, Pāṇinian tradition |
| **SEP**, "Language and Testimony…" | Kātyāyana and Patañjali, **200–100 BCE** | Scholarly reference work |
| **Wikipedia**, "Patañjali" | "**mid 2nd century BCE** by both Western and Indian scholars" | General reference, reporting a consensus that crosses the Western/Indian divide |

**The evidence is external and datable**: references to the Maurya empire and to contemporary historical events *used as grammatical examples*. Patañjali illustrates tense and aspect with things happening around him, and so timestamps his own book.

⚠ **Note the inversion, because it is the node's real finding.** [[panini]]'s date spans roughly 350 years and remains open. **Patañjali's is fixed to within about fifty.** The *commentator is dated far more precisely than the author he comments on* — and for a mundane reason: Patañjali wrote discursively, in prose, about a world he could not help mentioning, while Pāṇini wrote rules so compressed they name almost nothing outside the language. **Precision of transmission and recoverability of date are unrelated, and can run in opposite directions.** The corpus has repeatedly found dates propagating *forward* from a fixed anchor; here the anchor is downstream of what it would most like to date.

### T1: is this the Patañjali of the Yoga Sūtra? — tested, and no

The batch queued this as a named test rather than inheriting [[patanjali]]'s verdict. It **holds**, and the corpus records **both** sides, per the standing rule that a corpus which only ever reports one side of a dispute is not neutral.

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| **Same man** | **J. H. Woods (1914)**; **Surendranath Dasgupta (1922)**, who "presented a series of arguments to **tentatively** propose that the famed Grammar text and the Yoga text author may be identical" | Not recoverable from the sources consulted — ⚠ the arguments themselves were **not** obtained, only the positions. Recorded as unexamined |
| **Different men** | **Louis Renou** — "significant differences in **language, grammar and vocabulary**" | A stylometric argument across the two texts |
| **Different men** | The **historical silence**: "**Before the time of Bhoja (11th century), no known text conflates the identity of the two authors**" | Nothing asserts the identity for ~1200 years after the grammarian |
| **Different men** — consensus | "The view that these were likely different authors is **now generally accepted** by Western scholars" | Reported consensus |

⚠ **The silence argument is the strong one**, and it is the kind this corpus trusts most: not a judgement about style, but a **datable first appearance** of the claim itself. The identification is not an old tradition that modern scholarship doubts — it is a **medieval innovation**, traceable to **Bhoja's *Rājamārtaṇḍa*, 11th century**. Everything before that treats them as two people, because nobody had yet thought to merge them.

⚠ **A correction to the corpus.** [[patanjali]] dates the *Rājamārtaṇḍa* to "**~10th c.**" while citing the same Wikipedia entry that says "**11th century**." A one-century slip in an existing file, corrected there.

⚠ **And a trap avoided.** **Philipp Maas** is cited in the literature for the thesis that "the same person named Patañjali composed the *sūtras* and the *Bhāṣya* commentary." **This is not about the grammarian.** It is the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* unity thesis — that the Yoga Sūtra and the Yoga-Bhāṣya are one work by one author, *within* the Yoga tradition. Read carelessly it looks like support for a single Patañjali and it is nothing of the kind. **Flagged because it is exactly the shape of error §0 exists to prevent: a claim about identity in one literature quietly borrowed as evidence in another.**

### The conflation is three-way, and it has a doctrinal motor

Not two attributions but **three**: the Mahābhāṣya, the Yoga Sūtra, and **medical works** — a *Patañjalatantra* and "possibly a commentary on the *[[charaka|Charaka Samhita]]*" (a *Carakavārttika*). The tradition's Patañjali is grammarian, yogin **and** physician.

⚠ **This is not a simple name-collision, and that distinction matters.** Patañjali "is regarded as an **avatar of Ādi Śeṣa**," the cosmic serpent. **Given that premise, a single author of grammar, yoga and medicine is not a confusion but a consequence** — an incarnate divinity may reasonably be expected to have perfected several sciences. The merge has a *theological* motive, which is why it is stable, why it is "an oft-repeated **article of faith**" in traditional circles, and why arguing against it with stylometry misses what it is doing. **The corpus records the mechanism as well as the verdict**: this is the same three-fold pattern by which [[dhanvantari]] anchors Āyurveda and [[kapila]] anchors Sāṃkhya — authority conferred by identity rather than argued from evidence.

⚠ **Do not confuse with the Śeṣa *family* of grammarians** (Kṛṣṇaśeṣa, Rāmacandra Śeṣa, Vitthala Śeṣa), a real 16th–17th-century lineage in Abhyankar's dictionary. Same word, no relation.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand**): Patañjali in the 2nd c. BCE, and as "the **exponent of** Kātyāyana" — the relation stated correctly, commentator to vārttikakāra rather than directly to Pāṇini. Also the Śeṣa-family grammarians, kept distinct.
2. **Wikipedia, "Patañjali"** (fetched, independent): mid-2nd c. BCE "by both Western and Indian scholars"; the three attributed bodies of work; Woods 1914 and Dasgupta 1922 for identity; Renou against; the pre-Bhoja silence with the 11th-century date; the Ādi Śeṣa avatāra; the "article of faith" characterisation.
3. **SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy"** (fetched, independent): 200–100 BCE; and the substantive point that Patañjali with Kātyāyana effected "a significant reorganization of Brahmanical views in the face of opposition from Jains and Buddhists."
4. **Corpus-internal**: [[patanjali]] independently reached the same verdict on the identity question via Renou and Bhoja; this node **re-derived it rather than inheriting it**, and found the century wrong.
5. **Provenance weighting**: the dating is triply attested and externally anchored. The identity verdict is a **reported consensus** plus one **datable silence** — the silence being checkable, Renou's stylometry not (his study was not obtained).

**CONVERGED.** Confidence **medium**, not high, and the reason is specific: the *dating* would justify high, but the *identity* verdict rests on scholarship reported rather than read — Renou's argument, and Woods's and Dasgupta's contrary arguments, were all unavailable. **The corpus knows the positions and not the reasoning**, and will not call that high.
