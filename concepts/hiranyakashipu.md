## Gloss / Divergence map

**Hiraṇyakaśipu** is the Daitya king of *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* Canto 7 — brother of Hiraṇyākṣa (slain by Varāha), father of [[prahlada|Prahlāda]], and the being whose boon obliges Viṣṇu to appear as [[narasimha|Narasiṃha]]. He is the corpus's clearest specimen of a narrative built entirely around **the failure of an enumeration**.

### The boon, in its own words

He wins it by *[[tapas]]* and states its terms himself. In G. V. Tagare's translation (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 7.3.35–38):

> **7.3.35** — "Let me not meet death at the hands of any being **created by You**"
>
> **7.3.36–37** — "Let me not meet death **indoors or out of doors**; during **night or at day** time, by the hand of any other (being created by you) **or by means of weapons**; let there be no death to me either **on the earth or in the sky**, either **by men or beasts**; by **gods, demons and big serpents**; by beings whether **living or dead**. Grant me matchlessness in fight and the sole absolute rulership of all embodied beings."

Two things are worth noticing before any of the picturesque loopholes. First, **he never asks for immortality.** In Tagare's rendering of 35–38 there is no request for deathlessness *simpliciter* and no recorded refusal of one — the boon is framed as an enumeration from its first clause. Second, the request is not only defensive: he asks in the same breath for **absolute rulership over all embodied beings** and, at 7.3.38, for Brahmā's own station among the world-guardians, with "undiminishing glory and unfailing mystical powers." The fence is one half of a bid for the boon-giver's job.

### The finding: which clause actually fails

Retellings, and Ch 33 §5.3, concentrate on the theatrical evasions — Narasiṃha is **part man and part lion**, strikes at **dusk**, on the **threshold**, on his own **lap**, with **claws**. Each of those defeats one clause by occupying a boundary the clause assumed away, and together they make the point Ch 33 names: *the boon is defeated not by superior force but by exhaustive disjunction failing to be exhaustive.*

But laid against the text, the first clause is the one that fails first. Everything Hiraṇyakaśipu fences out is fenced out **"by any being created by You"** — by Brahmā. And in the same Purāṇa's cosmology Brahmā is not Viṣṇu's creator but his creature, born from the lotus at his navel. **Viṣṇu is not in the domain the boon quantifies over at all.** On this reading the man-lion, the twilight and the threshold are not what makes the killing possible; they are a *surplus* — the narrative satisfying, one by one and visibly, clauses that were already void.

The corpus flags this as **its own reading of the quoted verses, not a claim it found in a source**: none of the material fetched draws the distinction, and it is recorded because it changes what the story is about. On the popular reading the moral is *be careful how you enumerate*. On the textual reading it is *you cannot fence out what your fence-maker is smaller than* — a claim about the limits of a granted power rather than about the limits of a list. Both are available in the text; the corpus does not choose, and does not pretend the second is attested where it is not.

### The cursed antagonist, and a tension the tradition leaves standing

Hiraṇyakaśipu and Hiraṇyākṣa are, in the *Bhāgavata*'s own frame, **Jaya and Vijaya** — Viṣṇu's gatekeepers at Vaikuṇṭha, cursed by the Four Kumāras (Brahmā's sons) to be born in the world as demons, and discharging the curse over three lives: Hiraṇyakaśipu/Hiraṇyākṣa slain by Narasiṃha and Varāha, Rāvaṇa/Kumbhakarṇa by Rāma, Śiśupāla/Dantavakra by Kṛṣṇa, after which they return.

This sits awkwardly with the moral surface of Canto 7, and the corpus records the awkwardness rather than resolving it. Read morally, Hiraṇyakaśipu is a tyrant destroyed for persecuting his devotee son. Read structurally, **his enmity is assigned, not chosen** — a role three times required so that Viṣṇu has an adversary to descend against, and its holders are among his own intimates. The same family carries the tradition's model devotee ([[prahlada|Prahlāda]]) and, a generation on, its model righteous king (Bali). What the Hiraṇya cycle demonstrates is less that demons are wicked than that in this literature **the *asura* category has stopped doing moral work and become a narrative position** — which is the finding Ch 33 develops across four traditions, here visible inside a single family tree.

### On the logical resonance, stated exactly

The boon's structure invites comparison with Indian logic's interest in whether alternatives genuinely exclude, and the comparison is worth drawing — with its limits stated, because this is precisely where a §0 failure would occur.

| | what happens | |
|---|---|---|
| **the boon** | five or six pairs are asserted to partition the possibilities; the killer is found **between** the members of each pair | an **enumeration** failure |
| ***[[catuskoti]]*** | a scheme that deliberately keeps open the *both* and *neither* corners that a two-valued division suppresses | a **logical** position, held on purpose |
| ***[[many-valued-logic]]*** | bivalence is **denied**: propositions may take values other than true and false | a **formal semantics** |

The Purāṇa is not doing logic. Its author is not denying bivalence and is not defending a third truth-value: he is telling a story in which a list of alternatives turned out not to cover the cases, which is something that can happen in a perfectly two-valued world to anyone who writes a sloppy contract. **No influence is asserted in either direction**, and the edges are typed to say so. What the comparison earns is narrower and still real: the *narrative* dramatises a hazard the *logicians* theorised, and the two traditions were available to the same readers.
