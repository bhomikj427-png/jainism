## Gloss / Divergence map

**Raseśvara** — "lord of *rasa*," where *rasa* is **mercury** — is the alchemical school that appears as **chapter IX of the *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha***, the 14th-century doxography traditionally attributed to Mādhava/[[vidyaranya|Vidyāraṇya]]. Its position is short and hard: liberation requires a body that does not die, mercury is what makes a body not die, and therefore the pursuit of mercury is not a craft attached to a religion but the **operative part of a soteriology**.

### The argument, taken on its own terms

The chapter opens not with metallurgy but with an **objection to the other six systems**, and the objection is the whole point:

> "Liberation is declared in the six systems to follow the death of the body… Therefore a man should preserve that body by means of mercury and of medicaments."

Read as an argument, this is a charge of *deferral*: the orthodox darśanas locate release after death, which puts it beyond anyone's reach while they are alive and beyond anyone's report afterwards. The Raseśvaras accept the premise that release must be **had, not awaited**, and then notice what the premise costs — if liberation is to be enjoyed, there must be someone left to enjoy it, and so the body becomes a *soteriological requirement* rather than an obstacle. Everything alchemical follows from that inversion. The etymology is made to carry it: mercury is ***pārada*** "because it is a means of conveyance **beyond** (*pāra*) the series of transmigratory states," and — in the *Rasārṇava*'s alternative — "because it is employed for the highest end by the best votaries."

The technical apparatus is correspondingly specific, and worth recording as technique rather than as symbolism. Chapter IX enumerates **eighteen operations** on quicksilver — "sweating, rubbing, swooning, fixing, dropping, coercion, restraining, kindling, going, falling into globules, pulverising, covering, internal flux, external flux, burning, colouring, pouring, and eating it" — and **three states**: *swooning* (volatile), *dead* (heavy and immobile), and *bound* (fluent, luminous, heavy). Only after that processing does mercury enter the body. The theology is stated in the same register: mercury and mica are the seed of **Hara** and **Gaurī** — "Mica is thy seed, and mercury is my seed; the combination of the two… is destructive of death and poverty" — and "Śiva is god. Mercury is Śiva's own self."

### The finding: a 14th-century category built from 10th–12th-century texts, dated to the 1st century by an 1882 translation

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| the school arose "**about the commencement of the Christian era**" | **Cowell & Gough** (1882), in the notes to their *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* translation — **and Wikipedia, which repeats it** | a school roughly 1,000 years older than its own root texts, and a *Rasahṛdaya* whose author would predate his book by nine centuries |
| the root texts are **10th–12th century** | **Sulekha Biswas** (1990): *Rasahṛdaya* by "Govindabhagavat," **11th c.**, with "P. C. Ray thinks it could belong to 9th–10th Century"; *Rasārṇava* **12th c.**; *Rasendracūḍāmaṇi* (Somadeva) 12th c.; *Rasaratnasamuccaya* 14th c. Independently, **David Gordon White**'s *The Alchemical Body* treats the *Rasārṇava* as an **11th-century** text drawing on the **10th-century** *Rasahṛdayatantra* of Govinda | that "Raseśvara" names a body of literature of the Indian medieval period, and that its appearance as a *darśana* is Mādhava's editorial act |

The two readings are not in a scholarly dialogue — the second simply dates the texts, and the first dates the school without them. **Wikipedia's date is not an independent vote**; it attributes the claim to Cowell and Gough, so what looks like two sources is one 1882 source and its echo. §4 signal 1 exactly.

The consequence is worth stating plainly, because it changes what kind of object this node is. There is a real and continuous **rasaśāstra** literature. Whether there was ever a *school* that called itself Raseśvara and understood itself as a rival darśana is a different question, and the evidence for it is essentially **one chapter in a doxography** — a genre whose business is to arrange rivals in order. The corpus therefore treats "Raseśvara **darśana**" as at least partly a **classificatory artefact of the 14th century**, and "raseśvara *literature*" as the thing that certainly existed. This does not make Mādhava wrong; doxographies name real things. It means the school's *unity* is attested by the same source that supplies its doctrine.

### The Govinda knot, tightened

[[govinda-bhagavatpada]] records — at `confidence: low` — that the *Rasahṛdaya-tantra* is attributed to a Govinda Bhagavatpāda, that this is used to give [[shankara|Śaṅkara]]'s guru an alchemical second career, and that the attribution "fights the dates." This node supplies the missing dates, and they **close the identification from both sides**:

- Cowell & Gough's 1st-century school is **too early** for an 8th-century guru.
- Biswas's and White's 10th–12th-century *Rasahṛdaya* is **too late** for him — 9th c. at the very earliest end of P. C. Ray's range, and even that is a century after Śaṅkara.

Neither dating leaves room for Śaṅkara's Govinda. The honest conclusion is the boring one the corpus prefers: **"Govinda Bhagavatpāda" is a name two different men bore**, and the alchemical one is the later. The follow-up carried since Batch 42 can be marked *advanced* on this basis, not closed — no source fetched here argues the identification either way; the dates simply do not permit it.

**And the same pattern twice more.** The *Rasaratnākara* is attributed to **[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]]** (dated "200 A.D." with the compilation queried as 7th–8th c.), and the 14th-century *Rasaratnasamuccaya* to **"Vāgbhaṭācārya"** — where [[vagbhata]] already records a homonym problem so live that the *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha*'s own colophon is a grandson explaining it. Three of the corpus's Sanskrit-tradition names — Govinda, Nāgārjuna, Vāgbhaṭa — have alchemical namesakes. **Rasaśāstra attracts prestigious authorship**, which is what one expects of a literature that is simultaneously technical, disreputable and enormously promising, and it is a standing reason to check dates before merging persons.

### On mercury, without praise and without debunking

Mercury and its compounds are toxic; chronic exposure causes hydrargyria. Wikipedia's article states this and adds that it "contradict[s] the tradition's immortality claims." The fact is not in dispute and the corpus records it. The framing is doing more work than the fact, and §0 requires the same care in the debunking direction as in the inflating one: the Raseśvara texts do not prescribe swallowing quicksilver, they prescribe **eighteen operations** first and distinguish *bound* from *dead* from *swooning* mercury — so a rebuttal aimed at raw mercury is not addressed to the claim as the texts make it. **This is not a defence of the claim.** The corpus takes no position on whether any preparation was safe or efficacious, notes that none of the sources fetched analyses the products chemically, and records only that the argument and its refutation are about different objects. What can be said without any of that: **the school made a falsifiable-in-principle promise** — an imperishable body — of a kind the six darśanas were careful never to make, and that is the most interesting thing about it philosophically.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Mādhava, *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* ch. IX**, in the Cowell & Gough translation (1882) — the **primary source** for the school's doctrine, and effectively the only one: the anti-six-darśanas argument, the *pārada* etymologies, the eighteen operations, the three states, the Hara/Gaurī seed verses, and the named authorities (Govinda-bhagavat, Sarvajña-rāmeśvara, and Parameśvara/Parameśvarī in dialogue). Read via wisdomlib's reproduction of that translation.
2. **Wikipedia, "Raseśvara"** — the three root texts (*Rasārṇava*, *Rasahṛdaya*, *Raseśvarasiddhānta*); "liberation could only be achieved by using mercury to acquire an imperishable body"; and the Cowell-and-Gough date, **explicitly attributed to them**, which is what identifies it as a derivative rather than a second witness.
3. **Sulekha Biswas, "Minerals and Metals in Sanskrit Literature" (1990)** — the dated table of rasaśāstra texts, including P. C. Ray's alternative for the *Rasahṛdaya* and the *Rasaratnasamuccaya*/Vāgbhaṭācārya and *Rasaratnākara*/Nāgārjuna attributions. An independent literature (history of technology, not philosophy).
4. **David Gordon White, *The Alchemical Body: Siddha Traditions in Medieval India*** (Univ. of Chicago Press) — the standard scholarly treatment; **located, not read**, its datings (*Rasārṇava* 11th c., *Rasahṛdayatantra* of Govinda 10th c., *Rasendracūḍāmaṇi* of Somadeva 12th c.) taken from secondary report. Independent of Biswas and agreeing with her within a century.
5. **Swami Harshananda, *A Concise Encyclopaedia of Hinduism*, "Raseśvara Darśana"** — **paywalled, full entry not obtained**; a fragment quoted elsewhere on the same site supplies "The summum bonum is the attainment of the state of the divinity… if it is tranquilized by mercury. Śiva is god. Mercury is Śiva's own self."
6. **Corpus cross-check** — [[govinda-bhagavatpada]] (the attribution and its date-fight, recorded at low confidence in Batch 42), [[vagbhata]] (the homonym problem), [[nagarjuna]], [[jivanmukti]], [[siddha]]: four existing nodes that this one either sharpens or is sharpened by.

**CONTESTED** — decisively on date and on whether "Raseśvara" names a school or a doxographer's category. **Converged** on doctrine: there is only one substantial source for it, so agreement is trivial and is *not* treated as confirmation. Confidence **medium**, and its limits are specific: the doctrine rests on **a single 14th-century chapter in a single 1882 translation**, with no commentarial tradition consulted (§4 signal 2 is simply absent here); none of the three root texts was read in any edition; White's monograph, the one item that would properly ground the dating, is known only at second hand. What lifts it above `low` is that the *dating* claims come from three mutually independent literatures (Biswas, Ray via Biswas, White) that agree, and that the primary text was read in translation rather than summarised.
