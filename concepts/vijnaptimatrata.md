## Gloss / Divergence map

Vijñaptimātratā is **consciousness-only**: the claim that what is cognized is *vijñapti* (representation or mere cognition) without a mind-independent external object grounding it. Vasubandhu systematized this in two treatises: the *Viṃśatikā* (Twenty Verses, with auto-commentary) as the polemical defense against realism, and the *Triṃśikā* (Thirty Verses) as a systematic positive presentation integrating the eight-consciousness model.

**Vasubandhu's three arguments (Viṃśatikā)**

1. **Dream analogy** (*svapna-dṛṣṭānta*): Objects appearing in dreams have spatial-temporal qualities (perceived as over there, at this time) despite no external object being present. If consciousness produces spatiotemporal objects without external causes in dreams, it can do so in waking life too. Therefore the appearance of an object does not require an actual external object.

2. **Consciousness as necessary condition**: For any perceptual event, consciousness is required; without consciousness nothing is apprehended. The external object is supposed to explain *why* the perception has a particular character — but consciousness (specifically, the ripening of karmic seeds in the ālaya-vijñāna) can explain this equally well without positing an external object.

3. **Collective-karma account**: The objection is: if there are no external objects, why do different people perceive the *same* external world? Vasubandhu's reply: groups of beings share similar karmic histories, producing similar representations through collective karmic ripening — no external world required.

## Contested: idealism vs. phenomenology

| reading | who holds it | what it commits to |
|---|---|---|
| **Metaphysical idealism**: no external objects exist; all of reality is consciousness | Traditional Western reading; some Tibetan scholastics (Tsoṅkhapa's opponents) | Strong ontological thesis: material world does not exist independently of minds |
| **Phenomenological/therapeutic reading**: only consciousness is directly accessible; the argument shows we cannot prove external objects exist, not that they don't | Lusthaus (2002), *Buddhist Phenomenology*; Kellner & Taber (2014) | Epistemological humility: vijñaptimātratā is about the limits of knowledge, not a positive ontology |

The contested question is whether Vasubandhu's argument is meant as a proof that external objects *do not exist* (metaphysical idealism) or that we *cannot know* whether they do (epistemological/phenomenological). The Viṃśatikā's arguments work equally well under both readings, which is why this debate remains unresolved. This debate is the most important interpretive question about Yogācāra in contemporary scholarship.

**Vijñaptimātratā vs. Jain dravya-realism**

Jainism holds all six dravyas — including pudgala (matter) — as ultimately real, mind-independently existing entities. Vijñaptimātratā denies (or radically brackets) mind-independent material existence. The two positions are mutually exclusive: if pudgala exists mind-independently, vijñaptimātratā is false; if vijñaptimātratā is correct, the Jain ontological framework collapses (or must be reclassified as "conventional truth").

**Vijñaptimātratā vs. Advaita māyā**

Both appear to deny the ultimate reality of the material world, making them natural conflation targets. But the move is different: Advaita denies that the world is the ultimate ground (Brahman is), while still granting it *vyāvahārika* (conventional) reality. Vijñaptimātratā denies the mind-independence of representation — but the status of consciousness itself is not a single, permanent Brahman. The two doctrines converge on "the world is not what it appears to be" but for opposite reasons: Advaita because Brahman is the only *real*, Yogācāra because only *cognition* is directly accessible.
