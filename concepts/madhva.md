## Gloss / Divergence map

**Madhva** (also **Madhvācārya**, **Pūrṇaprajña**, **Ānandatīrtha**; born Vāsudeva) founded [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita Vedānta]]. As with [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]], the doctrine lives in the school-file and **converges**; this node exists for the man, and the man is **contested twice over** — on his dates, and on **whether roughly forty of the scriptural authorities he quotes ever existed.**

The second question is why this node matters beyond biography. It is a dispute about **citation practice**, adjudicated by exactly the method this repository runs on — and the adjudication came out against the modern scholar.

### Divergence 1 — the dates: four reference works, four answers

|---|---|---|
| **1238–1317** | Karl H. Potter, *Encyclopedia of Religion* (1987); B. N. K. Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School* 3rd ed. (2000); Bryant (2007) | The later bracket. Also the tradition's own: it dates the disappearance at Badarī to 1317. |
| **c. 1199–c. 1276** | *World Religions Reference Library*; B. N. K. Sharma (1962) | Roughly forty years earlier — and note that **Sharma held this in 1962 and the other in 2000**: one scholar, two published positions. |
| **c. 1197–c. 1276** | *Encyclopedia of World Biography* | A two-year variant of the above, unexplained. |
| **"dates uncertain, ranging from 1199–1278 to 1238–1317"** | John Bowker, *Concise Oxford Dictionary of World Religions* | That the honest report is the **range**, not a pick. This file follows Bowker. |

Four reference works, four answers, no argument offered by any of them. The corpus's `dvaita-vedanta.md` prints **1238–1317** flat; that is not wrong, but it is one option presented as settled, and it has been qualified.

### Divergence 2 — the unknown sources

Madhva quotes, as scriptural authority, a body of Śruti and Purāṇic texts — *Paiṅgi-śruti*, *Bhāllaveya-śruti*, *Sauparṇa-śruti*, *Vātsa-śruti*, *Brahma-Tarka*, *Parama-śruti* and others — for which **no surviving manuscript contains the quoted passage**.

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Madhva wrote them himself.** The titles are not works but "'work-titles', which Madhva employs as a loop to hang up his literary compositions"; he did it **sincerely**, being "firmly convinced that he was acting on the command of Viṣṇu" | **Roque Mesquita**, *Madhva und seine unbekannten literarischen Quellen* (De Nobili Research Library XXIV, Vienna, 1997), Eng. tr. *Madhva's Unknown Literary Sources: Some Observations* (Aditya Prakashan, 2000), pp. 175–176 | That Dvaita's scriptural base is partly **pseudepigraphic** — and, because Mesquita ties this to Madhva's Vāyu-avatāra claim (pp. 35–87), that the fraud and the self-understanding are the same act |
| **The sources are rare, not fictitious.** Mesquita's "research is monumentally incomplete"; he "is genuinely unfamiliar with the spread of the Vedic corpora," and labels Madhva the author of texts "for which we have collateral evidence other than Madhva's own word" | **Shrisha Rao & B. N. K. Sharma**, "Madhva's Unknown Sources: A Review," *Asiatische Studien* LVII/1 (2003), 181–194 | That a lost recension is a normal fact of Vedic transmission, and absence of a manuscript is not absence of a text |
| **Adjudication is premature** | this file | Both — see below |

**Rao and Sharma's method is the one that decides it**, and it is worth stating because it is the same method §4 prescribes: rather than argue about plausibility, they go looking for **independent attestation of each allegedly fictitious title in pre-Madhva literature.** For the *Paiṅgi* alone they produce eight: Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī* 4.3.105 via the *Kāśikā* (*kalpeṣu paiṅgī kalpaḥ*); Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* on 4.2.66, which shows the Kalpasūtra was available to *him*; four manuscripts of a *Paiṅgāyani Brāhmaṇa* in Oppert's 1885 catalogue of South Indian private libraries; quotation by Haradatta and Māskarī; the *Prapañcahṛdaya*, a pre-Rāmānuja text, listing Paiṅgi among Ṛgvedic śākhās; *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* 6.3.10; two citations in the *Āpastamba Śrautasūtra*; and a *Paiṅgi-śruti* quoted by **Śaṅkara himself** in his Brahmasūtra commentary. Parallel dossiers follow for *Bhāllaveya*, *Sauparṇa* and *Vātsa*.

If those attestations hold, the specific charge fails for those titles — a text quoted by Pāṇini's commentators, Patañjali and Śaṅkara is not one Madhva invented.

### The finding: a single charge that looks like three

The most transferable result here is **not** who is right. It is the **shape of the evidence**, and it is a §4 case as clean as the corpus has:

> **Appayya Dīkṣita (16th c.)** compiles a list of Madhva's suspect citations in his *Madhvatantramukhamardana*. → **Mesquita (1997)** "cites with apparent approval the list of unfamiliar sources from Madhva's BSBh. objected to by Appayyadīkṣita, and proceeds to assume without verification that all of them … must indeed be unknown" (Rao & Sharma, §2). → **Wikipedia (2020s)** reports Mesquita's conclusion as modern philological research, and separately reports Appayya's accusation as historical precedent.

Read casually, that is **a medieval polemicist and a modern indologist independently reaching the same conclusion** — apparent convergence, and strong. Read with provenance, it is **one list, propagated twice**. Rao and Sharma's sharpest objection is exactly this: Mesquita is "putting too much faith in a medieval traditionalist's claims, a rather odd thing for a modern scholar to do," and he **fails even to mention** the Mādhva replies to Appayya by **Vijayīndra Tīrtha** (*Madhvamatamukhabhūṣaṇa*, publ. 1994) and **Nārāyaṇācārya**.

Two further arguments from silence tighten it:

- **No one accused Madhva for three centuries.** The first clear accuser is Appayya, and "Appayya offers his criticisms **on his own, with no reference to previous views**." No follower of Madhva before Vijayīndra Tīrtha answered the charge — and Jayatīrtha and others "were quick to consider and explain other charges against Madhva."
- **The charge is absent from the debate where it would have been decisive.** In the *Nyāyāmṛta*–*Advaitasiddhi* exchange — "the locus classicus for the criticism and defense of Madhva" — [[vyasatirtha|Vyāsarāya]] (1460–1539) *quotes* some of the supposedly fictitious sources as authorities, and [[madhusudana-sarasvati|Madhusūdana Sarasvatī]], "who did not even refrain from name-calling," **makes no charge of unknown sources**; he argues instead over what the quoted verses mean. Rao and Sharma give the *Brahma-Tarka* and *Parama-śruti* passages and the *Advaitasiddhi*'s replies to show it.

⚠ **This file does not conclude that Mesquita is refuted.** Rao and Sharma are Dvaita scholars writing in defence, their evidence is explicitly **circumstantial** ("even in law, circumstantial evidence is acceptable when direct witnesses are not available"), they establish that *a recension of that name existed* rather than that *Madhva's quoted words are in it*, and the reply to Mesquita's *Purāṇic* and *Pāñcarātra* attributions is not made here. What is established is narrower and firmer: **the charge has one 16th-century polemical origin, not two independent ones, and the two learned Advaitins best placed to press it did not.**

### The avatāra claim, which is not incidental

Madhva holds — **in his own texts**, including a Brahmasūtra commentary, and grounding the claim on a personal encounter with Viṣṇu — that he is the **third incarnation of Vāyu**, after Hanumān and Bhīma. Mesquita makes this load-bearing (pp. 63–87): a man who believes he acts on Viṣṇu's command may compose scripture *as* transmission without experiencing it as forgery. The claim is therefore not devotional colour around the argument; on Mesquita's reading it **is** the argument's psychological premise. Recording the doctrine without it would misrepresent the dispute.

### The hagiography

The *Madhvavijaya* / *Sumadhvavijaya*, sixteen cantos, is by **Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍitācārya, son of Trivikrama Paṇḍita, a direct disciple** — a **second-generation** source, unusually close by the standards of Indian hagiography (compare [[shankara]], whose *Śaṅkaravijaya* is centuries removed, and whose dating tension `shankara.md` records). It nonetheless carries stilled ocean waves, an image retrieved from a shipwreck, walking on water, and a disappearance mid-lecture. **Proximity to the subject constrains the date of a legend, not its content.**

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Rao & Sharma 2003, *Asiatische Studien*** (peer-reviewed Swiss journal of Asian studies), **read in full**, 18 pp., with its 43 footnotes. This is the load-bearing source: it supplies Mesquita's exact thesis and page numbers, the full bibliography of both editions, the attestation dossiers, the Appayya-dependence, the Vijayīndra/Nārāyaṇācārya omission, and the *Nyāyāmṛta*–*Advaitasiddhi* argument. **Signal 2 — Karl H. Potter, "Madhva," *Encyclopedia of Religion* (1987)**, via encyclopedia.com: 1238–1317; ~37 works; the Udupi maṭhas. **Signal 3 — John Bowker, *Concise Oxford Dictionary of World Religions***, and two further reference entries on the same aggregator page, giving three *further* date-ranges — used as a **spread**, which is the finding, not as three confirmations. **Signal 4 — Wikipedia, "Madhvacharya"**: the Vāyu-avatāra claim's textual location, the *Madhvavijaya*'s authorship, the Appayya precedent, and Sharma's two published datings (1962 vs 2000).

**Independence, tested.** Signals 1 and 2 are fully independent (2003 journal article vs 1987 encyclopedia, different authors, different purposes). Signal 3's four entries share an aggregator but are four separate reference works. **Signal 4 is the one that fails the test**, and its failure is the node's central content: Wikipedia's Mesquita paragraph and its Appayya paragraph are **not two witnesses**, because signal 1 shows Mesquita took his list from Appayya. Had this node been written from signals 2–4 alone, it would have reported apparent convergence and been wrong about the evidence.

⚠ **Weighting a peer-reviewed source is not deferring to it.** Rao & Sharma call Appayya "17th cent." in §2 and, nine pages later, "three centuries later" than Madhva. Only the second is right: `appayya-dikshita.md` anchors him at **c. 1520–1593** on a **dated 1580 inscription**, which is better evidence than either phrase in the review. Corrected here rather than adopted.

**Why contested, not converged.** Two of the three central biographical questions are open, and the second is open *as a live scholarly dispute in print*, with a monograph on one side and a journal article on the other. **Why medium.** Both sides are read at first hand (Mesquita through extensive verbatim quotation and page citation in the review, not through his own text). **Not high**: no work of Madhva's was read; Mesquita's monograph itself was not obtained; the Purāṇic and Pāñcarātra half of his case is not answered here; and Rao & Sharma are interested parties.
