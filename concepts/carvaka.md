## Gloss

**Cārvāka** (a.k.a. **Lokāyata**, "[philosophy] prevalent in the world / of the people"; also Bārhaspatya) is classical India's **materialist and empiricist** school — the great *nāstika* outlier that denied the soul, karma, rebirth, liberation, and God. Its doctrinal texts (the *Bṛhaspati Sūtra*, ascribed to the mythical founder Bṛhaspati) are **lost**; the school survives almost entirely through hostile summaries in its opponents' works, which makes reconstruction inherently second-hand. Mapping it completes the picture of Indian philosophy: alongside the heterodox Buddhist and Jain schools and the six āstika darśanas, Cārvāka marks the materialist pole that every other tradition defined itself against.

**Ontology — four elements, no fifth, no soul.** All that exists is the four gross elements: earth, water, fire, air (notably *excluding* ākāśa/space, which is imperceptible). There is no immaterial soul. **Consciousness is an emergent product of matter** — it arises when the elements combine in a living body, just as an intoxicating power arises from fermenting ingredients that are individually non-intoxicating, and it vanishes at death. Hence no transmigrating self, and karma "as a moral law governing future lives is therefore meaningless."

**Epistemology — perception alone (pratyakṣa).** Cārvāka admits only direct perception as a valid *pramāṇa* and rejects inference (*anumāna*) and testimony (*śabda*). The core argument is an **attack on vyāpti** (the universal invariable concomitance, e.g. "wherever smoke, there fire" that grounds all inference): such a universal cannot be established, because (a) no one can observe all instances across all time and space, and confirming the universal by inference is circular; and (b) the **upādhi** (hidden-condition) objection — apparent regularities may hide unnoticed conditioning factors, and ruling them all out would already require the universal knowledge in question. Inference is thus a "leap in the dark." Testimony fails derivatively (judging a speaker's reliability requires inference), so scripture's unperceived gods, karma, and soul are epistemically baseless.

**Ethics.** With no afterlife to optimise for, the good is identified with **pleasure in this life** — a this-worldly, broadly hedonist ethic (its opponents caricatured it as crude egoism).

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1** (IEP, "Lokāyata/Cārvāka — Indian Materialism"): names Lokāyata/Cārvāka/Bārhaspatya and the lost *Bṛhaspati Sūtra*; the four elements; consciousness as a material construct; perception as the sole reliable pramāṇa with rejection of speculative inference; denial of immaterial soul, God, afterlife; pleasure-based ethics. Stresses the school is known only through fragments and opponents. **Signal 2** (Philosophy Institute, "Cārvāka's Epistemology"): independently gives the vyāpti-impossibility argument (no observation of all instances; circularity), the **upādhi** objection, the "leap in the dark" characterisation, and the derivative collapse of testimony/scripture. The two are independent (one a scholarly encyclopedia, one a teaching essay) and converge on ontology and epistemology. Medium confidence: there is **no extant primary text** — all readings are mediated through opponents (a structural ceiling §4 flags); the hedonist "borrow money and drink ghee" verse and Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa's *Tattvopaplavasiṃha* are commonly cited in the literature but were **not** verified from a source this session, so they are omitted from the gloss.
