## Gloss

The *Ṇamokāra mantra* (also *Namaskāra*, *Navkār*, *Pañca-namaskāra*) is the foundational Jain prayer — the most universal and least sectarian formula in the tradition, recited by Digambara and Śvetāmbara alike. It is a five-fold salutation to the **pañca-parameṣṭhī** (five supreme beings), followed by two closing lines (*cūlikā*) on its merit:

> **Ṇamo Arihantāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *arihants* (omniscient, still embodied)
> **Ṇamo Siddhāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *siddhas* (fully liberated, bodiless)
> **Ṇamo Āyariyāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *ācāryas* (heads of the monastic order)
> **Ṇamo Uvajjhāyāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *upādhyāyas* (preceptors/teachers)
> **Ṇamo Loe Savva-sāhūṇaṃ** — I bow to all the *sādhus* (ascetics) in the world
>
> **Eso pañca-ṇamokkāro, savva-pāvappaṇāsaṇo / maṅgalāṇaṃ ca savvesiṃ, paḍhamaṃ havaī maṅgalaṃ**
> "This five-fold salutation destroys all sins; of all that is auspicious, it is the foremost auspicious thing."

Its decisive feature: it **names no individual and no god** — not Mahāvīra, not any tīrthaṅkara, not a creator. It bows only to *categories of qualities*. This makes it structurally aspirational, not petitionary: one does not ask the parameṣṭhī for favours (the first two are beyond all worldly agency; see arihant.md, siddha.md), one venerates the *qualities* one aims to realize in oneself.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Wikipedia "Namokar Mantra":** Gives the five lines and the *cūlikā*; confirms the mantra "contains no mention of any particular names of the gods or any specific person," only categories. Earliest attestation: short inscriptions ~200–100 BCE with only *Namo Arahatanam*; the fuller form in the Hāthīgumphā inscription (Khāravela, 2nd c. BCE–1st c. CE). Explicitly "aspirational" not petitionary.

**Signal 2 — Jainworld / Cultural Samvaad / Dadabhagwan (Jain institutional):** Independently confirm the five parameṣṭhī mapping and the closing lines ("these five bowings destroy all the sins; … the foremost auspicious mantra"); confirm "does not ask for favors" — honours qualities to cultivate the same virtues.

**Independence:** Signal 1 (encyclopedic, with epigraphic dating) and Signal 2 (Jain practitioner sources) agree on text, structure, and the aspirational reading. Confidence medium: the Prakrit *cūlikā* wording is standard but the critical-edition apparatus (e.g. the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama *maṅgalācaraṇa*) was not fetched at verse level.

## The five it salutes — and the two-tier cut

| line | being | status | venerable because |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **arihant** | liberated, body remains | omniscient and still teaching in the world |
| 2 | **siddha** | fully liberated, bodiless | all eight karmas shed; at the siddha-śilā |
| 3 | **ācārya** | non-liberated monk, order-head | models and enforces right conduct |
| 4 | **upādhyāya** | non-liberated monk, teacher | teaches the āgamas |
| 5 | **sādhu/sādhvī** | non-liberated monk/nun | observes the five mahāvratas |

The order is **soteriological, not institutional**: the two *liberated* beings (arihant, siddha) come first even though only the lower three are living and reachable. A common ordering-puzzle — *why arihant before siddha, when siddha is "higher"?* — is answered in the tradition by gratitude: the arihant, still embodied, is the one who actually *teaches* the path; without the arihant the siddha-state would be unknown.

## Not addressed to a god

The mantra is the clearest single expression of Jain non-theism in liturgy. Lines 1–2 honour beings who, by doctrine, *cannot* respond — they have no agency in the world. Lines 3–5 honour living practitioners who are themselves still on the path. There is nothing here to petition; the recitation works on the *reciter*, by orienting the mind toward the qualities (omniscience, liberation, right conduct) it salutes. (Same structural point made for each being individually in arihant.md, siddha.md, acarya.md.)
