## Gloss

Dignāga's (~480–540 CE) theory of linguistic meaning as **exclusion** (*anyāpoha* = "exclusion of the other"). A word or concept does not refer to a real shared universal (*jāti*/*sāmānya*); instead it operates by excluding what falls outside its class. "Cow" means "not-non-cow" — a double negation where the two negations are differently scoped (nominal vs. verbal), so the result is NOT logically equivalent to simple affirmation. This double-negation structure is the key technical move: it preserves reference to cow-particulars without positing any real *cowhood* that inheres in them.

**Why this eliminates real universals**: concepts are delimiters, not graspers. The concept "tree" excludes all non-trees; its range is fixed by exclusion, not by collecting a shared essence. Concepts (*kalpanā*) are thus **imposed** onto the stream of unique, unrepeatable particulars (*svalakṣaṇa*) that perception directly contacts. The general image (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa*) that appears in inference is a conceptual construction, not a real feature of the world.

**Dharmakīrti's development** (bottom-up): rather than just showing concepts-as-exclusions (Dignāga's top-down approach), Dharmakīrti grounds concepts causally — distinct particulars generate similar perceptual images through karma-conditioned cognitive apparatus; the observer judges them "identical" not because they share a universal but because the causal chain produces indistinguishable appearances.

**Not simple Western nominalism**: the theory does not treat concepts as purely arbitrary mental impositions. Particulars causally constrain which concepts are projectible: only causally unified classes of particulars generate stable exclusion-sets.
