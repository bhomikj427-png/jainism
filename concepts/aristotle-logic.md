## Gloss

Aristotle's syllogistic is the first systematic formal logic in the Western tradition. A *sullogismos* is defined in *Prior Analytics* I.2 (24b18–20) as "speech in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so." The categorical syllogism has three terms — **major** (predicate of the conclusion), **minor** (subject of the conclusion), **middle** (appears in both premises, absent from the conclusion) — arranged in two premises that yield a conclusion by necessity. The three *figures* differ by the middle term's position: subject of one premise and predicate of the other (Figure 1); predicate of both (Figure 2); subject of both (Figure 3).

Aristotle distinguishes **apodeictic** (demonstrative) syllogisms — premises are "true and primary," establishing scientific knowledge — from **dialectical** syllogisms — premises are *endoxa* (accepted opinions), used for argumentation. This parallels a distinction that Indian logicians also mark: svārthānumāna (inference for oneself) vs. parārthānumāna (inference for another).

## Comparison with Indian anumāna

| feature | Aristotle syllogism | Nyāya anumāna | Buddhist trairūpya (Dignāga) |
|---|---|---|---|
| inferential core | 3 terms (major/middle/minor) | 3 terms (sādhya/hetu/pakṣa) | 3 conditions on hetu |
| verbal form | 2 premises + conclusion | 5 members (verbal exposition only) | 3 members |
| universals | assumed as predication | vyāpti empirically grounded in instances | rejected — only particulars; apoha replaces universals |
| primary character | purely formal / deductive | empirico-deductive (vyāpti requires positive + negative instances) | formal + causal |
| middle-term function | mediates predication between major and minor | is the locus of pervasion (vyāpti) | must satisfy trairūpya (3 conditions) |
| formal figures | 3 figures; 15 valid moods | no figure-analysis | no figure-analysis |
| negative instances | not a formal requirement | vipaksha (absence in counter-examples) required | vyāpti-grounding requires negative instances |

**Critical distinction**: Aristotle's major premise ("All men are mortal") is asserted as already universal — the formal system does not say how it was established. Nyāya's vyāpti requires that the universal connection be confirmed by positive instances (sapakṣa) and excluded from negative instances (vipakṣa); the universality is earned, not stipulated. This is the deepest structural difference: Aristotle's logic is syntactic-formal; Nyāya's is epistemically grounded in pervasion.
