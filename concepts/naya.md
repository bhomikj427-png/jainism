## Gloss / Divergence map

A naya is a **valid partial-aspect cognition** — a standpoint that genuinely apprehends one mode or attribute of an object without apprehending all of them simultaneously. It is not guesswork or bias; it is a partial use of full knowledge. Two independent commentarial definitions converge:

- **Pūjyapāda** (*Sarvārthasiddhi*, Digambara, ~5th–6th c.; via Vijay K. Jain 2018): "Ascertainment, without contradiction, of one particular state or mode of the object, with a view to describe truly the substance having infinite attributes."

- **Prabhācandra** (*Prameyakamalamārtaṇḍa*, Digambara, ~10th–11th c.; via Deepa Baruah, *A Study of the Philosophy of Jainism*, 2017): "The apprehension of the knower of a part of an object without discarding its opposite views."

Both definitions share the key qualification: a naya is only valid when it does not **deny** the complementary aspects it is not currently attending to. A standpoint that claims exclusivity — that only its aspect is real and all others are false — becomes a **nayābhāsa** (distorted/false standpoint). The nayābhāsa is the failure mode; the valid naya is characterised precisely by its restraint from over-claiming.

**Pramāṇa vs. naya** (TS 1.6: *pramāṇanayaiḥ* — "by means of valid cognitions and standpoints"): pramāṇa is complete valid knowledge of an object in all its aspects; naya is valid knowledge of one aspect. A fully omniscient being (kevala-jñāna) uses pramāṇa; finite beings use nayas. (Source: Deepa Baruah 2017, citing Jain epistemological tradition.)

**The seven nayas** (TS 1.33, Vijay K. Jain 2018):

| # | Sanskrit name | orientation | brief description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | naigama | dravyārthika | teleological/intentional: attends to purpose; treats as done what is in progress |
| 2 | saṃgraha | dravyārthika | generic class view: treats all members of a class as one (all is "being") |
| 3 | vyavahāra | dravyārthika | practical particularity: differentiates within a class (this pot, not that cup) |
| 4 | ṛjusūtra | paryāyārthika | momentary: attends only to the object's present mode, discarding past/future |
| 5 | śabda | paryāyārthika | verbal: synonymous words have identical meaning regardless of derivation |
| 6 | samabhirūḍha | paryāyārthika | etymological: synonyms express different aspects via their derivational roots |
| 7 | evaṃbhūta | paryāyārthika | functional: a term applies only when the object is actively performing the denoted function |

**TS 1.33 Sanskrit:** *naigamasaṃgrahavyavahārarjusūtraśabdasamabhirūḍvaivaṃbhūtā nayāḥ* (Vijay K. Jain 2018).

The seven nayas move from most general/abstract (saṃgraha: "everything is *sat* / being") to most particular/momentary (evaṃbhūta: this word applies only right now, in this act). Each naya is progressively more restrictive; each captures something real. None alone is sufficient.

**Naya is the backbone of anekāntavāda:** the doctrine that reality is many-sided is not a vague assertion but a claim grounded in the systematic availability of these seven standpoints, each yielding genuine but partial knowledge.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Vijay K. Jain 2018 (Motilal Banarsidass):** TS 1.33 Sanskrit + translation fetched directly; Pūjyapāda's definition fetched directly. Strong critical-edition provenance.

**Signal 2 — Deepa Baruah, *A Study of the Philosophy of Jainism*, WisdomLib, 2017:** Independently provides Prabhācandra's definition (different commentator, different century, different work from Pūjyapāda). Also articulates pramāṇa/naya distinction explicitly. Academic monograph-level essay; medium-high provenance.

**Signal 3 — Search result aggregation (encyclopedia.com, herenow4u, jainworld):** Seven-naya list and the two-fold division (dravyārthika/paryāyārthika) confirmed across multiple sources. Derivative.

**Independence assessment:** Signals 1 and 2 are genuinely independent (Pūjyapāda vs. Prabhācandra). Both definitions converge on "partial-aspect without discarding the whole." This is the strongest independence achieved in this batch. Confidence would be *high* but for Tatia 1994 Chapter 1 not directly verified; keeping at medium pending that check.

**No contested readings** on the core definition found. The number "seven" is stable and universally cited; the two-fold division (dravyārthika/paryāyārthika) is stable. The order and precise descriptions of each naya vary slightly across popularising sources but the seven names are consistent.
