## Gloss / Divergence map

**Dhamma** (Pali; Sanskrit cognate: *dharma*): among the most polysemous terms in the entire Indian philosophical lexicon. The Pali and Sanskrit forms are etymologically identical (*√dhṛ*, "to hold, support") but their semantic fields diverge radically across traditions. This file treats the **Pali Buddhist** usage; the Sanskrit dharma-as-cosmic-law (Hindu), dharma-as-moral-duty (BG 2.31), and Jain dharma-dravya (medium of motion) are NOT treated here — each occupies a different semantic space.

**Four primary senses of Pali dhamma (not mutually exclusive):**

| sense | Pali term / context | gloss | primary usage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The Buddha's Teaching | *pariyatti-dhamma*; the "Dhamma" as one of the Three Jewels | the corpus of what the Buddha taught; path + doctrine + enlightenment | Nikāyas throughout; *"Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi, Dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi"* |
| 2. Natural / Cosmic Law | *niyāma-dhamma*; Dhamma as universal order | the impersonal lawfulness of conditioned existence (kamma-niyāma, citta-niyāma, etc.) | Dhammapada opening; dependent origination (paṭicca-samuppāda) as *dhamma* |
| 3. Mental Phenomena / Objects | *dhammāyatana* (the sixth sense-base); *dhamma-ārammaṇa* | any cognizable mental object — including past, future, or imaginary objects | Abhidhamma psychology; the six-sense-base (āyatana) schema |
| 4. Constituent Elements of Reality | technical Abhidhamma: *dhamma* as irreducible momentary event | the 82 (Theravāda) elementary phenomena (citta, cetasika, rūpa, nibbāna) into which "things" are analysed | Dhammasaṅgaṇī; the Abhidhamma enterprise is named after this sense |

**The decisive philosophical point:** senses 3 and 4 together underpin the **anattā** (no-self) argument. If all that appears to be "a person" resolves into momentary, conditioned *dhamma*s — none of which is permanent or self-owning — then there is no ātman, no permanent individual soul. The Abhidhamma enterprise is the systematic analysis that makes this argument precise. This is the sharpest contrast with the Jain *jīva* (a real, permanent individual soul) and the Hindu *ātman* (identical with or distinct from brahman, but permanent).

**Cross-tradition semantic collisions:**

| term | tradition | meaning | relation to Pali dhamma |
|---|---|---|---|
| dharma (Sanskrit; Hindu) | Vedic / Mīmāṃsā / BG | cosmic moral order; social duty; righteousness | same root, overlapping cosmic-law sense (§ 2), but Hindu dharma is permanent, normative, duty-centred — not a transient phenomenal element |
| dharma-dravya | Jain | the medium of motion (one of 6 dravyas) | completely different referent; ONLY the name is shared; dharma-dravya is a physical/quasi-physical substance, not teaching or mental phenomenon |
| Dharma (Sanskrit; Buddhist Mahāyāna) | Mahāyāna | = dhamma in senses 1 and 4, but with Sanskrit orthography | substantively the same; orthographic difference reflects Pali (Theravāda) vs Sanskrit (Mahāyāna) transmission |

**The Three Jewels framing.** In Theravāda practice, taking refuge in the Dhamma (*Dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi*) encompasses all four senses simultaneously: the teaching (1), its natural-law grounding (2), the objects of meditative attention (3), and the phenomenal constituents whose real nature meditation reveals (4). This multi-layered meaning is deliberate and not a confusion — the Nikāyas use context to select the appropriate sense.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wisdom Library, "Dhamma"** (citing Ñāṇatiloka's *Buddhist Dictionary* and multiple Pali commentary sources): fourfold definition (teaching / cosmic law / mental phenomena / constituent element); *dhammāyatana* as sixth sense-base; *nissatta-nijīvatā* (no being, no life) as Abhidhamma characterization. Multi-commentary source.

2. **Wikipedia, "Dharma"** (section on Buddhist usage): distinguishes Pali *dhamma* from Sanskrit *dharma*; notes that the most common Nikāya sense is "teaching of the Buddha"; Abhidhamma sense = elementary mental phenomena; three-jewel context. Independent encyclopedia source.

3. **Just-Buddha.org / Bhikkhu Bodhi** (citing the *Connected Discourses*): three levels of *dhamma* — words of the Buddha, practice, attainment. Richard Gombrich: *dhamma* is both descriptive ("the nature of what-is") and prescriptive ("what to do"). Gombrich's framing is a genuine scholarly contribution; his and Bodhi's framings independently converge on the same multi-layered structure.

Confidence medium: the four-sense taxonomy is well-established in Pali scholarship; the cross-tradition collision table is based on corpus-internal knowledge (dharma-dravya.md, abhidharma.md, atman-vedanta.md) which has been independently sourced.
