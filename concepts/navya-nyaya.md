## Gloss / Divergence map

**Navya-Nyāya**, "the new Nyāya," is the last and most technically ambitious phase of Indian scholastic philosophy: a fusion of Nyāya epistemology with Vaiśeṣika ontology, equipped with a purpose-built technical Sanskrit precise enough that its practitioners exported it to poetics, law, grammar and Vedānta as a general-purpose analytical instrument.

⚠ **This node exists because the corpus had a hole in exactly the shape of a school.** Before Batch 46, "Gaṅgeśa" or "Navya-Nyāya" appeared in **seven concept files** (`tarka`, `vyapti`, `udayana`, `sriharsa`, `anandabodha`, `vacaspati-mishra`, `madhusudana-sarasvati`) **and three chapters** — and `tarka.md` carried *Tattvacintāmaṇi (Gaṅgeśa)* in its `source_text` front-matter — with **no node anywhere**. The corpus ran the old Nyāya line (Gautama → Vātsyāyana → Praśastapāda → Vācaspati → Udayana) and then stopped at precisely the point the tradition itself calls a new beginning.

### The divergence: **when does Navya-Nyāya begin?**

This is the finding, and it is not a quibble about a date. The three answers below disagree by **two and a half centuries**, and they disagree because they are answering *different questions* — one about a book, one about a method, one about a merger.

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **With Gaṅgeśa's *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, c. 1325** — a 14th-c. school with a founding text | Wikipedia "Navya-Nyāya"/"Tattvachintamani"; the *Cubical Type Theoretic Navya-Nyāya* paper; the standard textbook line | A school dated by its **foundational book**. Everything before Gaṅgeśa is old Nyāya, and "Navya-Nyāya style" applied to an 11th-c. author is an anachronism |
| **With Udayana, c. 1025–1100** — "Pracīnanyāya ended with him. At the same time, Navyanyāya started with him" | Debabrata Deb, *The Navya-Nyāya Theory of Pakṣatā* (Wisdomlib) | A school dated by its **method**. The same source concedes Udayana's own *usages* "were still in accordance with the old style" — so this is a claim about who started doing it, not who first did it fully |
| **A merger "at some point in the 11th or 12th century"** of two previously parallel schools | **Jonardon Ganeri**, *Navya-Nyāya: Analytical Philosophy in Early Modern India* | A school dated by the **event that constituted it** — Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika ceasing to be two traditions. Gaṅgeśa's text is then "the foundational text of this school," not its origin |
| **the question is open, and the three answers are compatible** | this file | Reading 1 dates the canon, readings 2 and 3 date the formation. **They do not actually conflict on the facts** — they conflict on what "begins" means |

⚠ **Readings 2 and 3 independently converge on the eleventh century, and that matters.** Deb names Udayana; Ganeri names no one and gives "the 11th or 12th century" for the Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika merger. But Udayana **is** the merger: his *Kiraṇāvalī* is a commentary on the Vaiśeṣika [[prashastapada|Praśastapāda]] and his *Ātmatattvaviveka* is Nyāya polemic — one man working both sides, in exactly Ganeri's window. Two sources with no visible dependence, using different criteria, land on the same century.

### What this falsifies inside the corpus

`anandabodha.md` (Batch 45) **rejected** an aggregator's description of Ānandabodha's works as written "in the Navya-nyāya style," on the ground that "Navya-Nyāya begins with **Gaṅgeśa in the 14th century** — two to three centuries *after* Ānandabodha."

**That rejection assumed reading 1 was the only reading.** On readings 2 and 3, an 11th–12th-c. Advaitin writing technical dialectic is a **contemporary** of Navya-Nyāya's formation, not two centuries early. The aggregator's phrase is still loose — it was almost certainly reaching for "technical and definition-driven" rather than making a periodisation claim — but the corpus's confident *anachronism* charge does not survive. `anandabodha.md` is amended by this node. **A batch that never falsifies its own corpus is not checking it.**

Both are recorded because both are in circulation and a reader will meet both — but a dated claim with a synchronism behind it is not the same kind of object as a dated claim without one. **c. 1325 is the better-justified reading.** It is not adopted as settled because no primary dating evidence was read here.

### What was actually new

Not a new metaphysics. Ganeri is explicit that Navya-Nyāya "incorporates and develops classical Vaiśeṣika metaphysics as well as classical Nyāya epistemology." What was new is **the apparatus**:

1. **A diagnostic conception of definition.** Inherited from [[vatsyayana|Vātsyāyana]]'s three-fold procedure — *uddeśa* (enumeration), *lakṣaṇa* (definition), *parīkṣā* (examination) — and sharpened. A definition is any characteristic **co-extensive** with the class defined; it "does not tell us what the essence of the class is - it merely supplies us with a syndrome or trait." A definition fails by ***ativyāpti*** (over-covering: it catches things outside the definiendum) or ***avyāpti*** (under-covering: it misses things inside), or both. In modern terms: necessary **and** sufficient. **This is why the school reads as an endless quarrel about definitions — a definition is a falsifiable object, and examining it is a named stage of the method.**
2. **A seventh category.** To [[kanada|Kaṇāda]]'s six *padārthas* — substance, quality, motion, universal, particularity, inherence — Navya-Nyāya adds ***abhāva***, **absence**, "a distinctive addition by the later school." Absence is an *entity*, not a logical operator; this is the single change that most distinguishes the new list from the [[vaiseshika-sutra|Vaiśeṣika-sūtra]]'s.
3. **The relational-abstract notation** — the technical idiom built on *-tā*/*-tva* abstract suffixes, iterated qualification, and above all the **limitor** ([[avacchedaka|*avacchedaka*]]), which relativises property-occurrence and thereby lets the system admit partially-located properties without violating non-contradiction.
4. **Exportability.** "Navya-Nyāya techniques proved to be so versatile that they were employed, not just by philosophers, but also in poetics, linguistics, legal theory, and other domains of medieval Indian thought." The corpus already carries a downstream case: [[madhusudana-sarasvati|Madhusūdana Sarasvatī]], an **Advaitin**, trained in Navya-Nyāya at Nabadwīp before writing the *Advaitasiddhi*.

### The geography, and a correction

The school has **two homes, in sequence**: it begins in **Mithilā** (Gaṅgeśa; his son Vardhamāna; Yājñapati, Jayadeva, Rucidatta) and moves to **Navadvīpa / Nabadwīp in Bengal**, where it reaches "expressive maturity" in the 16th–17th centuries.

⚠ **A search-level aggregator asserted that "Gaṅgeśa and others who came after him belonged to Navadvīpa in Bengal."** This is **wrong on Gaṅgeśa** and is rejected: every substantive source consulted — Wikipedia, Hindupedia, Ganeri, Deb, the Wisdomlib categories study — places Gaṅgeśa in Mithilā, and Wikipedia locates him precisely, in **Karion**, the same village as Udayana, twelve miles south-east of Darbhanga. The Bengal phase begins two centuries later with **Vāsudeva Sārvabhauma** (c. 1430–1540), who taught [[raghunatha-siromani|Raghunātha Śiromaṇi]].

| Mathurānātha Tarkavāgīśa | c. 1580 | — |
| Jagadīśa Tarkālaṃkāra | c. 1590 | — |
| [[gadadhara|Gadādhara Bhaṭṭācārya]] | c. 1650 | the last great systematiser |

⚠ **Note what happens to the literature after Raghunātha**: "many Navyanaiyāyikas commented on the *Dīdhiti* rather than Jayadeva's *Āloka*." A commentary displaces a commentary as the object of commentary. And Ganeri records the endpoint of that process — by the 17th–18th centuries the idiom "made it increasingly inaccessible, and so... several manuals or compendia were written to explain in simplified language the basic tenets of the school," the most successful being Annaṃbhaṭṭa's *Tarkasaṅgraha*, **nicknamed *Bāla-gādādharī*, "a sort of 'Beginner's Guide to Gadādhara'."** A school that needs a beginner's guide to its own most recent master has priced itself out of its own tradition.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Jonardon Ganeri, *Navya-Nyāya: Analytical Philosophy in Early Modern India*** (full article PDF, Columbia/Pollock SKS papers archive). By a wide margin the strongest source consulted: a named specialist, article-length, with its own bibliography and an explicit method (he expounds the school through Annaṃbhaṭṭa's *Tarkasaṅgraha* + *Dīpikā* rather than paraphrasing an encyclopedia). Supplies the 11th–12th c. merger, the exportability claim, the *uddeśa/lakṣaṇa/parīkṣā* method with the Vātsyāyana quotation, the diagnostic theory of definition with *ativyāpti*/*avyāpti*, the seven categories with absence as the addition, the *avacchedaka* argument in full, and the *Bāla-gādādharī* nickname.

**Signal 2 — Debabrata Deb, *The Navya-Nyāya Theory of Pakṣatā* (study), Wisdomlib.** An extended academic study on a single technical term, and the **only** source consulted that gives a dated author-chain with reasons: Udayana as the turning point, Śivāditya first to define *pakṣatva*, Maṇikaṇṭha's *Nyāyaratna* as the first text with a *pakṣatā* chapter, Matilal on Śaśadhara's interval, and the Mithilā→Bengal succession with date-ranges.

**Signal 3 — Wikipedia, "Navya-Nyāya" and "Tattvachintamani" and "Gaṅgeśa"** (three articles). Encyclopedia layer: the 14th-c. dating with "recent opinion now places him in the fourteenth century," the four *khaṇḍas*, Karion and Darbhanga, Vardhamāna, the Navadvīpa name-list, Vidyābhūṣaṇa's assessment.

**Signal 4 — the aggregator layer** (Hindupedia; Wisdomlib's *Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika categories* study). Gives c. 1200 for Gaṅgeśa and a commentator list with dates (Raghunātha 1500, Mathurā 1580, Jagadīśa 1590, Gadādhara 1650).

**Signal 5 — *Cubical Type Theoretic Navya-Nyāya* (arXiv 2605.12548)**, a formal-methods paper. Used **only** for its history-of-scholarship reporting, not as a philological authority: it attributes to Ingalls the assessment that Navya-Nyāya is "the most precise calculus of properties produced before Frege" and then declines it, arguing the school is "not a *calculus* in the Hilbert/Frege sense but a *precision logic*."

**Signal 6 — corpus-internal, written batches earlier and unaware of this node**: `tarka.md` (Gaṅgeśa's *Tattvacintāmaṇi* already in its front-matter; tarka as last-resort doubt-remover), `vyapti.md` (the formal Navya-Nyāya definition of pervasion; *sāmānya-lakṣaṇa*), `udayana.md` (Gaṅgeśa calls Udayana simply *Ācāryaḥ*), `sriharsa.md` (SEP's list of post-Gaṅgeśa Naiyāyikas who answered the *Khaṇḍana*), `madhusudana-sarasvati.md` (Nabadwīp training under Harirāma and Mathurānātha).

**Independence.** Signals 1 and 2 are independent in authorship, genre and conclusion, and they **disagree with signal 3 in the same direction** while disagreeing with each other about the criterion. Signal 4 shares an Indian-textbook substrate with parts of signal 3 and is the weakest; where it conflicts with 1 or 2 it loses. Signal 6 is the strongest check available, because those files were written without any Navya-Nyāya node to be consistent with — and `tarka.md`'s front-matter turns out to have been quietly citing a school the graph did not contain.

⚠ **One quotation held at arm's length.** The Ingalls "most precise calculus of properties produced before Frege" line reaches this file **through a paper that quotes it in order to disagree with it**, and Ingalls's *Materials for the Study of Navya-Nyāya Logic* (Harvard Oriental Series) was **not read**. Recorded as a reported assessment with its reporter's dissent attached, not as Ingalls's verified words.

**Why contested.** The school's start date splits three ways across two and a half centuries; its founder's date splits by 125 years; and one of the readings falsifies a claim already committed to this corpus. **Why medium.** The divergence is mapped with each position attributed and its criterion named, and the strongest source is a named specialist read at length. **Not high**: **no Sanskrit text of the school was read** — not the *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, not the *Dīdhiti*, not the *Tarkasaṅgraha*; Ganeri's account is itself mediated through a 17th-c. manual by his own declared method; Ingalls and Matilal are reached only at second hand; and the front-matter Devanāgarī is taken from Wikipedia's article title, not from an edition.
