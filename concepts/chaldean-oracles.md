## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Chaldean Oracles** are a body of **~227 fragmentary hexameter verses**, composed in the **2nd century CE** and traditionally attributed to **Julian the Chaldean** (a Chaldean priest) and/or his son **Julian the Theurgist** — likely originally a single mystery-poem, though no connected original survives. The Oracles describe a **hierarchical cosmos** descending from a transcendent **Father/First God**, whose Power generates **Intellect**, through further emanations including a mediating goddess (**Hecate**) down to the material realm — from which the embodied soul must **ascend** back toward the divine through **ritual practice**, not contemplation alone.

**The origin of theurgy itself.** The Oracles are the text in which the word ***theourgia*** ("god-work") is **first coined and used**. This matters directly for the school's later internal split recorded in [[iamblichus|Iamblichus]]'s file: Iamblichus's insistence that ritual theurgy, not contemplation alone, is necessary for the soul's ascent is a **direct application of Chaldean doctrine** against the purely contemplative route of [[plotinus|Plotinus]] and [[porphyry|Porphyry]].

**Reception: "the Bible of the Neoplatonists."** Despite being pagan verse of obscure authorship, the Oracles acquired **quasi-scriptural authority** among later Neoplatonists. [[proclus|Proclus]] cites them extensively in his *Platonic Theology*, integrating them directly into his own metaphysical system; **Iamblichus wrote a now-lost thirty-volume commentary** on them; and **Damascius** discusses their origins and philosophical implications at length. The scholarly epithet "the Bible of the Neoplatonists" reflects exactly this status — a revealed text treated as authoritative in a way Plato's own dialogues, for all their reverence, were not.

**A provenance problem worth naming, not smoothing over.** No fragment of the Oracles survives independently of a later Neoplatonist quoting it — chiefly **Proclus, Damascius, and (via a different transmission line) Michael Psellos**. This means the surviving text is filtered entirely through the interpretive interests of the very school being asked to treat it as authoritative: what we call "the Chaldean Oracles" today is inseparable from how Proclus and Damascius chose to read and preserve it. This is flagged as a genuine independence problem for CLAUDE.md §4 signal 1 — the ancient evidentiary base itself is not independent of its interpreters, even where modern secondary scholarship describing it is.
