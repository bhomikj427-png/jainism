## Gloss

The ***Mahābhāṣya*** — "the Great Commentary" — is [[patanjali-grammarian|Patañjali]]'s discussion of [[panini|Pāṇini]]'s sūtras **and** [[katyayana|Kātyāyana]]'s Vārttikas, written in the **mid-2nd century BCE**. It is divided into **eighty-five sections called *āhnika*** ("day's work"), and it is the text through which the whole Pāṇinian tradition is read.

⚠ **Its double object matters.** It is not a commentary on the Aṣṭādhyāyī simply; Abhyankar's phrase is that Patañjali is "**the exponent of Kātyāyana**." The Mahābhāṣya argues *with* the Vārttikas as much as it explains the sūtras — which is why [[katyayana|Kātyāyana]] survives at all, and why he survives **only** here.

### ⚠ The Great Commentary comments on less than a third of its text

It "covers **1228 of the 3981 sūtras** of the Aṣṭādhyāyī" — roughly **31%**. The most authoritative work in the tradition, the one whose author (by Kaiyaṭa's maxim, [[vyakarana]]) **outranks Pāṇini himself**, leaves more than two-thirds of Pāṇini undiscussed.

This has a consequence the corpus should state rather than leave implicit: **the tradition's highest authority is silent on most of its own root text.** Where the Mahābhāṣya does not speak, later grammarians are on their own — and the vast secondary literature (Abhyankar counts the Pāṇinian works at "well nigh… eight hundred" by "four hundred" authors) is partly an attempt to cover the gap. **Selectivity, not comprehensiveness, is the genre.**

### What is actually in it: the disputes it preserves

The Mahābhāṣya is the corpus's **earliest witness to Indian semantic theory**, and much of what it preserves is *other people's disagreements*.

**1. Does a word denote the individual or the universal?** Patañjali records the dispute between **Vyāḍi** — words denote "individual instances" — and **Vājapyāyana** — words denote "generic properties or class properties (*ākṛti*), such as cowness." **Patañjali's own verdict is inclusive:** "both the individual instances and the class property must be included within the range of meaning."

⚠ **This is the ancestor of a debate the corpus already holds.** The later positions line up directly on it: Mīmāṃsā takes words to denote the class property alone (*[[jati|jāti]]*); the *Kevalavyaktivāda* school takes them to denote only individuals; **Nyāya** takes them to denote "an individual object **qualified by** a generic property (*jāti-viśiṣṭa-vyakti*)" — which is Patañjali's inclusive answer, hardened into a formula. **A second-century-BCE grammarian's compromise becomes a darśana's official doctrine.**

**2. What is a linguistic sound?** Patañjali answers Kātyāyana's *avasthitā varṇāḥ* ("true sounds which are fixed") by introducing a distinction that the tradition never afterwards abandons: ***[[sphota|sphoṭa]]*** ("the sound as it initially breaks out into the open") versus ***dhvani*** ("uttered sounds"). His formulation: "the real sound (*śabda*) is thus the *sphoṭa*… and the quality [length or speed] of the sound is part of *dhvani*."

⚠ **This is the T2 result, and it lands here.** SEP states that **Patañjali introduced the term *sphoṭa* for the first time.** The doctrine's later career belongs to [[bhartrhari|Bhartṛhari]], but its *first technical use* is in this text — not in Pāṇini, and not in a lost pre-Pāṇinian sage. See [[sphota]] for the full divergence.

**3. How do word-meanings combine into a sentence-meaning?** Kātyāyana and Patañjali hold ***abhihitānvaya*** — word-meanings are grasped uncontextualised and combined afterwards. This is the position **Bhartṛhari later rejects** in favour of sentence-holism, and that Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā rejects in the other direction (*anvitābhidhāna*). **The grammarians are not a single voice across time**: the *munitraya*'s semantics and Bhartṛhari's are opposed on the central question.

### Why it is dated, and dated well

Patañjali illustrates grammar with the world around him — the Maurya empire, contemporary events — and so **timestamps his own book**. Hence the unusually firm mid-2nd c. BCE ([[patanjali-grammarian]]), against [[panini]]'s open 350-year range. **The commentary is better dated than the text it comments on.**

SEP adds a motive the technical sources omit: Kātyāyana and Patañjali together effected "a significant **reorganization of Brahmanical views** in the face of opposition from Jains and Buddhists," elevating Sanskrit's status with the claim that "intelligent use of Sanskrit, backed by explicit understanding of its grammar, **leads to prosperity**." ⚠ **The Great Commentary is partly an apologetic document** — grammar defended as a religious good against rival traditions using vernaculars. The corpus records this because a purely technical reading of the text misses why anyone was arguing.

### Five tiers deep

The commentarial stack on this text is the deepest in the batch:

> Pāṇini's **sūtras** → Kātyāyana's **Vārttikas** → Patañjali's **Mahābhāṣya** → Kaiyaṭa's ***Pradīpa*** → Nāgeśa's ***[Mahābhāṣyapradīpa]uddyota***

Abhyankar's dictionary names that last title at first hand. **Nāgeśa, in the early 18th century, is writing a commentary on a commentary on a commentary on a commentary on a rule-book.** Bhartṛhari's ***Mahābhāṣyadīpikā*** (also called *Tripādī*) sits at tier 4 alongside Kaiyaṭa.

⚠ **This is [[tattvacintamani]]'s finding, deeper.** There the corpus found that "by the 17th c. the live text is a commentary on a commentary" — two tiers up. Here the live front by the 18th century is **four** tiers up.

⚠ **CORRECTED, Batch 48 — this file previously claimed the Kaiyaṭa maxim "supplies the doctrinal warrant that makes the climb respectable rather than decadent." It does not, and the error was one of scope.** With the maxim now read at first hand ([[kaiyata]], and Deshpande's exact citation *Pradīpa* on M.Bh. on P. 1.1.29), *yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam* ranks **the three sages and stops**: the authority "increases successively, and **it effectively peaks in Patañjali**." Tiers 4 and 5 — Kaiyaṭa and Nāgeśa — lie **outside the scope of the maxim Kaiyaṭa himself wrote**. ⭐ **So the climb above Patañjali has no doctrinal warrant at all; Kaiyaṭa formulated a principle of ascending authority that stops one tier below himself.** Nāgeśa's gloss at the same page (*uttarottarasya bahulakṣyadarśitvāt*, "the later one has seen a wider range of usage") would license the further climb — but Deshpande notes it over-generates: it "does not explain why this process must end with Patañjali." **The parallel to [[tattvacintamani]] is therefore closer than this file first said, not further: in *both* literatures the climb is a drift, and the one explicit justification turns out not to cover it.**

### ⚠ A contested transmission story, recorded as unresolved

The much-debated verses near the end of **Vākyapadīya** II describe a **decline and recovery** of Mahābhāṣya study, naming **Candrācārya** (widely identified with the Buddhist grammarian **Candragomin**, author of the *Cāndra-vyākaraṇa*).

**The secondary literature available to this session flatly contradicts itself on his role** — one account has Bhartṛhari naming him among those who "did much to push the Mahābhāṣya to the background," another has him "linked with the **revival** of the Mahābhāṣya study, as Bhartṛhari and Kalhaṇa testify." **Blamed for the neglect, or credited with the rescue.**

✅ **NARROWED IN BATCH 49 — and the contradiction is very probably not a dispute at all.** [[candra-vyakarana]] establishes two things this file did not have: (a) **Abhyankar gives the address at first hand — *Vākyapadīya* II.489** is where Bhartṛhari refers to Candra; and (b) the **blame-verse is a different verse with different names in it** — at **VP 2.484** Bhartṛhari criticises **Vaiji, Saubhava and Haryākṣa** for *śuṣka-tarka* and for ignoring Pāṇini and Patañjali, and **Candrācārya is not in that list**; five verses later he is named in the **other** connection, recovering Māhabhāṣya study **in Kashmir**, on a tradition **Kalhaṇa** independently records. ⭐ So one secondary writer very likely attached Candrācārya to the blame-list five verses too early. ⚠ **NOT closed**: the verse numbers are first-hand, but neither verse has been read, and the 2.484 content reaches the corpus at search-summary level **from the same weak provenance that produced the problem** — the corpus does not resolve a weak source with itself. Follow-up **sharpened**: read VP II.481–490 in Rau or Aklujkar.

✅✅ **CLOSED IN BATCH 51 — the verses were read** (GRETIL e-text + **Aklujkar 1978**; full account and sources in [[candra-vyakarana]]). Three results: **(a)** the split is real — the blamed are *Vaiji, Saubhava, Haryakṣa*, "**following dry reasoning**" (*śuṣka-tarkānusāribhiḥ*), and the restorers are "**following the seeds of the Bhāṣya**" (*bhāṣya-bījānusāribhiḥ*, *cāndrācāryādibhiḥ*); **(b)** ⚠⚠ **but the gap is two verses, not five — "five" was arithmetic across two editions' numbering**, Abhyankar's II.489 and the secondary literature's 2.484 being different scales; **(c)** ⭐ the two verses are a **deliberate antithesis built on matched compounds**, which is a better account of the secondary error than a stray name. ⚠ **New uncertainty in exchange:** Aklujkar holds the ten epilogue verses to be **a pupil's, not Bhartṛhari's**.

⚠ **Neither is asserted here.** Both come from **weak provenance** (a personal website), the verses are described in the literature itself as "much debated," and the corpus has not read them. Recorded as an **open question and a batch follow-up**: what VP II actually says about Candrācārya, in a critical edition. What *is* corroborated, from Abhyankar at first hand: **Candra** "is believed to have been the same as Candragomin," and his grammar "is based upon that of Pāṇini, but it **does not treat Vedic forms and accents**" — a competing grammar that drops precisely the vedāṅga content ([[vyakarana]]).

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Mahābhāṣya"** (fetched): 85 āhnikas; "1228 of the 3981 sūtras"; commentary on Pāṇini's rules and Kātyāyana's Vārttika-sūtra; 2nd c. BCE.
2. **SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy"** (fetched, independent, scholarly): the sphoṭa/dhvani distinction with Patañjali's own formulation and Kātyāyana's *avasthitā varṇāḥ*; the Vyāḍi/Vājapyāyana dispute and Patañjali's inclusive verdict; the later Mīmāṃsā/Nyāya/Kevalavyaktivāda alignment; *abhihitānvaya*; the apologetic motive; 200–100 BCE.
3. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand**): Patañjali as "the exponent of Kātyāyana"; the āhnika divisions used as his standard citation unit throughout; Nāgeśa's *Mahābhāṣyapradīpoddyota*; the ~800 works / ~400 authors figure; Candra ≈ Candragomin and the Vedic omission; Vyāḍi "lived after Kātyāyana and before Patañjali."
4. **Wikipedia, "Patañjali"** (fetched, independent): the mid-2nd c. BCE dating cross-checked from a different article.
5. **Internal consistency**: the commentarial-stacking finding is checked against [[tattvacintamani]], reached independently in Batch 46 in the Navya-Nyāya literature.

**CONVERGED** on what the text is, when it was written, how it is structured and what it argues — three independent sources plus a first-hand lexicon, with no conflict on any of it. Confidence **medium**: the Mahābhāṣya itself was **not read**, in any edition or translation; every claim about its contents is mediated. The transmission episode is explicitly left open.
