## Gloss / Divergence map

**Pratyakṣa** ("before the eyes," Sanskrit *prati* + *akṣa*): in the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti Buddhist epistemological tradition, the first and more basic of the two pramāṇas (valid cognitions), defined by two necessary conditions:

**Dignāga's definition** (*Pramāṇasamuccaya* 3, ~5th c. CE):
> *pratyakṣam kalpanāpoḍham nāmajātyādyasaṃyutam*
> "Perception is free from conceptual construction (*kalpanāpoḍha*) — not associated with names, class-characters, or generic features."

**Dharmakīrti's refinement** (*Nyāyabindu* 1.4, ~7th c. CE):
> *tatra kalpanāpoḍham abhrāntaṃ pratyakṣam*
> "Of these [pramāṇas], perception is [cognition that is] free from conceptual construction and non-erroneous (*abhrānta*)."

Dharmakīrti adds *abhrānta* (non-erroneous) to exclude cases where a cognition is free from concepts but still misrepresents reality (e.g., perceiving a tree as moving while on a moving ship). Both conditions together are necessary: nirvikalpaka + abhrānta.

**Four types of Buddhist pratyakṣa** (*Nyāyabindu* 1.7 — *taccaturvidham*):

| type | Sanskrit | object |
|---|---|---|
| Sense-perception | *indriya-pratyakṣa* | external svalakṣaṇas via sense-faculties |
| Mental perception | *mānasa-pratyakṣa* | follows immediately after sense-cognition; same momentary object |
| Self-cognition | *svasaṃvedana* | consciousness apprehending its own existence reflexively |
| Yogic apprehension | *yogijñāna* | direct intuition of e.g. the Four Noble Truths after meditative cultivation; clear and trustworthy |

All four remain *nirvikalpaka* (free from conceptual construction) — this is not relaxed for yogic perception.

**Why only nirvikalpaka counts as genuine perception:** The ontological ground is *svalakṣaṇa* (unique momentary particular) as the only ultimately real (*paramārthasat*) object. Svalakṣaṇas are causally efficacious (*arthakriyāśakti*), momentary, and inexpressible — to perceive them is to contact reality as it is. *Sāmānyalakṣaṇas* (general characteristics / universals) are mind-imposed constructs, not real features of objects (*apoha* theory). When the mind adds conceptual framing ("this is fire," "this is a class of F"), the act has moved from perception to inference. Conceptual perception (*savikalpaka*) is not a degraded form of genuine perception — it is a different cognitive act altogether.

**Three-tradition comparison on pratyakṣa:**

| feature | Buddhist (Dignāga/Dharmakīrti) | Nyāya | Jain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is conceptual perception (*savikalpaka*) valid pratyakṣa? | No — excluded by definition | Yes — *savikalpaka-pratyakṣa* is a legitimate subtype | N/A — Jain pratyakṣa is not divided this way |
| Object of perception | *Svalakṣaṇa* (momentary particular, non-linguistic) | Particular with universal features (Nyāya realism) | Jain: kevala-jñāna perceives all substances in all modes |
| Is omniscience a type of pratyakṣa? | Yogijñāna approaches it but is limited; full omniscience is not claimed | No | Yes — kevala-jñāna is the perfect pratyakṣa (*sakalādeśa*) |
| Role of senses | Sense-perception is the most basic type | Sense-perception is primary and paradigm case | Sensory cognition is *parokṣa* (indirect) in Jain epistemology |
| Universals real? | No (nominalism — apoha) | Yes (realism) | Yes (Jain realism about sāmānya) |

**The deepest divergence from Nyāya:** Nyāya holds that *savikalpaka-pratyakṣa* (determinate, conceptual perception — "this is a cow") is fully valid perception. Dignāga denies this: any cognition that involves class-concepts (*jāti*), names (*nāma*), or generic features (*sāmānya*) has gone beyond perception into inference territory. This is not merely terminological — it reflects opposed ontologies: Nyāya's realism about universals vs. Buddhist nominalism.

**The deepest divergence from Jain:** Jain *pratyakṣa* = unmediated/direct knowledge, which includes super-sensory knowledge (clairvoyance, telepathy, omniscience). Buddhist *pratyakṣa* is strictly non-conceptual sensory/mental/yogic — it never reaches omniscience in the Jain sense. The same word designates opposite ends of the knowledge hierarchy.
