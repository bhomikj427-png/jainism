## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Kātantra** — also called **Kalāpa**, also called **Kaumāra** — is the oldest surviving **non-Pāṇinian** system of Sanskrit grammar: **1,412 sūtras** in **four adhyāyas**, attributed to [[sarvavarman|Sarvavarman]], and for centuries the ordinary teaching grammar of Bihar, Bengal, Gujarat and Kashmir. Abhyankar describes it as "a **systematic abridgment** of the [[astadhyayi|Aṣṭādhyāyī]]" which "ignores many unimportant rules of Pāṇini, adjusts many, and **altogether omits the Vedic portion and the accent chapter**," laying its sūtras out "in an order **different from that of Pāṇini**" — technical terms, sandhi, declension, syntax, compounds, taddhita affixes, conjugation, voice, verbal derivatives.

**Contested on three separate points**, and they are independent of one another:

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐ The finding, and it is bigger than the node: what makes a grammar "a different system" is that it stops being a *vedāṅga*.** Abhyankar's preface — not an entry, and therefore not written to defend any particular headword — sets out the whole post-Pāṇinian field at once:

> Pāṇini "was followed by a number of grammarians who wrote **popular treatises** on grammar, based, no doubt, on Pāṇini's grammar… These treatises, written by **Sarvavarman, [[candragomin|Candragomin]], Devanandin, Pālyakīrti Śākaṭāyana, [[hemacandra|Hemacandra]], Kramadīśvara, Jumaranandin, Supadma** and others… came to be looked upon as **different systems of grammar**." And: "**The special feature of all these grammars was that they entirely omitted the Vedic peculiarities and accents.**"

The corpus's [[vyakarana]] and Ch 40 ("What Grammar Was For") establish grammar's classical self-description: an **ancillary science of the Veda**, one of the six vedāṅgas, whose reason for existing is to preserve and expound śruti. ⭐ **Every named rival system, as received, lacks exactly that.** ⚠ (Written first as "deletes exactly that" — **corrected**; see the box below.) The eight authors Abhyankar lists span roughly six centuries and four religions — Brahminical, Buddhist ([[candragomin]]), Jain (Devanandin, Śākaṭāyana, [[hemacandra]]) — and the one feature they share is the one that would disqualify them as vedāṅgas. ⚠⚠ **CORRECTED LATER IN THIS SAME BATCH — the strong form of this is false, and [[candra-vyakarana]] is where it broke.** Ramhari Timalsina (Heidelberg) shows the Cāndravyākaraṇa had **eight** chapters, not six: **7 (Vedic rules) and 8 (svara/accent)**, placed at the **end** rather than distributed through the work as Pāṇini does — and those two chapters were **lost**. Abhyankar's "six chapters… no section on Vedic Grammar and accents" describes the **received** text and misreports the **composed** one. ⭐ **A transmission accident read as an authorial choice** — by the tradition's leading lexicographer, and then by this node for exactly two commits. **The surviving claim is weaker and better:** *every named post-Pāṇinian system, **as received**, lacks Vedic and accentual material; in at least one case that is demonstrably a **transmission loss**, and the corpus therefore does not know, for any of the others, which it is.* ⚠ The Kātantra is **not** known to have had Vedic chapters — no source says it did, and Abhyankar's *Sarvavarman* entry has the omission as deliberate and motivated. So Abhyankar is not shown wrong here; his **class-level generalisation** is shown to be assembled from received texts and unable to bear an authorial claim. See [[candra-vyakarana]] for the full correction.

⚠ **The corpus does not claim they omitted the Veda *in order to* leave the vedāṅga frame**, and Abhyankar offers no motive; the plainer explanation is on the page — Vedic rules are the hardest part of the Aṣṭādhyāyī and these are teaching books. **Both readings are consistent with the evidence and the corpus asserts neither.** What is established is the *fact* of the shared omission, from a source that states it as a generalisation about the whole class.

**⭐ T1 — "rival or simplification?" — answered, and the batch's framing was wrong in an instructive way.** The queued expectation was that the Kātantra "competes in **use** and not in **theory**." That is right as far as it goes and is confirmed three times over (Abhyankar's "beginners and students of average intelligence"; LinguIndic's "practical grammar"; *Glimpses*'s "shortest extant grammar"). But the frame the batch was testing — that the corpus has been reading **the winner's account** — is the wrong shape. ⭐ **The Kātantra did not lose.** It "attained for several years a very prominent place among text-books on grammar especially in Behar, Bengal and Gujarat," carries "a large number of glosses and commentary works, many of which are in a manuscript form at present," and per *Glimpses* "had a **considerable influence on the Pali grammarian Kaccāyana and also on the Dravidian grammarians**." ⚠ That last claim is **single-sourced and unverified here**, and is flagged rather than relied on — but if it holds, the grammatical tradition that reached Pali and the south is **this one, not Pāṇini's.** The honest correction to the batch premise: the corpus has not been reading the winner's account of a defeated rival. It has been reading the account of the system that **won prestige** while a different one **won classrooms**.

**⭐ Arrangement: the "post-Pāṇinian" systems are, in method, pre-Pāṇinian.** Abhyankar distinguishes two treatments among them — some follow the Aṣṭādhyāyī's element-by-element order; "others give a **topic-wise treatment** following in that respect the ancient grammarians before Pāṇini such as **Indra, Vedic Śākaṭāyana** and others." Of the Kātantra specifically: "As the arrangement of topics is **entirely different** from Pāṇini's order, inspite of considerable resemblance of Sūtras and their wording, it is probable that the work was **based on Pāṇini but composed on the models of ancient grammarians** viz. Indra, Śākaṭāyana and others whose works, although not available now, were available to the author." ⭐ **Pāṇinian in substance, pre-Pāṇinian in form.** ⚠ Note what Abhyankar is doing: inferring the *availability* of lost works from the *shape* of a surviving one. That is a real argument and it is also unfalsifiable in the direction it runs — the lost grammars cannot be checked. Recorded as his inference, marked "it is probable" in his own words.

**⭐⭐ The origin myth, and the hierarchy inside it.** The Kātantra is called **Kaumāra** because, per Abhyankar, "the original instructions for the grammar were received by [Sarvavarman] from **Kumāra or Kārttikeya**" ([[kartikeya]]); the fuller legend has Sarvavarman propitiate **[[shiva|Śiva]]**, who *bids his son* Kumāra do the teaching. And *Kalāpa* is glossed from the same legend — Kārttikeya inscribed the grammar "on the tail (*kalāpa*) of his peacock."

Set that beside what the corpus already holds. [[panini]]'s [[pratyahara|Śivasūtras]] are traditionally traced to **Śiva's drum**; [[kaiyata]] records Deshpande's finding that Kaiyaṭa's ladder of authority stops at [[patanjali|Patañjali]] because he knew *Patañjali-as-Śeṣa* and **not** Pāṇini-taught-by-Śiva. ⭐ **Two Sanskrit grammars, two divine warrants, and the deities stand in exactly the relation the two grammars stand in: Pāṇini gets the father, the Kātantra gets the son — and gets him by the father's delegation.** ⚠ **The corpus records this as a relation between the legends and makes no claim that it was designed** — the alternative, that a Kārttikeya legend attached itself to a grammar already called *Kaumāra*/*Kalāpa* for other reasons, is equally available and Abhyankar in fact treats *Kalāpa* as the primary name. ⭐ But it does extend the "theological motor" pattern of [[patanjali]] and [[kaiyata]] to a **second, competing lineage**: divine attribution is not a Pāṇinian peculiarity, it is how a Sanskrit grammar was made authoritative at all.

**⚠ The date evidence and the corpus's own already-recorded verdict on it (§4 signal 4).** The Sātavāhana dating comes through the legend of Sarvavarman, Vararuci and Guṇāḍhya at the Sātavāhana court — a story whose surviving vehicle is the ***Kathāsaritsāgara***. [[vararuci]] and [[panini]] already record **Abhyankar's own judgement on that text**: "only legendary and hence, it has very little historical value." ⭐ **The Kātantra's date and Pāṇini's biography rest on the same 11th-century Kashmiri tale-collection, and this corpus had already rejected it for the second before it met the first.** ⚠ The corpus therefore does **not** endorse any of the three dates offered; it records the spread and its common weak footing. Abhyankar himself, notably, gives a *reign* rather than a century and separately demolishes the one non-legendary argument (the supposed Pāṇinian reference to Kalāpin at P. IV.3.108 / IV.3.48 and the Patañjali support) as having "not much strength."

**⭐ Abhyankar's "beginners" judgement, twice, and the pattern it makes.** Batch 48's [[kasika]] found him calling the only full commentary on Pāṇini an "excellent help to **beginners**." Here he says Sarvavarman omitted the hard parts "with a view to making his grammar useful for **beginners and students of average intelligence**." ⭐ **The two texts that most people actually learned Sanskrit grammar from are both, in the tradition's own leading reference work, described as books for beginners.** ⚠ Whether that is a fact about the texts or about Abhyankar's register is not settled here; it is at least consistent, which is signal 4 doing its job.

**⚠⚠ An independence failure caught before it entered the file — and it is Batch 48's #5, exactly.** wisdomlib's ***Vyakarana glossary***, which search returns as a separate authority for Sarvavarman ("a reputed grammarian who is believed to have been a contemporary of the poet Guṇāḍhya in the court of Sātavāhana…"), **is Abhyankar's dictionary reprinted verbatim** — the sentence was located word-for-word in the scan at first hand. It is **not a second witness** and is not counted as one anywhere in this node. ⭐ Recorded as a standing rule for the rest of the corpus: **`wisdomlib.org`'s "Vyakarana glossary" = Abhyankar; never count them as two.** (wisdomlib's *other* books — the *Glimpses* volume, the Kṣīrasvāmin study — are separate works and *are* independent of him.)

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Abhyankar**, read at first hand (the preface's account of the whole post-Pāṇinian field; s.vv. *Kātantra*, *Kalāpa*, *Kaumāra*, *Sarvavarman*; the 1,412 count; the Vararuci fourth adhyāya; the refusal of the Kalāpin argument) — **specialist, and on internal matters exactly the kind of source Batch 47's caution says to trust.** **LinguIndic** (independent database: non-Pāṇinian school, ~1,400 sūtras, "practical grammar," no Vedic language, Aṣṭādhyāyī as basis, Durgasiṃhavṛtti). **wisdomlib's *Glimpses of the History of Sanskrit Literature*** (a different book: "shortest extant grammar," Durgasiṃha's Vṛtti in the **8th** century, Ugrabhūti's 11th-c. *Nyāsa* called *Śiṣyahita*, and the Kaccāyana/Dravidian influence claim). ⚠ They agree on the substance — count, arrangement, purpose, Vedic omission — and **disagree on chronology in two places**: the date of the work (50 CE / 1st c. / c. 300 CE) and the date of Durgasiṃha (Abhyankar "the ninth or the tenth century"; *Glimpses* the eighth). ⭐ **That split falls precisely where Batch 47's source-weighting rule predicts it will: the specialist is strong on contents and weak on dates.** **CONTESTED**, confidence **medium** — ⚠ **not a single sūtra of the Kātantra has been read here**, in the original or in translation, and the whole node is a description of a text from outside it.
