## Gloss / Divergence map

**Michael Psellos** (c. 1018 – c. 1096) is the **Byzantine channel** through which a substantial part of late-antique Neoplatonism reached the modern world. Courtier and adviser to a succession of emperors, "the leading professor at the University of Constantinople," and holder of the honorific ***hypatos tōn philosophōn*** — "**Chief of the Philosophers**" — he "championed the reintroduction of classical philosophical study to Byzantine scholarship, particularly Plato and Neoplatonism," a revival whose influence runs on into the Italian Renaissance.

For this corpus his significance is specific and mechanical: **the [[chaldean-oracles|Chaldean Oracles]] survive substantially because Psellos quoted them.**

### The transmission fact

> "The Chaldean Oracles were lost, but many verses have been gathered from the writings of Michael Psellus, whose commentary survives."
> "**The most extensive surviving commentary** was written by the Christian philosopher Michael Psellus in the eleventh century."

And the point compounds: his commentary is also a route back to **[[proclus|Proclus]]'s own lost treatise on Chaldean philosophy**. So Psellos is not one link but two — a source for the 2nd-century oracles *and* a source for the 5th-century Neoplatonist's reading of them.

[[chaldean-oracles]] already records the provenance problem: no fragment survives independent of a later Neoplatonist quoting it, chiefly Proclus, [[damascius|Damascius]], and Psellos "via a different transmission line." This node is what makes that parenthesis concrete, and it sharpens the problem rather than easing it:

- Proclus and Damascius quote the Oracles as **pagan scripture they believe**;
- Psellos quotes them as a **Christian describing a pagan system**, roughly six centuries later, with no living theurgic practice behind him.

Two filters, not one, and they distort in *different directions* — which is genuinely useful (an item present in both is unlikely to be an artefact of either interest) and genuinely limiting (a doctrine Psellos found unremarkable, or dangerous, would leave no trace in his line). The corpus records both halves.

The modern editions inherit the problem directly. **Wilhelm Kroll's *De oraculis Chaldaicis* (1894)** arranged the known fragments by subject with a Latin translation and remains the basis of most later work, including **Hans Lewy (1956)** and **Édouard des Places's Greek–French edition (1971)** — and des Places's edition, on one reviewer's assessment, "has not published **all** Chaldean fragments quoted by Neo-Platonists or later interpreters like the Byzantine intellectual Michael Psellus." The standard critical text is therefore **not a complete record of what Psellos preserves**, which is exactly the kind of fact §4 signal 5 exists to catch: *provenance problems do not stop at antiquity.*

### The contested part: what was he doing?

Psellos's own position is a live scholarly dispute, and it bears directly on how his testimony should be weighted.

| reading | evidence | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Sincere Christian Platonist** | he interpreted "Homeric literature and Platonic philosophy as **precursors to and integral components of Christian doctrine**" — a standard and defensible Byzantine harmonising position | his Chaldean commentary is a work of scholarship and refutation; the pagan material is preserved *because* it is being explained, not endorsed |
| **A pagan sympathiser under cover** | in **1054** he "was accused by his erstwhile friend, the future Patriarch **John Xiphilinos**, of **forsaking Christ to follow Plato**" (Kaldellis); some commentators find irony "transmitting highly critical and subversive messages… even about Byzantine Christian beliefs"; the secondary literature discusses his relation to the Oracles under the heading of "a hypocritical compliance with the tenets of Christianity" | his preservation is closer to *transmission of something held valuable*, and the selection is shaped by sympathy rather than by refutation |

The corpus does not adjudicate. It records that **the charge is contemporary and came from a friend who became Patriarch** — not a modern suspicion projected backwards — and that the safest handling of a Psellos fragment is: *sourced, filtered, and filtered by an author whose commitments were disputed in his own lifetime.*

His pupil **John Italos** succeeded him as Chief of the Philosophers and was formally accused of "Hellenizing" — teaching metempsychosis and the eternity of the world — and "confessed and recanted" twice. That is the institutional limit of the revival stated as an event: **the Byzantine church would tolerate the study of Plato and not the adoption of him**, and the line ran somewhere between master and pupil.

### The pattern this node belongs to

The corpus has now recorded five **interested transmission channels** — sources through which a doctrine survives only because someone with their own purposes chose to preserve it:

| channel | what survives through it | the interest |
|---|---|---|
| Buddhist opponents | [[vindhyavasin|Vindhyavāsin]]'s Sāṃkhya | refutation |
| Hostile doxography, incl. the *Sarva-darśana-saṅgraha* | [[carvaka|Cārvāka]] | ranking it lowest in a hierarchy ending at Advaita |
| [[simplicius|Simplicius]] | the Presocratics | philosophical commentary with its own agenda |
| [[vacaspati-mishra|Vācaspati Miśra]] | [[varsaganya|Vārṣagaṇya]]'s Sāṃkhya | doxographic completeness |
| **Psellos** | the **Chaldean Oracles** and Proclus's lost commentary | a Christian revival of Platonism, in a court, under suspicion |

Psellos is the **most distant** of the five — separated from his material by six centuries, a religion, and an empire — and preserves some of the most. The lesson the corpus draws is not that such testimony is unreliable but that **distance and interest are two different variables**, and that the honest question is always *what would this channel have had no reason to keep?*
