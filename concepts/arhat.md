## Gloss / Divergence map

**Arahant** (Pali; Sanskrit: *arhat* — "worthy one," "one who has destroyed the enemies [of defilements]"): the fourth and final stage of Theravāda liberation — the **fully liberated being**. An arahant has eradicated all ten fetters (*saṃyojana*) that bind consciousness to the cycle of rebirth (*saṃsāra*), and upon physical death (*parinibbāna*) will not be reborn in any realm.

**Standard canonical definition** (SN 22.110, *Araham Sutta*):
> "a monk in whom the cankers (*āsavā*) are destroyed, who has lived the life to perfection, done what had to be done, put down the burden, gained the highest goal, worn through the fetters of rebirth, and is liberated by perfect insight."

**The four progressive stages of liberation** (cutting the ten fetters in sequence):

| stage | Pali | fetters eliminated | rebirth remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stream-enterer | sotāpanna | 1–3: sakkāyadiṭṭhi (self-view), vicikicchā (doubt), sīlabbata-parāmāsa (rite-attachment) | at most 7 more rebirths in human / heavenly realms |
| Once-returner | sakadāgāmī | 1–3 fully + 4–5 weakened: kāmarāga (sensual lust), byāpāda (ill-will) | one more rebirth in the sensual realm |
| Non-returner | anāgāmī | 1–5 fully: all five lower fetters | reborn in Pure Abodes (suddhāvāsā), attains arahantship there |
| Arahant | arahant | all 10: + rūparāga, arūparāga, māna (conceit), uddhacca (restlessness), avijjā (ignorance) | no further rebirth; parinibbāna at death |

**What is destroyed:** The arahant has extinguished the three roots of unwholesomeness — **lobha** (greed), **dosa** (hatred), **moha** (delusion) — in their deepest forms. No new kamma is generated. The arahant continues to live out the lifespan's remaining kammic results, but creates no fresh bondage. The final destruction is *avijjā* (ignorance, especially ignorance of the four noble truths and of anattā).

**The anattā (no-self) implication.** An arahant does NOT "go somewhere" at parinibbāna. The five aggregates (*khandha*s) disperse; no individual self (*attā*) persists to reside in a heaven. This is the sharpest contrast with the **Jain siddha** (individual soul permanently residing at Siddhāśilā) and the **Hindu ātman** (either identical with brahman or in eternal relation to Viṣṇu). For Theravāda, the question "where does the arahant go after death?" is unanswerable — it is one of the *avyākata* (undeclared) questions the Buddha rejected as ill-formed.

**Arhat vs bodhisattva — the central Mahāyāna critique:**

| dimension | arahant (Theravāda) | bodhisattva (Mahāyāna) |
|---|---|---|
| liberation goal | personal nibbāna: destruction of one's own defilements | liberation of all sentient beings (sarvasattva-hitāya) |
| attitude to rebirth | ends rebirth as quickly as possible | deliberately takes rebirth to help others |
| Mahāyāna label | "hīnayāna" (lesser vehicle) — criticised as "selfish" liberation | "mahāyāna" ideal — compassionate, universal |
| Theravāda response | the arhat IS the Buddha's taught path (SN 22.110); bodhisattva ideal is post-canonical innovation |

Walpola Rahula (Theravāda scholar-monk) argued both traditions accept the bodhisattva ideal in principle; Theravāda restricts it to the historical Gotama's past lives (Jātaka tales). But for practice, the arahant remains the Theravāda normative goal.
