## Gloss / Divergence map

**Śaurasenī** is a Prākṛt — "the dialect of the Śūrasenas," notionally of the Mathurā region — which the grammarians and dramaturgists treat as the **prose register of the stage**, as against [[prakrta-prakasha|Māhārāṣṭrī]] for verse. ⭐ **It is this corpus's first node for a Prākṛt language rather than a Prākṛt grammar**, and it exists because Batch 50's reading found the same textual gap read in two irreconcilable ways by two first-hand sources.

## Reasoning across signals

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The same gap, read twice — and the corpus corrects a file it committed this batch

This is the finding the node exists for, and it is a **direct collision between two first-hand sources over a single fact.**

**Cowell (1854)** finds no Bhāmaha commentary on Section XII and reads it as **loss**. The judgement is not idle: on its strength he declares the sūtras "obscure and corrupt" and **declines to translate a printed text he has in front of him** — the only section of the book he leaves untranslated.

**Ollett (2017)** finds the identical absence and reads it as a **terminus**: the chapter was not there to be commented on. And unlike Cowell he does not argue from the absence alone. Abhinavagupta, in the eleventh century, glosses *ardhasaṃskṛta* by reporting that others take it to be "Prakrit as characterised by the Prakrit definitions composed by Vararuci and others, **excluding regional languages such as Śaurasenī**" (*Abhinavabhāratī* 4:385, on *Nāṭyaśāstra* 32.382). ⭐⭐ **An eleventh-century reader describes a Vararuci that stops short of Śaurasenī.** Ollett: "This confirms that the version of *Light* known to Abhinavagupta did not define Śaurasenī; Bhāmaha's commentary also does not extend to the chapter on Śaurasenī."

⭐⭐⭐ **This is not one scholar's opinion against another's. It is Ollett *with a dated external witness* against Cowell *with an inference from absence*, and the corpus takes Ollett.** It also explains an anomaly found at first hand and left unexplained in [[prakrta-prakasha]]: **why the section that both other dialect sections declare as their base is printed last.** Because it was added last.

⚠⚠ **And it forces a correction to [[bhamaha]], written and committed earlier in this same batch.** That node states that Bhāmaha's commentary on Section XII "is LOST," and builds a paragraph on the loss — a printed text untranslatable for want of a commentary — as a demonstration of Ch 45 §1.2. ⭐ **That paragraph inherited Cowell's inference without testing it, three hours after this same batch caught Cowell's contemporaries doing the same kind of thing.** Corrected there, with the reasoning left visible rather than tidied away.

⭐⭐⭐ **The error has a shape, and the corpus has met its mirror image.** Batch 49's `candra-vyakarana` caught **a transmission accident read as an authorial choice** (Abhyankar on the Cāndra's missing Vedic chapters). This is the inverse: **a redactional terminus read as a transmission accident.** ⚠ Both errors sit at the *end* of a text, and both are made by first-rate editors, because **the end of a text is where "absent" and "lost" are hardest to tell apart** — and where, per [[prakrta-prakasha]], material is most likely to have been added. **A file's last chapter is the least trustworthy thing in it.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Why the three chapters were added — a datable event, and it supersedes the corpus's own explanation

Batch 49 proposed a mechanism for the terminal-module pattern: "the organisation that makes a grammar teachable is the organisation that makes its parts detachable," offered as "a pattern with three instances, not a law." **Ollett supplies a documented mechanism for two of the three, and it is not that one.**

> "The threefold schema of Sanskrit, Prakrit, and Apabhramsha was replaced by a **sixfold schema that added Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, and Paiśācī**. The earliest text to exhibit this refiguration is **Rudraṭa's *Ornament of Literature*, composed in Kashmir in the early ninth century**."

And then, in a list: "Other multilingual grammars from around this time include **the 'expanded' version of the *Light on Prakrit*, with chapters on Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, and Paiśācī**… The most complete and most influential grammar of this type was **[[hemacandra|Hemacandra]]'s *Siddhahemacandra*, which adopts Rudraṭa's 'six languages' as its organizing principle**."

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The three chapters bolted onto *Light* are precisely the three languages Rudraṭa added to the schema — and Hemacandra's celebrated eighth book is organised by that same schema.** So the "terminal module" in both cases is not a pedagogical habit; it is **a new genre — the multilingual grammar — appearing in the ninth to twelfth centuries**, and going at the end of existing books because it was *added* to them.

Ollett even supplies the cause behind the cause: a shift of critical attention from "**literature heard**" (*śravyakāvya* — lyric and courtly epic, monoglossic) to "**literature seen**" (*dṛśyakāvya* — the stage, polyglossic), traceable to Bhaṭṭa Udbhaṭa's Kashmiri *Nāṭyaśāstra* commentarial tradition under Jayāpīḍa (**779–813**). ⭐ **Śaurasenī and Māgadhī "were used exclusively in the theater"** — so when theatre became the object of theory, the languages of theatre needed grammars.

⚠ **The corpus's synthesis is therefore superseded for the two dialect cases and untested for the third** ([[candra-vyakarana]]'s Vedic and svara chapters, a different kind of module entirely, and one that was **lost** rather than added). ⭐ **Recorded as a supersession, not a refutation, and the earlier reasoning is left standing in `prakrta-prakasha.md` where it can be seen to have been wrong for an instructive reason: it generalised from architecture when the evidence was about audience.**

### ⭐⭐ Śaurasenī is the base twice over, in two systems that are not each other's sources

The *Prākṛtaprakāśa*'s redactors make it the *prakṛti* of Paiśācī and Māgadhī. The *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s scheme of seven *bhāṣās* (Māgadhī, Āvantī, Prācyā, Śaurasenī, Ardhamāgadhī, Bāhlīkā, Dākṣiṇātyā) has it as the default. ⭐ **Two descriptions converge on one language as the unmarked case** — which is a real result and not a large one, and the corpus states it at that size: it tells us what the *describers* took as neutral, not what anyone spoke. ⚠ **Ollett's warning is explicit and this node adopts it**: the *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s compilers "had defined one kind of 'Prakrit' by reference to another," borrowing "the name, as well as the basics of a grammatical description, from the discourses of '**literature heard**'… in order to characterize the language practices of the **stage play**." ⭐ **The stage languages were described in vocabulary built for lyric poetry.** So their apparent relationships may be relationships between *descriptions*.

⭐⭐ That is exactly why Ollett refuses **Manomohan Ghosh's** inference that Śaurasenī is simply an older stratum than Māhārāṣṭrī. ⚠ **And note the source-handling this forces**: Ghosh is *also* the authority Ollett cites approvingly for "further arguments against the identification of Bhāmaha with the Kashmiri poetician" (n. 35). ⭐ **The corpus takes Ghosh on Bhāmaha and leaves him on Śaurasenī, following Ollett — which is §4's "take the term, leave the editorializing" applied to a scholar rather than to a website.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Jain Śaurasenī — and a §0 warning aimed straight at this repository's anchor

Pischel's classification, as Ollett reports it: the Jains used **Ardhamāgadhī** in the [[svetambara|Śvetāmbara]] canon, **Jain Śaurasenī** in the **[[digambara|Digambara]] doctrinal literature**, and **Jain Māhārāṣṭrī** in the commentarial and narrative literature of both sects. ⭐ **The corpus's [[kundakunda]] and [[samayasara]] belong to the second of those**, and until now nothing in the repository said what language they were in.

⚠⚠⚠ **And Ollett immediately warns against the use this fact is usually put to, in terms this charter should adopt verbatim.** The proposition "that the language of the Jain tradition is, and always was, Prakrit, and that the use of Prakrit is part of what characterizes Jainism as an **inclusive and egalitarian religion** in contrast to the Brahmanical traditions, which insisted on using the obscure and exclusive Sanskrit language" — is, he says, resting on "a **threefold continuity**" that has to be argued and is not; and Alsdorf's version of it ("an uninterrupted tradition on the soil of the motherland," "anti-brahmanic, popular linguistic origins") he calls "plausible, if **sentimental and indigenist**."

⭐⭐⭐ **This is a §0 failure mode the charter did not have a name for.** §0 guards against **inflation into modern physics**. This is inflation into **modern politics** — a language's sociolinguistic status read off its literary distribution, and then read as the ethical character of a religion. ⚠ **The mechanism is identical**: a real structural fact (Jains wrote in Prākṛts) quietly mapped onto a modern category (popular, egalitarian, anti-elite), with the mapping never stated as a mapping. ⭐ **And it is aimed at the tradition this repository is anchored on, by the specialist the repository just started reading.** Recorded here, and flagged for the charter.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Cowell 1854** at first hand (the register definition, `prakṛtiḥ śaurasenī` at X.2/XI.2, the Section XII headnote, Hemacandra's Śaurasenī chapter printed as Appendix C, the *Sāhityadarpaṇa* verse via Lassen); **Ollett 2017** at first hand (the Abhinavagupta witness, Rudraṭa and the six languages, the *Nāṭyaśāstra* default, the Ghosh rejection, Pischel's Jain classification, the *Sāhityadarpaṇa* verse independently); **Abhinavagupta** as a dated eleventh-century witness reaching the corpus through Ollett (⚠ **not** independent of Ollett — one channel). ⭐ **The Sāhityadarpaṇa verse is the batch's cleanest independence check**: two editors, 163 years apart, no shared descent, same verse, same import. ⚠ **CONTESTED** — Cowell and Ollett directly contradict each other on the missing chapter, and Ghosh and Ollett on the language's age — confidence **medium**: the register, the base-position and the Jain distribution are secure; **the language's history is not, and Ollett's own argument is that it may not be recoverable from these sources at all.**
