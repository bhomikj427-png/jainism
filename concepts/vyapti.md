## Gloss

Invariable concomitance — the relation of universal pervasion between a logical reason (hetu/liṅga) and what is to be proved (sādhya). Vyāpti is the backbone of inference (anumāna) in all three classical Indian epistemological schools: without established pervasion, no inference is valid. The canonical formulation is: *yatra dhūmaḥ tatra vahniḥ* — "wherever there is smoke, there is fire." All schools accept this structure; they disagree sharply on what grounds it and whether it is knowable with certainty.

## Two forms

| form | Sanskrit | statement |
|---|---|---|
| **anvaya-vyāpti** | positive concomitance | "wherever there is smoke, there is fire" (co-presence) |
| **vyatireka-vyāpti** | negative concomitance | "wherever there is no fire, there is no smoke" (co-absence) |

Both forms are recognised across traditions. A complete inference typically deploys both.

## Cross-tradition comparison: how vyāpti is grounded

| tradition | position | grounding mechanism | key texts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nyāya** | vyāpti is knowable via repeated observation + tarka (hypothetical reasoning) | universals (sāmānya-lakṣaṇa): perceiving particular smoke and fire also perceives "smokeness" and "fireness" — universal properties that bind the concomitance across past/present/future | Nyāyasūtra; Navya-Nyāya (Gaṅgeśa, Tattvacintāmaṇi) |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | vyāpti established by trairūpya — the three conditions a valid reason must satisfy | reason must be present in the subject-locus (pakṣa), present in similar cases (sapakṣa), absent in all dissimilar cases (vipakṣa) — purely formal, no appeal to universals | Pramāṇasamuccaya (Dignāga, ~5th c.) |
| **Buddhist (Dharmakīrti)** | vyāpti grounded in real necessity, not just formal patterns | two ontological grounding types: causal relation (kāryahetu — smoke caused by fire) or identity relation (svabhāvahetu — oak is-a-tree); rejects Nyāya universals as abstract fictions | Pramāṇavārttika (Dharmakīrti, ~7th c.) |
| **Jain** | vyāpti derived from induction; both anvaya and vyatireka forms recognised | not a separate positive account found in fetched sources; Jain inference (anumāna) shares the hetu/sādhya framework with Nyāya; naya-standpoint view of inference adds the caveat that any inferential claim is partial | Tattvārtha Sūtra Ch. 1 (pramāṇa chapter) |
| **Cārvāka (rejects)** | universal concomitance is unknowable | no mechanism can verify all past/present/future cases; induction is always incomplete | Lokāyata tradition |

## Key contested point: universals vs. particulars

Nyāya grounds vyāpti in universals — abstract properties (sāmānya) that inhere in particulars and are perceptible through "supernormal connection" (sāmānya-lakṣaṇa-pratyāsatti). Buddhist nominalists reject universals as real entities; Dignāga and Dharmakīrti recast vyāpti in purely particular-level terms (trairūpya, causal necessity). This is not a dispute about whether vyāpti exists — all inference-accepting schools use it — but about what the world must be like for vyāpti to be knowable.
