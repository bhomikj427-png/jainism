## Gloss

The ***Nyāsa*** — its author's own title is the ***Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā*** — is [[jinendrabuddhi|Jinendrabuddhi's]] commentary on the [[kasika|Kāśikāvṛtti]], and therefore a **sub-commentary at the third remove from the [[astadhyayi|Aṣṭādhyāyī]]**. It is **the oldest extant commentary on the Kāśikāvṛtti**, written in the eighth or ninth century, and it is the text through which the Kāśikā was read: Abhyankar names it and [[haradatta|Haradatta's]] *Padamañjarī* as the two "well-known" commentaries on the Kāśikā, and says Haradatta "has drawn considerably from Nyāsa."

⚠ **The key is tradition-suffixed on purpose.** *Nyāsa* is not a title but a **genre label** — see the collision table in [[jinendrabuddhi]] — and *nyāsa* is separately a live technical term in this corpus at [[nikshepa]].

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐⭐ The finding, read at first hand and stated by working editors: the commentary is a better witness to the Kāśikā's text than any manuscript of the Kāśikā.** Ajotikar, Ajotikar and Scharf, building a digital critical edition, set out the situation in four sentences:

> "**Manuscripts in India last no more than about five hundred years. The oldest readable manuscript of the KV. dates only to the early fifteenth century.** Yet several centuries earlier, the KV. was commented upon in the *Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā* by Jinendrabuddhi in the **eighth or ninth century** and then in the *Padamañjarī* by Haradatta in the **thirteenth century**. These commentators provide information about the constitution of the text of KV. in several ways: **by direct citation and incipits, as well as less directly by discussion on the text.** The information provided by commentators **hundreds of years prior to the oldest manuscript is invaluable to reliably establishing the text of the KV.**"

⭐⭐ **This qualifies a caveat the corpus placed on itself in Batch 48, and qualifies it in the direction of the commentators.** [[helaraja]] and [[avacchedaka]] record, as a warning, that "the corpus has never seen those verses apart from the commentary." Scharf and the Ajotikars say the same fact and call it **invaluable**. ⭐ **Both are right, and holding both is the whole discipline in miniature:** the sub-commentary is simultaneously the **only long-range witness** to its base text and an **interested** one, and there is no position outside that pair from which to check. ⚠ The corpus's Batch-48 caveat is therefore **not withdrawn and not weakened** — it is *relocated*: the risk is not that commentarial mediation is avoidable, it is that it is **universal**, so the honest move is to say which commentary you are standing on, every time. ⭐ **"Manuscripts in India last no more than about five hundred years" is the single most useful sentence this batch has read**, because it explains why.

**⭐⭐ And they state, technically, the epistemic asymmetry the corpus has been circling since Batch 47.** The passage is worth having exactly:

> "When a critical edition is prepared, the assumption is that each manuscript covers the entire span of text edited… Hence only variants are reported in the critical apparatus and **it is assumed that silence regarding a witness reports its support for the adopted text**… In contrast, commentaries on scientific texts, and on grammatical texts in particular, **generally do not mention or comment upon every word of their base text**… **Subcommentaries as a rule specifically mention only a small proportion of the words in the base text. Since the full text is not always cited, one cannot assume that silence regarding a reading in the base text indicates support.**"

⭐ **A commentary is a partial witness with no valid argument from silence.** That is the precise, technical form of a caution the corpus has been stating loosely — [[kasika]] already records the untidy version of it ("the Mahābhāṣya is silent on 69% of the sūtras, so for most of the Aṣṭādhyāyī there was no Mahābhāṣya wording to follow"). ⚠ **Note the sharper consequence for this corpus's own habits:** whenever a node says "the commentator does not dispute X," that is worth **nothing**. The editors' remedy is to record **positive readings as well as variants**, so that coverage can be *measured* rather than assumed — and their unit problem ("neither a word nor a reading can accurately serve"; they will count characters in SLP1, noting that "traditional accounting of the extent of text in India used the orthographic syllable") is the same instrument that produces [[mahabhashya]]'s **1,228-of-3,981** figure and [[kasika]]'s "all 3,981." ⭐ **The corpus's two best coverage statistics turn out to be early, coarse instances of a measurement these editors are now making properly.**

**⚠ A correction to [[jinendrabuddhi]], made one node later and caused by a gap in the OCR.** That node reported, from Abhyankar, that the *Nyāsa* "unfortunately is available only in a fragmentary state at present." **That is wrong.** The scan breaks mid-sentence across two blocks, and the clause belongs to **Maitreya Rakṣita's twelfth-century commentary on the Nyāsa — the *Tantrapradīpa*** — which is "very largely quoted by subsequent grammarians, but which unfortunately is available only in a fragmentary state." The *Nyāsa* itself is fully published (a two-volume edition sits on archive.org) and is the base of an active critical-edition project. ⭐ **Corrected in place.** ⚠ The mechanism is worth naming because it will recur: **an OCR gap silently reattached a relative clause to the wrong noun** — which is, exactly, the [[nagesha-bhatta|Hindupedia appositive]] defect of Batch 48, arriving this time by a different road and caught by an independent source rather than by re-reading.

**⭐ The stack over the Nyāsa, and a ranking rule that never came.** Abhyankar names four commentaries on it: **Maitreya Rakṣita's *Tantrapradīpa*** (12th c.), **Śivabhaṭṭa's**, **Mahāmiśra's**, and one by ⭐ **Mallinātha, "the standard commentator of prominent Sanskrit classics"**; plus an *Anunyāsa* quoted in Sīradeva's *Paribhāṣāvṛtti*. The chain is **Aṣṭādhyāyī → Kāśikāvṛtti → Nyāsa → Tantrapradīpa / Mallinātha** — four and five tiers. ⭐⭐ **[[kaiyata]]'s maxim ranks the three sages on the *Mahābhāṣya* branch and stops at [[patanjali|Patañjali]]; this branch is just as deep and has no ranking rule at all** — and its base is the text Abhyankar calls a help to beginners. ⚠ No source consulted remarks on the asymmetry; it is the corpus's observation from first-hand entries.

**⭐ What the critical-edition project reveals about the Kāśikā's own state — and it closes a Batch 48 follow-up.** The standard critical edition (**Sharma, Deshpande & Padhye, 1969–70**) rests on "**only nine manuscripts** as well as four previous editions," while the *New Catalogus Catalogorum* notices "**more than two hundred**." ⭐ **The Kāśikā — [[kasika|"the only extant full-length commentary on the sūtras"]] — has been critically edited from under 5% of its known witnesses.** And Batch 48 carried as follow-up #6 "**Pascale Haag, *Studies in the Kāśikāvṛtti: The Section on Pratyāhāras***"; this paper supplies its place in the sequence — "**Haag and Vergiani (2009)**," the pratyāhāra section, the first fruit of an effort "begun nearly thirty years ago" — and names the current successor: "**new funding under the direction of Malhar Kulkarni at the Indian Institute of Technology Bombay**," aiming at the first pāda of the first adhyāya. ⭐ **A follow-up advanced from a bare title to a dated publication inside a named research programme.**

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Ajotikar, Ajotikar & Scharf** — ⭐ **read at first hand**, the PDF fetched and extracted: the manuscript-lifespan claim, the fifteenth-century date, the two commentaries' dates, the argument-from-silence passage, the Sharma/Deshpande/Padhye and Haag/Vergiani and Kulkarni bibliography, the TEI apparatus and the unit problem. **A peer-reviewed digital-humanities paper by working editors of the text — the strongest provenance in this batch, and a D1 result.** **Abhyankar** at first hand, independently: the *Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā* title and the "Nyāsa" byname, the eighth-century dating, the eastern school, the four sub-commentators, the Haradatta debt, and the *Nyāsa*-as-genre note. ⚠ They **agree** on authorship, base text, relative order and importance, and **differ only in precision** on the date — Abhyankar's flat "eighth century" against Scharf's "eighth or ninth," which is Batch 47's source rule yet again and in the same direction. **CONVERGED**, confidence **medium** — ⚠ **the *Nyāsa* itself has not been read**, and everything here is about the text's position rather than its contents.
