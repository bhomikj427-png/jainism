## Gloss / Divergence map

**Āyurveda** (*āyus*, "life/longevity" + *veda*, "knowledge") is the traditional Indian system of medicine, organized around the **tridosha** theory (three bodily humours — vāta, pitta, kapha; see `tridosha.md`), the **dhātus** (seven bodily tissues) and **malas** (waste products), and the notion of **prakṛti** — an individual's fixed constitutional dosha-balance, determined at conception, which Āyurvedic diagnosis and treatment aim to keep in equilibrium against a person's fluctuating current state (*vikṛti*).

**The "5,000-year-old" claim vs. the textual record.** Popular and promotional literature routinely calls Āyurveda a 5,000-year-old system, tracing it to the Vedas (particularly scattered plant/disease references in the Atharvaveda). Wikipedia's sourced account is markedly more conservative: ayurvedic *terminology and concepts* appear in the Buddhist Canon from the mid-1st-millennium BCE onward, but "the main classical ayurveda texts are datable in their present form to the first centuries CE" — roughly 2,000 years, not 5,000. This is a real gap between the tradition's own self-presentation and the verifiable textual record, and it is recorded here rather than smoothed into the rounder, more marketable number.

**Two foundational Saṃhitās.** Āyurveda's classical textual base rests on two large compendia with different centers of gravity: the **Charaka Saṃhitā** (internal medicine; see `charaka.md`) and the **Suśruta Saṃhitā** (surgery; see `sushruta.md`). Both are layered, multi-redactor texts rather than the single-author treatises their titles suggest (detailed in each concept's own file). A later synthesis, **Vāgbhaṭa's Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdayam**, is frequently cited alongside them as a third major classical authority but is not itself a batch concept here.

**Modern scientific standing.** Wikipedia's sourced position is blunt: contemporary critics hold that the doshas "are not real, but are a fictional concept"; there is no evidence Āyurveda can treat or cure cancer; and some rasaśāstra (mineral/metal-based) preparations have been found to contain toxic levels of lead, mercury, and arsenic. This is recorded as the mainstream secular-scholarly assessment, distinct from and not overriding the internal, source-grounded account of what the tridosha theory *claims* (the concern of this file and `tridosha.md`).

Three independent Wikipedia articles (Ayurveda, Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita) converge on the dating band and the two-text structure → **converged**. Confidence held at **medium** rather than high because all signals trace back to one platform (Wikipedia) even though the underlying articles are separately maintained and cite different primary sources; no independent academic monograph (e.g., Meulenbeld's *History of Indian Medical Literature*) was directly fetched for this umbrella file — that fetch was done for the two Saṃhitā files instead.
