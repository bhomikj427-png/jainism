## Gloss / Divergence map

**Vāgbhaṭa** is the third member of the ***bṛhat-trayī***, the "great triad" of classical Āyurvedic authorities — "traditionally regarded as a successor to [[charaka|Caraka]] and [[sushruta|Suśruta]], and together they are often described… as forming a classical triad." Two large works bear his name, and **whether one man wrote both is unresolved.**

He is placed "broadly… in the early medieval period, often around the sixth century CE," with origins in the "north-western Indian subcontinent," and "precise biographical details remain uncertain." His own concluding verses are the main biographical source — the *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha*'s author "identifies himself as the son of **Siṃhagupta** and a pupil of **Avalokita**."

### The two works

| | ***Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha*** (AS) | ***Aṣṭāṅgahṛdayasaṃhitā*** (AH) |
|---|---|---|
| form | prose — "a longer and less concise work" | verse — "written in poetic language," **7120 Sanskrit verses** |
| scale | **150 chapters**; "greater in size… and greater in scope (subjects and details)" | **120 chapters** |
| afterlife | less widely transmitted | translated "into many languages, including **Tibetan, Arabic, Persian** and several modern Indian and European languages" |

The *aṣṭāṅga* of both titles is Āyurveda's **eight branches** — the organising scheme both works are built on, and the reason they function as *compendia* rather than as new doctrine. The **AH is the one that travelled**, and it is the text this corpus has already been leaning on: the tissue-fire relation cited in [[dhatu]] and [[agni-ayurveda]] (A. Hṛ. Sū. 11/24) is his.

That travel is the historically significant fact about Vāgbhaṭa. A Tibetan and an Arabic *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* means Indian medicine entering **two entirely separate later traditions** — the Tibetan *gso ba rig pa* and the Islamicate medical literature — through this text rather than through Caraka or Suśruta. Concision travels.

### One Vāgbhaṭa or two?

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Two Vāgbhaṭas — grandfather and grandson** | "some old commentators have referred to two different Vāgbhaṭas, one the **Elder** Vāgbhaṭa and the other the **Junior** Vāgbhaṭa. On the basis of this scholars have suggested existence of a duo of the grandfather and his grandson" | the AS colophon's own genealogy: "There was a great physician by name Vāgbhaṭa, who was my grandfather, **I bear his name**; from him was born Siṃhagupta and I am from him; I was born in the **Sindhu country**." Two men, same name, two generations apart — which is exactly the shape a later reader would need to explain two texts |
| **Two authors, relation unresolved** | "modern philological research… argues that these two texts are **unlikely to be the work of a single author**," and the relationship between the treatises "remains a subject of ongoing scholarly debate and **has not been conclusively resolved**" | the texts differ enough that shared authorship is doubted on *internal* grounds — but this does **not** endorse the grandfather/grandson story, which is a traditional harmonisation, not a philological finding |

The corpus keeps these apart deliberately, because they are usually run together. **The philological claim is negative** (one author is unlikely); **the traditional claim is positive and specific** (two named men in one family). Accepting the first does not give you the second. Note also that the "I bear his name" verse would, if authentic, *already* be a grandson explaining a homonym — i.e. **the ambiguity is attested inside the primary source**, not introduced by later readers.

This makes Vāgbhaṭa the third instance in the Āyurvedic cluster of the same problem: [[charaka]] and [[sushruta]] are both recorded here as `contested`, both multi-redactor compendia with unresolved chronology. **All three great authorities of classical Indian medicine are, as persons, uncertain** — while the texts under their names are perfectly definite. That pattern is worth stating as a pattern (compare [[vyasa]], [[valmiki]], [[kapila]], [[govinda-bhagavatpada]]): in this literature, *a name is a title on a compendium before it is a biography.*

### The Avalokita question — flagged, not asserted

**Avalokita**, named as his teacher, is a **Buddhist name** (cf. Avalokiteśvara), and Vāgbhaṭa is widely described in the secondary literature as a Buddhist or as writing in a Buddhist milieu — Sindh in this period being a plausible setting for it. **The Wikipedia article as fetched does not address his religion at all**, and no fetched source states it. The corpus therefore records the teacher's name as a datum, notes the inference it invites, and **does not make the claim**. Anyone upgrading this node should look for the argument, not for the assertion — the relevant evidence would be his handling of Buddhist medical material and his invocatory verses, neither of which was consulted here.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Vagbhata"** (fetched): the *bṛhat-trayī* placement as successor to Caraka and Suśruta; "early medieval period, often around the sixth century CE" and north-western origin with "precise biographical details remain uncertain"; the **AS colophon self-identification** as son of Siṃhagupta and pupil of Avalokita; AS as prose and "longer and less concise," AH as verse with **7120 Sanskrit verses**; "modern philological research… argues that these two texts are unlikely to be the work of a single author"; and the **Tibetan, Arabic, Persian** translation range. Explicitly checked and found to carry **no** Sindh reference, **no** treatment of his religion, and **no** commentary or chapter-count detail.
2. **Search aggregation** (*International Research Journal of Ayurveda & Yoga*, "A Comparative Study of Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdaya and Aṣṭāṅga Saṃgraha"; easyayurveda's "Acharya Vagbhata"; the Motilal Banarsidass and Exotic India edition listings; the Internet Archive scans of both texts): the **150 vs 120 chapters** and "greater in size… and greater in scope"; the **Elder/Junior Vāgbhaṭa** commentarial tradition and the grandfather/grandson hypothesis built on it; and the **AS colophon verse** quoted in full, including "I was born in the **Sindhu country**" — the Sindh datum, which signal 1 lacks.
3. **Corpus cross-check** ([[charaka]], [[sushruta]], [[ayurveda]], [[dhatu]], [[agni-ayurveda]]): both other members of the triad are already recorded `contested` with multi-redactor composition and unresolved chronology, which is what turns Vāgbhaṭa's authorship problem from an isolated puzzle into the third instance of a pattern; and the A. Hṛ. Sū. 11/24 citation reached this corpus in the present batch through the Caraka digitisation, i.e. **his text was being used here before he had a node**.
4. **Provenance weighting** (§4 signal 5): the primary texts exist in **printed critical editions and full Internet Archive scans** (the *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha*; the *Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdaya* with two Sanskrit commentaries), and in the standard Motilal Banarsidass three-volume translation. These were **located and not read** — an unusually well-attested upgrade path for a node this uncertain, and the reason the confidence cap here is about *effort spent*, not about *evidence available*.

**CONTESTED**, on the central question of authorship, and on his dates. Converged on the two works, their forms and relative scale, the *bṛhat-trayī* placement, and the AH's onward transmission. Confidence **medium**: two source-lines agree on everything checkable, and one of them is thin; but neither primary text was read in any edition, the chapter counts and verse total come from secondary summaries, and the two most interesting biographical items — Sindh and Avalokita — each rest on a single source-line.
