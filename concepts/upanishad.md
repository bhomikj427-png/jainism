## Gloss / Divergence map

**Upaniṣad** (Sanskrit, *upa-ni-ṣad*, "sitting near [a teacher]"): the philosophical-contemplative texts forming the final stratum of Vedic literature, collectively termed **Vedānta** ("end of the Veda"). The Muktikā canon lists **108** Upaniṣads; the **12–13 mukhya** ("principal") ones — those commented on by Śaṃkara (8th c. CE) — constitute the scriptural foundation of all Vedānta schools.

**Principal mukhya Upaniṣads by Vedic affiliation:**

| Veda | Upaniṣads |
|---|---|
| Ṛgveda | Aitareya, Kauṣītakī |
| Sāmaveda | Chāndogya, Kena |
| Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda | Taittirīya, Kaṭha, Śvetāśvatara, Maitrī |
| Śukla Yajurveda | Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Īśā |
| Atharvaveda | Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Praśna |

**Dating (contested in detail, converged at order-of-magnitude):** Olivelle (1998) places the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya — the two oldest — in the **7th–6th c. BCE** (pre-Buddhist); three subsequent prose Upaniṣads (Taittirīya, Aitareya, Kauṣītakī) in the 6th–5th c. BCE. Radhakrishnan (1953) widened this to 900–600 BCE. Western scholarship generally accepts 700–500 BCE for the earliest texts. The remaining ~95 Upaniṣads span the last centuries BCE through the 15th c. CE.

**Central doctrines:**
- **Brahman**: the one ultimate reality; see brahman.md
- **Ātman**: the individual self or soul; the Upaniṣads argue ātman and brahman are identical or structurally related
- **Mahāvākyas** ("great sayings") — four, one per Veda, identified by Śaṃkara as asserting ātman-brahman identity:
  - *prajñānaṃ brahma* ("consciousness is brahman") — Aitareya Upaniṣad 3.3
  - *ahaṃ brahmāsmi* ("I am brahman") — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.10
  - *tat tvam asi* ("that thou art") — Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.8.7
  - *ayam ātmā brahma* ("this self is brahman") — Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 1.2

**The Vedānta divergence on the ātman-brahman relation** — a genuine finding, not a defect in the texts:

| school | reading | proponent | what it commits to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advaita | strict identity: ātman = brahman; world is māyā | Śaṃkara (8th c.) | one undifferentiated consciousness; liberation = recognition of identity |
| Viśiṣṭādvaita | qualified monism: selves and matter are real modes of brahman | Rāmānuja (11th–12th c.) | brahman internally differentiated; liberation = eternal participation, not merger |
| Dvaita | permanent distinction: brahman ≠ ātman; world fully real | Madhvācārya (13th c.) | five-fold eternal difference (pañcabheda); liberation = eternal proximity |
| Bhedābheda | simultaneous identity and difference | Nimbārka, Vallabha | real but non-absolute distinctness |

All four schools read the *same* mukhya Upaniṣads. The Brahmasūtra's bhāṣyas do not resolve the ambiguity — they produce the disagreement. This is itself a scholarly consensus (Olivelle, Radhakrishnan, and all major Indologists agree the texts are ambiguous at precisely this point).

**European transmission.** Dārā Shukūh's Persian translation (*Sirr-i Akbar*, 1656) led to Anquetil-Duperron's Latin (*Oupnek'hat*, 1801–1802), which drove Schopenhauer's engagement. This transmission path is independent of and parallel to the Śaṃkara bhāṣya tradition.

2. **Olivelle, Patrick, *The Early Upaniṣads: Annotated Text and Translation*, Oxford University Press (South Asia Research), 1998** — critical Sanskrit edition with full apparatus; 32 Upaniṣads; identifies Bṛhadāraṇyaka + Chāndogya as oldest (pre-Buddhist); emphasizes composite textual evolution across centuries. OUP catalogue confirmed.

3. **Radhakrishnan, Sarvepalli, *The Principal Upaniṣads*, George Allen & Unwin, London, 1953** — 18 Upaniṣads translated; 900–600 BCE dating; notes mutual śramaṇa-Upaniṣadic exchange. A genuinely independent second scholarly treatment with different selection and chronological methodology.

Independence confirmed: Olivelle (1998) and Radhakrishnan (1953) are 45 years apart, differ in Upaniṣad selection and dating method, and reach independently similar conclusions about the brahman-ātman-mahāvākya core. Confidence medium: individual verse texts were not independently fetched from primary critical editions.
