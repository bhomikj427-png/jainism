## Gloss / Divergence map

A tīrthaṅkara ("ford-maker") is a **human being who attains omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*) through their own effort and then teaches the path to liberation to others**, thereby creating a *tīrtha* (fordable passage across the sea of saṃsāra). A tīrthaṅkara is also called a *jina* ("conqueror") — one who has conquered all passions and karma.

**Key properties of a tīrthaṅkara:**
- Attains omniscience (guṇasthāna 13 — sayoga-kevalī) by their own effort, not by divine grace
- Teaches the four-fold community (*cāturvarṇya-saṅgha*): monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen
- At physical death (guṇasthāna 14 → mokṣa), resides permanently at Siddhashilā (lokāgra) as a siddha
- **Does not intervene** after liberation — no prayers answered, no boons granted; veneration of tīrthaṅkaras is for inspiration, not supplication
- Is **not a creator of the universe** — Jainism has no creator God; the universe is eternal and self-subsisting

**24 per cosmic half-cycle:** Jain cosmology posits endless alternating half-cycles — descending (*avasarpiṇī*, conditions worsen) and ascending (*utsarpiṇī*, conditions improve). In each descending half-cycle of our *mahākalpa*, 24 tīrthaṅkaras appear at progressively later intervals.

**The 24 tīrthaṅkaras of the current avasarpiṇī** (our present descending half-cycle):
- **First:** Ṛṣabhanātha (Ādinatha) — mythological; said to have taught agriculture, arts, and the Jain path at the dawn of civilization
- **23rd:** Pārśvanātha (~8th c. BCE) — widely accepted as historical; taught four mahāvratas (before brahmacarya was added)
- **24th (final):** Vardhamāna Mahāvīra (~599–527 BCE) — accepted as historical; reformer and systematiser; added celibacy (*brahmacarya*) as the fifth mahāvrata; the *Tattvārthasūtra* is the systematic exposition of his teaching

**Scholarly consensus on historicity** (Wikipedia, citing Padmanabh Jaini and academic sources): Pārśvanātha (~8th c. BCE) and Mahāvīra (~6th c. BCE) are broadly accepted as historical. The first 22 tīrthaṅkaras are mythological/legendary.

**Relationship to the Tattvārthasūtra:** The TS was composed by Umāsvāti (~2nd–5th c. CE) as a systematic Sanskrit summary of Mahāvīra's teaching. Umāsvāti is not himself a tīrthaṅkara but a scholar (ācārya) working within the tradition. The text's authority derives from its claim to faithfully represent the omniscient tīrthaṅkara's knowledge.

**Non-theistic note:** Despite occupying a role analogous to a religious founder, a tīrthaṅkara is structurally non-theistic. The tradition is explicit: tīrthaṅkaras do not create, sustain, or destroy the universe; they do not hear or answer prayers after liberation; their status as siddhas is a state of perfect individual consciousness at lokāgra, not a cosmic governance role.
