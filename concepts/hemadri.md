## Gloss / Divergence map

**Hemādri** (*hema* + *adri*, "golden mountain" — a name of Sumeru), **fl. 1260–1309**, son of Kāmadeva, son of Vāsudeva, son of Vāmana, is the **chief minister and archivist of the Yādava court at Devagiri** and the author of the ***Āyurvedarasāyana***, the second standard commentary on [[vagbhata|Vāgbhaṭa]]'s *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* — shorter than [[arunadatta|Aruṇadatta]]'s *Sarvāṅgasundarā*, and printed beside it in every standard edition.

He is the rare case in this corpus of a commentator whose **day job is documented from outside the text**:

| evidence | what it fixes |
|---|---|
| In the *Caturvargacintāmaṇi* he describes himself as **in charge of the state records** of king **Mahādeva of Devagiri (r. 1260–71)** | a self-statement, in a dated reign |
| From the *Āyurvedarasāyana* **and a contemporary inscription**, he was **archivist and chief minister** of Mahādeva's successor **Rāmacandra (r. 1271–1309)** | an *external*, epigraphic confirmation |
| Aufrecht's *Catalogus Catalogorum*: "lived under king Mahādeva (1260-71) of Devagiri, son of Caitrapāla, and under his successor Rāmacandra (1271-1309)" | a manuscript catalogue, agreeing |

His output is enormous and spans two disciplines that rarely meet: the ***Caturvargacintāmaṇi***, a Dharmaśāstra encyclopedia, is his magnum opus; the *Āyurvedarasāyana* is a medical commentary; and Aufrecht adds the *Kaivalyadīpikā*, *Muktāphalaṭīkā*, *Kālanirṇaya*, *Tithinirṇaya*, *Dānavākyāvalī* and *Śrāddhapaddhati*. Popular tradition additionally credits him with temples across the Maratha country — the *Hemāḍpanti* style is named for him — and with inventing the **Moḍī** script; both are **recorded here as attributions, not as findings**, since no critical source consulted supports either.

### The finding: this node loosens a date-bracket the corpus had drawn too tight

[[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]] is dated in this corpus by **citation-bracketing** — he quotes [[cakrapanidatta|Cakrapāṇi]] (fl. 1060) and is *quoted by Hemādri* — and the bracket's upper end was taken from Wikipedia as "**Hemādri (fl. 1260)**," giving "between the late 11th and the early 13th century."

**1260 is the earliest year of Hemādri's recorded career, not the date of his medical work.** Gode is explicit: "Evidently the *Āyurvedarasāyana* must have been composed **between 1271–1309 A. D.**" — the reign of Rāmacandra, whose minister Hemādri was. And the *Āyurvedarasāyana* is his only medical work, so it is where a quotation of the Suśruta commentator would sit.

A *terminus ante quem* is only as late as the citing work, so the consequence runs one way: **Ḍalhaṇa's upper bound moves later.** "Early 13th century" is too tight; the evidence as now assembled supports an upper bound of **c. 1309**, and cannot support anything earlier than **1271** unless the quotation is shown to be in a different work of Hemādri's. ⚠ **No source consulted names which of Hemādri's works quotes Ḍalhaṇa** — Wikipedia says only "Hemādri." That is the gap, and it is stated rather than papered over. [[dalhana]] is updated to carry the looser bound and the reason.

This is the batch's clearest instance of a general point: **a date-bracket is only as good as the dating of the men who form its walls.** Ḍalhaṇa's bracket looked like a fact about Ḍalhaṇa; half of it was a fact about how carelessly Hemādri had been dated.

### Two commentaries on one text, and what the pair is for

Aruṇadatta (c. 1220) and Hemādri (1271–1309) are always named together because they are always **printed** together, and the sources agree on the division of labour: Aruṇadatta "explained nearly each and every word of Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya, while the commentary of Hemādri is quite shorter." Hemādri also **quotes Aruṇadatta by name** — Gode gives the form: "*maireyo* … '*kharjurāsavaḥ*' *iti aruṇadattaḥ*."

So the second commentary is not a replacement but a **response**: written some fifty to ninety years later, with the first one open on the desk, arguing with it where it disagrees. The *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*'s apparatus is therefore a **conversation**, and the standard edition preserves both sides — which is a materially different object from the single-commentary editions of Caraka and Suśruta this corpus has met at [[cakrapanidatta]] and [[dalhana]].

### The court and the corpus

Worth registering plainly, because it cuts against a habit: **the man who wrote a medical commentary on Vāgbhaṭa was a state archivist, not a physician of record.** The corpus has assumed nothing about the professions of Cakrapāṇi or Ḍalhaṇa, and this node is a caution against assuming — commentary on a technical śāstra was work a senior bureaucrat with an encyclopedist's habits could do, alongside a Dharmaśāstra summa and the state's records. It also explains the unusual quality of his evidence: he is well dated **because he was a minister**, exactly as [[dalhana]] predicted ("how well a pre-modern Indian figure can be dated is mostly a fact about what kind of institution they stood next to").

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Three+ sources, tested for independence** — **Aufrecht's *Catalogus Catalogorum*** (19th-c. critical manuscript catalogue, via Wisdomlib); **P. K. Gode**, *Studies in Indian Literary History* (manuscript-based literary history, supplying the 1271–1309 composition window and the Aruṇadatta quotation); and **search-level summaries of the Āyurvedic secondary literature**, which independently give the self-statement in the *Caturvargacintāmaṇi* and the corroborating inscription. The first two are the load-bearing ones and are not derivable from each other or from the web layer.
2. **Commentarial tradition** — the anchor, and here it runs three deep: Vāgbhaṭa's root text, Aruṇadatta's gloss, Hemādri's gloss that quotes Aruṇadatta. The corpus can see the whole stack.
3. **Lexicon + morphology** — *hema* (gold) + *adri* (mountain); the dictionaries give "the mountain Sumeru" as the primary sense and then, tellingly, "**name of an author of the encyclopædic work *Caturvargacintāmaṇi***" as a second — the lexicon knows him as the Dharmaśāstra encyclopedist, not as the Āyurvedic commentator, which is a fair index of where his reputation actually sits.
4. **Internal consistency** — swept `concepts/`: he was **already load-bearing in this corpus without a node**, holding up the upper wall of [[dalhana]]'s date-bracket. Writing him turned out to *change* that bracket rather than confirm it, which is the strongest argument this batch offers for writing the nodes a corpus merely cites.
5. **Provenance** — good at the top (Aufrecht, Gode, and an epigraphic corroboration reported by both), thin below. The one item written on exactly this subject — *Journal of Ayurveda* 16:1 (2022), "Hemādri — Renowned Commentator of *Āyurvedarasāyana* Commentary on *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdayam*" — is **paywalled (HTTP 402) and unread**, as is the *Journal of Indian Medical Heritage* paper on both scholiasts. Two directly relevant peer-reviewed papers located and neither obtained is the reason this is `medium`.

**CONVERGED** on dates, lineage, patrons, office and works — Aufrecht and Gode agree in detail, and the reign-dates are externally anchored. Confidence **medium**: neither the *Āyurvedarasāyana* nor the *Caturvargacintāmaṇi* was read; both dedicated modern papers are paywalled; the temple-style and Moḍī-script attributions are popular tradition and are labelled as such; and the node's own central claim — that Ḍalhaṇa's upper bound must move — depends on an inference (that the quotation sits in his only medical work) that **no source states**.

⚠ **Added Batch 45 — Hemādri as a witness in a modern dispute.** Rao & Sharma (*Asiatische Studien* LVII/1, 2003, §2.4) adduce **Hemādri's quotation of the *Vātsa Gṛhya*** as collateral evidence that a Vedic recension Roque Mesquita labelled fictitious was still being read in the 13th century — one strand of the case against the claim that [[madhva|Madhva]] invented his own scriptural authorities. Two cautions: they give Hemādri only as "13th cent." and **do not name the work**, and they take the fact from Datta & Satya Shrava rather than from Hemādri directly. The *Caturvargacintāmaṇi*, a dharmaśāstra digest, is where a Gṛhyasūtra citation would naturally sit — but that is an **inference, not a source**, and is recorded as a lead only. It is the same open question in a different key as this file's own unresolved "which of Hemādri's works quotes Ḍalhaṇa."
