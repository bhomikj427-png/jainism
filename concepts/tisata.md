## Gloss / Divergence map

**Tīsaṭa** (10th century) wrote the ***Cikitsākalikā***, a short therapeutic manual of about **400 verses** in varied metres, and is the father of [[candrata|Candraṭa]], who wrote the commentary on it. He is one of the most **quoted** authors in the whole Āyurvedic literature and one of the least **known**.

**Converged.** Meulenbeld — read here at first hand — settles the work, its structure, its sources, its reception and its date, and the other sources add nothing that contradicts him. What is *contested* is not the scholarship but the tradition's own claim about his parentage, which Meulenbeld demolishes and this file records as demolished.

### ⭐ The finding: Batch 45 got one half of a colophon; this is the other half

`candrata.md` (Batch 45) reported the collapse of its own queue premise: **Candraṭa is called "Vāgbhaṭa's grandson" only through colophons Meulenbeld calls untrustworthy.** Reading the *Tisaṭa* section supplies the missing piece — **the same colophon tradition calls Tīsaṭa the son of Vāgbhaṭa**:

> "The colophons of a number of MSS call Tīsaṭa the son of Vāgbhaṭa."

**That is what makes Candraṭa the grandson.** One genealogical claim, asserted at two generations, in the same manuscript apparatus. Meulenbeld rejects it in three moves:

1. **"these colophons may date from a later age"** — they are not the author's testimony;
2. **"the absence of the name of Tīsaṭa's father in the text of the *Cikitsākalikā* and its commentary"**;
3. **"no information is available on the identity of this Vāgbhaṭa."**

⚠⚠ **Reason 2 is the one worth dwelling on, because it exposes the mechanism.** The *Cikitsākalikā*'s **opening verse praises Tīsaṭa's father** — alongside Sūrya, the Aśvins, Dhanvantari and Suśruta — **and does not name him.** Meulenbeld: he "was the son of a physician whose name is not disclosed."

**The text leaves a slot open, and the tradition later fills it with the most famous name available.** That is a concrete, observable mechanism for the manufacture of a false genealogy, and it explains both halves of the Vāgbhaṭa claim at once. The corpus has been circling this in `candrata.md`, `arunadatta.md` and `jejjata.md`; here it is with the blank still visible in the text.

⚠ Note also what Meulenbeld does with **P. V. Sharma**, twice — a named scholar checked and not followed:
- Sharma's claim that Tīsaṭa used **Vāgbhaṭa**'s works "is in need of corroboration," and although some drug-groups "may have been inspired by Vāgbhaṭa … **this author is not referred to as a source by Tīsaṭa and clear resemblances cannot be detected**."
- Sharma's view that Tīsaṭa borrowed from **[[vrnda|Vṛnda]]'s *Siddhayoga*** "**has no firm basis, since the formulae referred to are not closely similar in wording**."

### The absent name

> "**The name of Vāgbhaṭa is conspicuously absent.**"

That is Meulenbeld's own sentence, verified here at first hand, and it is the claim `candrata.md` leaned on. The *Cikitsākalikā*'s two introductory verses name **Hārīta, Suśruta, Parāśara, Bhoja, Bhela, Bhṛgu, Agniveśa and [[charaka|Caraka]]** as its sources, and the authorities cited in the body are **Āstīka, the Aśvins, Bhela, Bhṛguja (Śukra), Caraka, Janakātmaja, Kaṅkāyana, Parāśara, Pracetas (Varuṇa), Suśruta and Videha**. Candraṭa's commentary adds **Caraka, Hārīta and Kṣārapāṇi**.

**A 10th-century therapeutic manual lists a dozen authorities and omits the man whose son the colophons say he was.** ⚠ **Argument from silence, and labelled as one** — it does not prove Tīsaṭa did not know Vāgbhaṭa; it shows he did not treat him as an authority, which is what the genealogy would lead you to expect.

### ⚠ The date: an author dated by his own son's footnote, through a third party

This is the sharpest illustration the corpus has of Ch 35's thesis that **citation evidence is ordinal, not cardinal** — because here there is an extra hop.

> "The chronological position of the *Cikitsākalikā* can be deduced **only from Candraṭa's commentary**."

- **Terminus ad quem**: **[[niscalakara|Niścalakara]]'s statement** that [[cakrapanidatta|Cakrapāṇidatta]] (*c.* A.D. 1050) used Tīsaṭa's work in composing his own compendium. ⚠ **Follow that chain: a 12th–13th-century commentator's remark about an 11th-century author's sources is what supplies the upper limit for a 10th-century writer.** The evidence is not Tīsaṭa's, not Cakrapāṇi's, but Niścalakara's *about* Cakrapāṇi.
- **Terminus post quem**: Candraṭa quotes the *Siddhasāra* and its author **Ravigupta**, *c.* A.D. 650. **This fixes Tīsaṭa and Candraṭa together between 650 and 1050** — a four-hundred-year bracket for a father and son.
- **The narrowing to 900–1050** depends on a conditional: **if** Candraṭa wrote a commentary on **Vṛnda's *Siddhayoga***, "as seems to follow from a remark of Śivadāsasena."
- **Conclusion**: "The evidence available points to the **tenth century** as the age in which Tīsaṭa must have lived."

⚠ **And note the method Meulenbeld refuses.** Candraṭa's commentary shows he knew **[[haricandra|Haricandra]]'s and [[jejjata|Jejjaṭa]]'s** commentaries, which would give a terminus post quem — but "**as Jejjaṭa's date is disputed, it is preferable to depart from another fact**." This is exactly the rule `jejjata.md` extracted in Batch 45: **a disputed date cannot anchor another date.** Here is Meulenbeld applying it in his own words, in a different section, reached independently. **The corpus derived the rule; this passage shows the scholar stating it.**

⚠ **A dating argument rejected**: **Jolly** proposed that "Bhoja" in the second verse points to the eleventh century as a terminus post quem. Meulenbeld: "It is quite uncertain who this Bhoja was and when he lived." **A name in a source-list is not a date.**

### The man, so far as there is one

> "**Nothing is known with certainty about Tīsaṭa, except that he was the father of Candraṭa** … and … that he was the son of a physician whose name is not disclosed."

Two further inferences, both Meulenbeld's and both flagged by him as inference:
- **"Tīsaṭa's name may indicate that he was born in Kaśmīr."**
- **He was a *brāhmaṇa* of the Saura (sun-worshipping) tradition** — Candraṭa explains that **Sūrya**, praised in the *Cikitsākalikā*'s first verse, was **Tīsaṭa's family deity**, and Candraṭa's own *Yogaratnasamuccaya* shows he "followed his father's footsteps in being a sun-worshipper." ⚠ Meulenbeld adds: **"just like [[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]]."** **A dedication verse, read by a son who knew the household, yields the family's religion — and joins Tīsaṭa to another Āyurvedic author the corpus already holds.**
- **G. Halder** supposes Tīsaṭa also wrote a *Vaidyatriṃśat*. Recorded as one scholar's supposition.

### The book

**Two unequal parts**: verses **3–94** on principles and general treatment, **99–399** on therapy — the latter divided by the **eight *aṅga*s** of Āyurveda: *kāyacikitsā* (99–325), *śālākya* (326–352), *śalya* (353–364), *bhūtavidyā* (365–377), *kaumāra* (378–380), *viṣa* (381–389), *rasāyana* (390–394), *vājīkaraṇa* (395–399).

Notable inside it:
- **Verses 17–40 on the [[tridosha|tridoṣa]]** — "the famous verses, **often quoted by later authors**, on the causes of excitement of each of the three *doṣa*s and the symptoms of this excitement." **This is the corpus's clearest case of a specific passage, not a whole work, becoming canonical.**
- **Eighteen *gaṇa*s of drugs** (48–65), a number "peculiar to Tīsaṭa's work"; the first six subdue *vāta*, *pitta*, *kapha*, *vāta-pitta*, *vāta-kapha* and *pitta-kapha*. Several *gaṇa*s "may have been borrowed from the *Suśrutasaṃhitā*."
- **A list of diseases ascribed to bad acts committed in previous lives** (10–12) — karma as an aetiological category inside a therapeutic manual.
- The three chief ***marman*** given as the **cardiac, ano-rectal and umbilical** regions (25).
- **"Tīsaṭa's arrangement of the diseases in the section on *kāyacikitsā* has no parallel in other treatises."**
- ⚠ **The new diseases introduced by Mādhava and Vṛnda are absent** — noticed by Meulenbeld as "worthy of notice" but explicitly **not** used as evidence for an early date, "since many works composed after the eighth century disregard Mādhava's scheme."

### Reception: the measure of him

Meulenbeld's list of authors quoting or referring to Tīsaṭa or the *Cikitsākalikā* runs to roughly **thirty** names across eight centuries, and includes five the corpus already holds — **[[hemadri|Hemādri]], [[niscalakara|Niścalakara]], Ṣoḍhala, Śivadāsasena, Vijayarakṣita** (in the *Madhukośa*) — plus Adhamalla, Anantakumāra, Bhāvamiśra's *Bhāvaprakāśa*, Todara, Trimalla, the *Vīrasiṃhāvaloka*, the *Yogaratnākara*, and modern commentators down to **Priyavrat Sharma**'s auto-commentary on his own *Dravyaguṇasūtra*. Harṣakīrti, Jayaratna and Māgacandradeva name the *(Cikitsā)kalikā* among their sources.

**A man of whom "nothing is known with certainty" is cited continuously for eight hundred years.** That gap — between the thinness of the life and the density of the reception — *is* the finding about him.

**Why converged.** Every question about the work — its size, structure, sources, peculiarities, reception and period — has one well-evidenced answer, and the one traditional claim that conflicts (the Vāgbhaṭa parentage) is not a rival scholarly reading but a manuscript-colophon assertion that the scholarship rejects with reasons. **A tradition contradicted by evidence is not a divergence.** **Why medium, not high** — and this is the honest constraint: **it rests on one modern authority**, and §4 reserves `high` for independent agreement. **No Sanskrit was read**: not the *Cikitsākalikā*, not Candraṭa's commentary, not the introductory verses whose contents are so load-bearing here; Sharma, Jolly, Halder and Śivadāsasena are all reached through Meulenbeld's report; and the reception-list is taken on his authority. `term_devanagari` is **omitted** rather than guessed (§3).
