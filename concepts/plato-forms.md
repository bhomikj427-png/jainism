## Gloss

The Platonic Forms (eidos / idea) — eternal, unchanging, aspatial, atemporal archetypes that constitute true reality. Physical particulars (this beautiful painting, this just act) are imperfect, temporary copies that merely *participate in* or *imitate* the Forms (Form of Beauty, Form of Justice). The Form of the Good is supreme — it grounds the intelligibility and value of all other Forms. Forms are known through the intellect, not the senses; sense-perception gives only opinion (*doxa*) about changing particulars, while reason (*nous*) grasps the eternal Forms. This is the most extreme "universals realism" in Western philosophy: universals exist separately from and independently of all particulars.

## Key features

| feature | content |
|---|---|
| ontological status | separately existing, mind-independent realities — more real than physical things |
| relationship to particulars | participation (*methexis*) / imitation (*mimesis*) — particulars "share in" Forms without depleting them |
| Form of the Good | supreme Form; the source of intelligibility and being for all other Forms (Republic 509b) |
| primary sources | *Phaedo* (immortality of soul + Forms), *Republic* (Cave allegory, Form of Good), *Timaeus* (Demiurge using Forms as patterns), *Parmenides* (Plato's own self-critique) |
| epistemology | Forms are known by recollection (*anamnesis*) — the soul encountered them before birth; sense-experience merely triggers recollection |

## Aristotle's critique — the Third Man Argument

Aristotle rejects separately existing Forms: if the Form of Man explains what many men share, and if the Form is itself a man-like entity, then a third entity (a "Third Man Form") is needed to explain what the Form and particulars share — generating an infinite regress. Aristotle's solution: forms are immanent (in things, not separate from them) — this is the root of his hylomorphism in *aristotle-substance.md*.

## Cross-tradition comparison

| tradition | position on universals | parallel/contrast to Platonic Forms |
|---|---|---|
| **Platonic** | transcendent universals — exist separately, independently, eternally | (defining position) |
| **Aristotelian** | immanent universals — exist in particulars; no separate Form of Man | direct rejection of Plato; substance-in-particular is primary |
| **Advaita Vedānta** | Brahman alone is real; particulars are māyā (appearance) | closest Indian parallel: Forms ≈ Brahman as true reality; physical world ≈ appearance; difference: Brahman is ONE, Forms are many |
| **Jain** | sāmānya (universal) is real but depends on individuals — immanent | closer to Aristotle: Jain universals exist only in and through individual dravyas; no separately existing Form of Jīva |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | no universals — only momentary particulars; apoha theory explains general terms without positing universals | strongest rejection: Buddhist nominalism eliminates Forms entirely |
| **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** | sāmānya (universal) is real and immanent via samavāya (inseparable inherence) | immanent universals, not transcendent — viśeṣa (ultimate particular) is also a distinct category |

## The Plato-Brahman conflation: warning

The parallel between Platonic Forms and Advaita Brahman is often overstated. Key differences:
- Brahman is **one** (non-dual Absolute); Forms are **many** (Form of Good, Form of Beauty, etc.)
- Brahman is the *substance* of the world; Forms are *archetypes* outside the world
- For Advaita, the individual ātman ultimately *is* Brahman; there is no equivalent in Plato of particular souls merging with the Form of Soul
The structural analogy (true reality behind appearance) is genuine; the identity claim is not.
