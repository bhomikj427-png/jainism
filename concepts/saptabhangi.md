## Gloss / Divergence map

The saptabhaṅgī is the **sevenfold predication scheme**: the formal logical completion of syādvāda. It enumerates all canonical ways a *syāt*-qualified assertion can be made about any subject-predicate pair, using three primitives — **asti** (is / exists), **nāsti** (is not / does not exist), and **avaktavya** (inexpressible / indescribable) — and their combinations:

| # | Sanskrit | English rendering |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *syād asti* | "in some respect, it is" |
| 2 | *syān nāsti* | "in some respect, it is not" |
| 3 | *syād asti nāsti* | "in some respect, it both is and is not" (sequentially — from two standpoints) |
| 4 | *syād avaktavyaḥ* | "in some respect, it is inexpressible" (asti and nāsti asserted simultaneously — language fails) |
| 5 | *syād asti avaktavyaḥ* | "in some respect, it is, and is inexpressible" |
| 6 | *syān nāsti avaktavyaḥ* | "in some respect, it is not, and is inexpressible" |
| 7 | *syād asti nāsti avaktavyaḥ* | "in some respect, it is, it is not, and is inexpressible" |

(Source: Wikipedia "Jaina seven-valued logic," June 2026, citing Mallavādin and later systematisers.)

**Why avaktavya arises:** Predicates 1 and 2 are *sequential* assertions from two different standpoints. Predicate 3 holds them together sequentially. Predicate 4 (*avaktavya*) arises when *asti* and *nāsti* must be asserted *simultaneously* — language, which is linear, cannot express this simultaneously. Inexpressibility is not ignorance; it is a structural feature of finite linguistic expression when confronted with genuinely complex reality.

**Relation to syādvāda and naya:** Saptabhaṅgī does not generate new philosophical content; it *formalises* the one-predication-at-a-time method of syādvāda into a complete, closed set. Each of the seven assertions corresponds to a subset of the naya standpoints being applied.

**Authorship:** Wikipedia (citing scholarly literature including Matilal, Dundas, Ganeri) identifies **Mallavādin** (5th–6th c. CE, Śvetāmbara) as the first systematic formulator. The scheme was elaborated by Akalanka (~8th c.), Haribhadra, and Hemacandra (~12th c.). The key text for the scheme is Mallisena's *Syādvādamañjarī* (1292 CE). It is not in the TS.

**Buddhist critique:** Dignāga and Dharmakīrti objected that predicates 3 and 7 violate the law of non-contradiction. Jain response: *asti* and *nāsti* in predicate 3 are relativised to different standpoints (dravya-naya vs. paryāya-naya), not asserted simultaneously of the same object under the same description — so non-contradiction is not violated, it is *parametrised*. (Wikipedia, citing Matilal.)

**Modern many-valued-logic comparison (caution):** Some scholars draw a parallel between the three primitives (asti / nāsti / avaktavya) and Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic (T / F / indeterminate). Wikipedia notes that "some researchers caution against reinterpreting it" — the parallel oversimplifies. Saptabhaṅgī is a *predication scheme* for qualified assertions, not a *truth-value algebra*: the seven forms are not seven truth-values but seven modes of standpoint-qualified assertion. The mathematical logic apparatus does not transfer cleanly.
