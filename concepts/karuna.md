## Gloss

**Karuṇā** is **compassion** — the heart's response to the suffering of others, the wish that they be *free from* suffering. It is the **second of the four *brahmavihāra*** and second of the four ***appamaññā*** (immeasurables). Where mettā wishes beings well unconditionally, karuṇā is mettā meeting suffering specifically: it "takes away from the heart the inert weight, the paralyzing heaviness" (Nyanaponika), opening one beyond the boundary of self-concern.

**Near and far enemy:**
- **Far enemy:** cruelty / harming (and, in some lists, callous indifference) — its outright opposite.
- **Near enemy:** sentimental **pity** or grief that *resembles* compassion but sinks into the sufferer's distress and weakens the capacity to actually help. True karuṇā remains clear and effective; its counterfeit is to be overwhelmed.

**Mahāyāna amplification.** Karuṇā is shared across all Buddhism, but Mahāyāna makes ***mahākaruṇā*** ("great compassion") the engine of the entire bodhisattva path: it is the motive (paired with the wisdom of *śūnyatā*) that generates **bodhicitta**, the vow to attain Buddhahood for the sake of *all* beings. In Theravāda karuṇā is one of four sublime abidings cultivated toward liberation; in Mahāyāna it expands into the defining orientation of the vehicle.

**Distinction worth keeping.** Buddhist compassion is grounded in *intention* (*cetanā*) and in the recognition of shared suffering — not, as in Jainism, in an ontological account of harm to a *jīva*. Karuṇā motivates non-harm (*avihiṃsā*), but the mechanism is psychological-empathic, not the matter-physics of Jain ahiṃsā.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Wikipedia, "Brahmavihara":** karuṇā as the second of the four brahmavihāra / immeasurables (*appamaññā*); compassion as "identifying others' suffering as one's own"; the Tevijja Sutta and Visuddhimagga (Buddhaghosa, 5th c.) as canonical/commentarial sources; cultivation by radiating to all beings; Yoga-Sūtra 1.33 listing the same four.

**Signal 2 — Nyanaponika Thera, "The Four Sublime States" (Access to Insight):** independently defines karuṇā as the sympathetic response that "removes the barriers of selfishness," names its near enemy (sentimental pity that weakens real help) and far enemy (cruelty/indifference), and places it among the four immeasurables.
