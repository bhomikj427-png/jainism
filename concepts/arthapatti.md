## Gloss

Postulation / presumption — a sixth pramāṇa (valid cognition) accepted by the Bhāṭṭa school of Mīmāṃsā: the positing of an unobserved fact required to make two known facts coherent with each other. Arthāpatti is the Indian analogue of "inference to the best explanation": when two established facts are mutually inconsistent without a third, the mind cannot rest until that third is posited. The Mīmāṃsā claim is that this positing mechanism cannot be reduced to ordinary inference (anumāna) — it is an independent pramāṇa. All other Indian schools disagree.

## Classic examples

| example | two known facts | posited resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Fat Devadatta** | (1) Devadatta is fat; (2) Devadatta does not eat during the day | He must eat at night — the only way to make both facts coherent |
| **Absent Caitra** | (1) Caitra is alive (testified); (2) Caitra is not at home (seen) | He must be outside somewhere — otherwise "alive" + "absent from home" cannot both be true |

In both cases: the posited fact is not directly perceived and not inferred via prior vyāpti. It is *required* by the logical pressure of the two facts together.

## Two sub-types

| type | Sanskrit | basis of tension |
|---|---|---|
| **seen-based** | dṛṣṭa-arthāpatti | both known facts from direct experience (seeing fat body + daytime behaviour) |
| **heard-based** | śrūta-arthāpatti | one or both facts are testimonially given (told that Caitra is alive; seeing absence from home) |

## Why Mīmāṃsā treats it as an independent pramāṇa

Mīmāṃsā's central argument: arthāpatti involves **simultaneous apprehension** of the contradiction and its resolution. This differs structurally from anumāna (inference), which is **sequential**: first the hetu is observed, then vyāpti is recalled, then the sādhya is concluded. In arthāpatti there is no prior universal to recall — the posited fact is generated *de novo* by the logical pressure of the two contradictory facts. Since the mechanism differs, the pramāṇa is genuinely distinct.

Furthermore, inference requires an already-established vyāpti relation ("wherever there is smoke, there is fire"). In the Devadatta case there is no prior rule "wherever there is a fat man who doesn't eat by day, there is night-eating." The postulation is ad hoc, not universal — which is precisely its epistemological character.

## Cross-school debate: is arthāpatti reducible to anumāna?

| school | position | argument |
|---|---|---|
| **Mīmāṃsā (Kumārila Bhaṭṭa)** | independent pramāṇa (6th) | simultaneous contradiction-resolution; no prior universal; cannot be an inference |
| **Mīmāṃsā (Prabhākara school)** | rejected — only 5 pramāṇas | treated as a sub-form of inference rather than an independent source |
| **Nyāya** | reduces to anumāna | the contradiction creates an implicit vyāpti: "a fat man who does not eat by day must eat at night" — this becomes the major premise of an inference |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga/Dharmakīrti)** | reduces to anumāna | only 2 pramāṇas; any reasoning from apparent contradiction is a form of inference — specifically svabhāvahetu or kāryahetu |
| **Jain** | not explicitly addressed in fetched sources | Jain 2-pramāṇa system (pratyakṣa/parokṣa) would subsume arthāpatti under parokṣa/anumāna |

## Relationship to mimamsa-pramana

Arthāpatti is the most contested of the Mīmāṃsā pramāṇas. The Mīmāṃsā-pramāṇa file (Batch 10) documents the full 5–6-pramāṇa Mīmāṃsā system; arthāpatti is the sixth accepted by Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (Bhāṭṭa sub-school) but dropped by Prabhākara (Prābhākara sub-school). This intra-Mīmāṃsā split is as significant as the inter-school debates.
