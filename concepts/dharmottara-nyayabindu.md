## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Nyāyabindu** ("Drop of Logic," c. 635–650 CE) is Dharmakīrti's **concise teaching manual** — a brief, sūtra-like formulation of his epistemology in three chapters, designed as an accessible entry-point to the larger *Pramāṇavārttika*. Dharmottara's **Nyāyabinduṭīkā** ("Commentary on the Drop of Logic," c. 740–800 CE) is the **only surviving Sanskrit commentary** on this text and the primary vehicle through which the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti tradition entered both Kashmiri intellectual culture and Tibetan monastic curricula.

**Three chapters of the Nyāyabindu:**

| chapter | topic | key content |
|---|---|---|
| I. Pratyakṣa | Perception | Four types (indriya/mānasa/svasaṃvedana/yogijñāna); definition: *kalpanāpoḍham abhrāntam* (NB 1.4) |
| II. Svārthānumāna | Inference for oneself | Middle-term (hetu) qualities; three types of valid reason; trairūpya |
| III. Parārthānumāna | Inference for others | Three-member syllogism; hetvābhāsa (fallacies of the middle term); pakṣābhāsa |

**The NB vs. the Pramāṇavārttika:** The PV is Dharmakīrti's magnum opus (~2000 verses, four chapters); the NB is a compressed manual covering the same ground in a fraction of the length. The NB was the preferred teaching text; the PV provided the philosophical depth. Dharmottara's ṭīkā on the NB is accordingly more explanatory and less technical than his lost PV commentary — it is the scholar's portal to the tradition.

**Dharmottara's key contributions in the ṭīkā:**
- Defines pramāṇa (valid cognition) as "capable of verification" — cognition that does not disagree with the objective reality it represents.
- Introduces *niścaya-pratyaya* (ascertaining cognition): a three-stage model (raw perception → ascertaining cognition → action) that bridges non-conceptual sensation and pragmatic knowledge. This was subsequently challenged by Prajñākaragupta.
- Interprets Dharmakīrti through a Yogācāra (mind-only) lens: the objects of cognition are ultimately internal, making external-object realism (Sautrāntika reading) a conventional rather than ultimate claim.

**The Kashmir Śaivism interface:** The Dignāga–Dharmakīrti school flourished in medieval Kashmir simultaneously with the Śaiva *Pratyabhijñā* (Recognition) school (Utpaladeva, c. 925–975 CE; Abhinavagupta, c. 975–1025 CE). The two traditions were explicit philosophical adversaries, not merely neighbours:

| issue | Buddhist (NB/Dharmottara) | Kashmir Śaivism (Pratyabhijñā) |
|---|---|---|
| **Self** | No enduring self; cognition is a momentary series of svalakṣaṇas | Self is self-luminous, continuous, universal Śiva-consciousness (cit); "recognition" presupposes a self that re-recognises |
| **Perception** | Non-conceptual (nirvikalpaka), momentary, pre-linguistic | The perceiving subject is already self-aware (*svasaṃvedana* as proof of continuous consciousness, re-read against Buddhists) |
| **Universals** | Nominal constructs (apoha — meaning by exclusion) | Real; Śiva's creative freedom (*svātantrya*) grounds universals ontologically |
| **Consciousness** | Multiple, momentary, causally linked (no over-arching mind) | One, self-luminous Śiva-consciousness; multiplicity is apparent, unity is real |

The Śaivas explicitly co-opted Dharmakīrti's apoha theory and his *svasaṃvedana* (self-cognition) as entry points — using Dharmakīrti's own arguments to argue for positions Dharmakīrti would reject. Abhinavagupta's *Īśvarapratyabhijñāvivṛtivimarśinī* engages Buddhist epistemology in direct polemic.

**The Nyāyabindu's text-critical status:** The NB survives in Sanskrit (unlike most of Dharmottara's other works, which survive only in Tibetan). Dharmottara's ṭīkā also survives in Sanskrit — making the NB/ṭīkā pair the best-preserved complete text + commentary unit of the entire hetu-vidyā tradition in Sanskrit. This accounts for its privileged position in both Kashmiri and Tibetan reception.
