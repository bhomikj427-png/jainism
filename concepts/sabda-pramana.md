## Gloss / Divergence map

Śabda-pramāṇa is **verbal testimony** as a source of valid knowledge — the question of whether spoken or written language from an authority can be an independent means of knowledge, irreducible to the other pramāṇas.

**The four traditions diverge sharply on this question:**

| tradition | position on śabda | key commitment |
|---|---|---|
| **Nyāya** | Independent 4th pramāṇa | *āptopadeśaḥ śabdaḥ* (Nyāyasūtra 1.1.7): "the utterance of a trustworthy person (*āpta*) constitutes verbal testimony." Valid only when the speaker has direct knowledge and communicates it without error or deception. God can be *āpta* for the Vedas. |
| **Mīmāṃsā** | Independent pramāṇa; Vedas are *apauruṣeya* | Vedic statements are eternally valid because they have no author (*apauruṣeyatā*) and are self-certifying (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*). They do not derive validity from any speaker's trustworthiness. This makes Vedic śabda categorically different from human testimony. |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | Not independent; reduces to inference | Dignāga collapsed śabda into anumāna: words convey meaning by exclusion (*apoha*), not by revealing objects directly. Understanding the word "fire" is an inferential act, not a direct perception. Therefore śabda is not a separate pramāṇa but a species of inference. |
| **Jain** | Parokṣa (indirect) pramāṇa via śruta | Śruta-jñāna (scriptural knowledge, TS 1.9) is the closest Jain analog: it is valid indirect cognition mediated by language and testimony, but classified as *parokṣa* (indirect), not as a separate pramāṇa class. Jain śruta requires that the testimony be traceable to the perception of an omniscient (kevalin). |

**Nyāya's definition and the āpta requirement**

Nyāyasūtra 1.1.7: *āptopadeśaḥ śabdaḥ* — śabda is the instruction/pronouncement (*upadeśa*) of a trustworthy expert (*āpta*). The Nyāya school holds that śabda is irreducible to inference because understanding the meaning of a sentence is a single, direct cognitive event — not a chain of inferential steps. However, the validity of that cognition depends on the speaker's reliability.

**Mīmāṃsā's apauruṣeyatā and the authority problem**

Mīmāṃsā argues that if the Vedas were authored (even by God), they would be fallible — dependent on the author's knowledge and honesty. By positing Vedas as *apauruṣeya* (authorless, eternal), their authority becomes non-derivable and non-contestable. This creates a pramāṇa entirely independent of any speaker's trustworthiness — the most extreme position on śabda's independence. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa's *Ślokavarttika* is the classic defense of this position.

**The Dignāga reduction and the Kumārila-Dignāga debate**

Dignāga argued: words produce understanding through the apoha (exclusion) mechanism — "cow" means "not-non-cow." This exclusion process is inferential. Therefore śabda is a subspecies of anumāna. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa explicitly opposed this, arguing that verbal understanding is immediate and unmediated, not a chain of inference (see mimamsa-pramana.md for the full Kumārila-Dignāga debate).
