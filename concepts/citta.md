## Gloss

In Buddhist philosophy, *citta* (Pali and Sanskrit) means **heart-mind** — the term covers both thought and emotion and is etymologically linked to *will* or *intention*. In the Pali Nikāyas, the Buddha used *citta* when teaching mindfulness of mind (*cittānupassanā* in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta). The Pali–English Dictionary emphasises the "heart" side — the emotional-volitional dimension — as opposed to manas (intellect).

**Citta as process, not substance**: the Abhidhamma characterises citta as a *series of momentary cittas* — momentary acts of consciousness arising and passing in rapid succession. What we call "mind" is not a thing but a causal sequence. This is directly compatible with anattā: there is no permanent self behind the citta-stream; the stream is the mind.

## Three mind-terms in Buddhist analysis

The Nikāyas use three overlapping but technically distinct terms:

| term | function | emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **citta** | "builds up" virtuous and non-virtuous states | emotional-volitional; the overall mindset |
| **manas** | calculates, examines, grasps mental objects | cognitive-intellectual |
| **vijñāna** | discriminates among sensory stimuli; provides awareness and continuity | discernment; the 6th "sense faculty" for mental objects |

In ordinary usage all three are synonymous. In Abhidhamma analysis they are technically distinct. The Yogācāra school (Vasubandhu, Asaṅga) adds a fourth: **ālaya-vijñāna** (storehouse consciousness) — a functional continuity mechanism that holds karmic seeds and solves the rebirth-without-self paradox, while still denying a permanent ātman.

## Abhidhamma classification

Theravāda Abhidhamma enumerates **89 types of citta** (or 121 with finer differentiation):
- 81 mundane (*lokiya*) cittas — within samsāra
- 8 supramundane (*lokuttara*) cittas — the four path-moments and four fruition-moments of liberation

Consciousness arises in series of **17 mind-moments** governed by 24 causal relations. Each moment arises, peaks, and passes — there is no "background" consciousness persisting between moments.

## Cross-tradition comparison: what kind of thing is citta?

| feature | Buddhist citta | Sāṃkhya-Yoga citta | Jain upayoga |
|---|---|---|---|
| material or conscious | **conscious process** — citta itself is awareness | **material** — citta is part of *prakṛti* (antahkarana); consciousness (puruṣa) is separate | activity of a conscious substance — the jīva's mode of knowing/seeing |
| substance or process | processual (series of momentary acts) | a faculty-instrument (stable evolving from *ahamkāra*) | mode of a substance (jīva's attribute, not a separate thing) |
| permanent or changing | impermanent (moment-to-moment arising/passing) | evolves with prakṛti; relatively stable | impermanent modes of a permanent jīva-substance |
| independent of a self | no self behind it — compatible with anattā | depends on puruṣa's light to be illuminated | belongs to jīva (inseparable from soul-substance) |
| liberation implication | citta ceases grasping; stream stops (nirvāṇa) | puruṣa recognises it is *not* the citta (viveka) | jīva's citta expands to omniscience (kevala-jñāna) |

**Critical distinction — Sāṃkhya citta**: In Sāṃkhya, *citta* is **material** (evolute of *prakṛti*), not inherently conscious. Consciousness is puruṣa — a separate, passive witness. Citta is illuminated *by* puruṣa like a mirror reflects light; it is not itself luminous. This is a direct inversion of the Buddhist account: Buddhist citta *is* the consciousness process; Sāṃkhya citta is a material cognitive apparatus that receives the appearance of consciousness from outside.

**Critical distinction — Jain upayoga**: Upayoga is the jīva's own mode of consciousness-activity — intrinsic to the soul-substance, not a separate process. There is no citta-stream independent of a subject; the jīva *is* the subject and upayoga is its characteristic operation. Buddhist citta involves no underlying substance; Jain upayoga is the activity of one.

**The Yogācāra ālaya-vijñāna** is often conflated with a permanent self. It is not: ālaya-vijñāna is a functional stream that stores karmic seeds and provides experiential continuity — but it too is impermanent and conditioned. It is the most sophisticated Buddhist attempt to explain rebirth without a permanent self, and it does not restore the ātman that anattā denies.
