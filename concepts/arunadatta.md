## Gloss / Divergence map

**Aruṇadatta**, **c. 1220 CE**, son of **Mṛgāṅkadatta**, is the author of the ***Sarvāṅgasundarā***, the principal commentary on [[vagbhata|Vāgbhaṭa]]'s *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* — the gloss that "explained nearly each and every word" of its base text, and the one printed alongside the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* in the standard editions (Paradkar's, with [[hemadri|Hemādri]]'s shorter *Āyurvedarasāyana* beside it).

With him this corpus finally has a commentator for **each of the three saṃhitās**: [[cakrapanidatta|Cakrapāṇidatta]] on Caraka, [[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]] on Suśruta, Aruṇadatta on Vāgbhaṭa.

### The finding: he is dated by having been contradicted about the eye

[[dalhana]] set out three ways a pre-modern Indian figure gets dated — regnal anchor, lineage arithmetic, citation-bracketing — and argued that the precision available depends on **what kind of institution the man stood next to**. Aruṇadatta adds a fourth, and it is the strangest of them. P. K. Gode's reasoning, in full:

1. **Vijayarakṣita "controverts a certain doctrine of Aruṇadatta regarding the structure of the eye."**
2. Vijayarakṣita quotes **Guṇākara (1239 A.D.)**.
3. Therefore Vijayarakṣita ≈ 1240 A.D.
4. Therefore "**Aruṇadatta flourished about 1220 A. D.**"

So the floruit rests on a **disagreement about ocular anatomy**. Not on a patron, not on a lineage, not even on being quoted approvingly — on being *wrong*, in someone else's opinion, in a datable book. Extend [[dalhana]]'s table:

| figure | how the date is fixed | what it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| [[cakrapanidatta]] | regnal anchor | an external institution's records |
| [[sarvajnatman]] | lineage arithmetic | the tradition's account of itself |
| [[dalhana]] | citation-bracketing | that quotation runs one way in time |
| **Aruṇadatta** | **refutation-bracketing** | that *disagreement* also runs one way in time — you cannot controvert a man who has not written yet |

The fourth is the third's mirror image, and it is worth naming separately because of what it implies about the sources. A tradition that preserves its **quarrels** can date itself from them. Aruṇadatta is legible to us at all because a scholar two decades later thought he had the eye wrong and said so in a book that cites a dated authority. **Polemic is chronology.**

### The namesake problem — and a live conflation the corpus should not repeat

Popular accounts give Aruṇadatta a polymath's bibliography: besides the *Sarvāṅgasundarā*, "he wrote a commentary on Suśruta Saṃhitā also and composed another work entitled '*Manuṣyālaya Candrikā*' dealing with geology and architecture." Gode says otherwise, and says it directly:

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **One** Aruṇadatta — physician, Suśruta commentator, and author of an architecture treatise | web summaries of the Āyurvedic secondary literature | a 13th-century polymath spanning medicine and *vāstu* |
| **Three** men of the name — "three persons of the same name viz. Aruṇadatta, wrote on four different subjects viz. Medicine, Lexicography, grammar and architecture" | **P. K. Gode**, *Studies in Indian Literary History* | that the medical Aruṇadatta wrote the *Sarvāṅgasundarā*, and the architecture and lexicography belong to other men |

This corpus takes Gode's reading, on provenance: a scholar working from manuscripts and colophons, who **states the namesake problem as his finding**, outranks summaries that silently merge the entries. The merged biography is the same error-shape already catalogued at [[amalananda]] (a commentary slid onto the wrong author) and [[citsukha]] (a lexicon entry naming a different man entirely) — and it is *why* those cases were recorded. **The polymath biography is not asserted here.** What Aruṇadatta wrote, on the strongest source, is the *Sarvāṅgasundarā*.

### Where his doctrine already sits in this corpus

[[cakrapanidatta]] records the four *dhātu-poṣaṇa nyāya*s — the models of how bodily tissues are nourished — and Aruṇadatta is already in that table, holding ***eka-kāla dhātu-poṣaṇa***: all *dhātus* fed **at once**, *rasa* distributed by *vyāna-vāyu* with the heart's action. The other three are Suśruta's *kedārī-kulyā* (sequential irrigation), Caraka-with-Cakrapāṇi's *khale-kapota* (selective), and the transformational model.

That is worth restating as a claim about **who owns Āyurvedic theory**: the scheme is assembled *across* the commentarial traditions of all three saṃhitās, and Aruṇadatta's contribution is the one that makes nourishment **simultaneous and circulation-driven** rather than sequential. He is a commentator advancing a physiological position that his base text did not fix — which is the same thing [[dalhana]] was found doing when he made *mala* a *śalya*. Commentators in this tradition legislate.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Three+ sources, tested for independence** — **P. K. Gode**, "Vāgbhaṭa, the author of the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*, and his Commentators," in *Studies in Indian Literary History* (the dominant signal: a manuscript-based literary historian, giving his dating chain step by step rather than asserting a date); **web summaries of the Āyurvedic secondary literature**, which supply the *Sarvāṅgasundarā*'s character and the polymath biography Gode contradicts; and the **corpus's own peer-reviewed source** at [[cakrapanidatta]] (Sharma et al., *IJRAR* 11:1, 2024) for the *eka-kāla* attribution, which was gathered for another node entirely and is therefore genuinely independent of everything fetched here.
2. **Commentarial tradition** — the anchor, doubled: he *is* a commentator, and he is himself commented on and quoted by [[hemadri|Hemādri]] a generation later, which is what closes his date from above.
3. **Lexicon + morphology** — *sarvāṅga-sundarā*, "beautiful in every limb," which is a pun on the *aṣṭāṅga* ("eight-limbed") of the base text's title: a commentary claiming to be sound in each of the eight branches its root text divides medicine into. The title is an argument.
4. **Internal consistency** — swept `concepts/`: he was **already cited in this corpus** at [[cakrapanidatta]] as an *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* commentator holding a named physiological position, without a node. This node does not introduce him; it gives an existing citation somewhere to point.
5. **Provenance** — sharply uneven, and the node is built to reflect that. Gode is a critical literary historian and is followed wherever he speaks; the popular layer is used only for what it is competent to report (the commentary's style, the standard editions) and is **explicitly overruled** on the biography. The one peer-reviewed Āyurvedic item located directly — Journal of Indian Medical Heritage 27:1 (1997), "Aruṇadatta and Hemādri, scholiasts of Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya," PMID 12575692 — is **paywalled and unread**, and is named below as the upgrade path.

**CONVERGED** on date (c. 1220), father, principal work and its character; the sources agree and Gode supplies the reasoning rather than the assertion. Confidence **medium**: the *Sarvāṅgasundarā* itself was **not read**; the one directly relevant peer-reviewed paper is paywalled; and the **bibliography is genuinely split** — a divergence mapped above rather than resolved, since Gode's three-namesakes finding is single-sourced even though it is much the better-provenanced side.
