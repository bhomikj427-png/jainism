## Gloss / Divergence map

**Yāmunācārya**, called **Āḷavandār**, is the **earliest Śrī Vaiṣṇava ācārya whose philosophical works survive** — grandson of Nāthamuni, second of the three great ācāryas, and the man whose unfinished programme [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]] carried out. The corpus has been leaning on him since `gita.md` was written: the **three-hexad division of the Bhagavadgītā** attributed there to "Yāmunācārya" is his *Gītārthasaṅgraha*.

This node is **contested** on one thing only — **his relation to Rāmānuja**, which three sources describe three incompatible ways — and it carries a second finding about **his dates** that is arithmetic rather than testimony.

### Divergence — what was he to Rāmānuja?

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **His preceptor.** Yāmuna "became preceptor to Rāmānuja" | Wikipedia, "Yamunacharya" | A direct teacher–pupil transmission |
| **His teacher's teacher** — Rāmānuja's *guru's* guru | sreenivasaraos.com (aggregator) | One remove: doctrine reaching Rāmānuja through Mahāpūrṇa or another intermediary |
| **Neither — they never met.** Rāmānuja reached Śrīraṅgam to find him dead; the designation was posthumous, by sign | IEP (Ranganathan); *Encyclopedia of Religion* (Carman); the Śrī Vaiṣṇava tradition itself | That the chain's central link transmits **authority without content** — see `ramanuja.md` |

The third is the best attested and is what the tradition itself says. The first two are not different *evidence*; they are the ordinary compression by which "his successor in the lineage" becomes "his student." **Recorded because the compression is invisible once made** — and because the whole interest of the Śrī Vaiṣṇava succession lies in the fact that it is *not* a normal teacher-to-pupil handoff.

Wikipedia's own account is internally consistent with the third reading and undercuts its own summary line: it reports that before dying Yāmuna **expressed three unfulfilled wishes** — among them that commentaries be composed on the major texts — and that Raṅganātha instructed him to send for Rāmānuja from Kāñcīpuram. A dying man's programme, handed on. The *Śrī Bhāṣya* is the discharge of a wish, not the completion of a course of study.

### The dates, and an arithmetic observation

Sources give **918–1038** (Wikipedia), **918–1036**, and **916–1041**. All cluster; none argues for its endpoints.

⚠ **918–1038 is exactly 120 years.** So is the traditional dating of Rāmānuja — **1017–1137** — already flagged in `ramanuja.md` as "a schematic number, not an observation." **Two consecutive ācāryas of one lineage, each assigned precisely 120 years**, is not plausibly two independent observations; 120 years is the conventional ideal full human span. This file therefore treats the *precision* of these dates as **hagiographical convention**, while accepting the **10th–11th century** placement, which is independently constrained by his grandfather Nāthamuni and by Rāmānuja after him.

⚠ **Strengthened later in the same batch.** Writing [[nathamuni]] added the third data point and turned the observation into a pattern: Nāthamuni is conventionally **823–951 — 128 years**, with an alternative dating of 582–922 that Wikipedia itself calls implausible at "400+ years." A further source gives Yāmuna as **917–1042**, a fourth span (125 years). **All three ācāryas of the *munitraya*, consecutively, are assigned lifespans no human reaches.** That is a convention, not three observations.

This is not a new argument — it is Sydnor's argument about Rāmānuja, applied to the whole lineage, and it **strengthens** his case: a lineage that hands out 120-year lifespans is not a lineage keeping records.

### The works

| work | what it is |
|---|---|
| ***Āgamaprāmāṇya*** | the major polemic: that the **Pāñcarātra Āgamas carry Vedic-grade authority**, against Mīmāṃsaka and Advaitin denial |
| ***Siddhitraya*** | three treatises — ***Ātmasiddhi*** (the finite self), ***Īśvarasiddhi*** (God), ***Saṃvitsiddhi*** (knowledge/empirical cognition) |
| ***Gītārthasaṅgraha*** | 32 verses summarising the Gītā; source of the **three-hexad** division used in `gita.md` |
| ***Stotraratna*** | 65 verses to Nārāyaṇa |
| ***Catuḥślokī*** | four verses to Lakṣmī — see [[lakshmi]], where Śrī Vaiṣṇavism's elevation of the goddess to necessary mediator is recorded |
| ***Mahāpuruṣanirṇaya*** | on Lakṣmī–Nārāyaṇa; **lost** |
| ***Nityam*** | manual for daily worship |

Note that *Ātmasiddhi* opens with **a list of earlier Vedāntins** — "Ācārya Ṭaṅka, Bhartṛprapañca, Bhartṛmitra, Bhartṛhari, Brahmadatta" — which van Buitenen discusses in his edition of the *Vedārthasaṅgraha*. This is a **pre-Rāmānuja witness list to a Vedānta that mostly does not survive**, and it is the single most valuable thing in the node for future work.

### What the *Āgamaprāmāṇya* actually argues — and a warning about how to read it

The treatise answers a Mīmāṃsaka challenge stated in strict pramāṇa terms: verbal testimony (*śabda*) is valid **independently** only if authorless; testimony "formulated by a person" is valid only where some other pramāṇa has already established the fact. Since perception cannot establish that Pāñcarātra consecration and worship yield *mokṣa*, and no person of "superhuman sensibility" can be shown to have perceived it, the Pāñcarātra is left without warrant. A second thrust runs by inference: "Pāñcarātra Śāstra is non-authoritative, because it is **non-Vedic, like Buddhism**." Yāmuna's reply attacks the inference at its definition — what *is* the non-authoritativeness being proved? If it means the text produces no knowledge, that is refuted by perception, since a competent student plainly does understand its sentences.

⚠ **A specimen of how a quotation can invert if lifted from its dialectical position.** Early in the work stands this, which reads like a striking rejection of caste essentialism:

> "We do not regard Brahmins as a distinct species, different from the species man"

It is **not Yāmuna's own thesis, and its purpose is restrictive**. The sentence sits inside an Objection/Refutation exchange in which the speaker is arguing **against** Bhāgavata authority: the Objection proposes that Bhāgavatas are exemplary *because* they are Brahmins; this Refutation denies that brāhmaṇatva is a perceptible natural kind — tuft and sacred thread prove nothing, being "worn illegally by blackguards" — and concludes that the only criterion is **settled usage**, which does not call Bhāgavatas Brahmins. It then cites Manu to class *Sātvata* as a low-caste term. A nominalist premise driving an exclusionary conclusion.

Quoted alone, it makes a 10th-century Vaiṣṇava sound like a critic of caste; read in position, it is the **objection Yāmuna's book exists to defeat**. Logged as a method specimen alongside `shrikantha.md` (a title read as a thesis) and `madhva.md` (one charge read as two sources).

⚠ **Text caveat:** the OCR of the scanned volume has **page-order disturbances** — §§10–11 reappear after §15. Passages were located by the translation's own section numbers, and the dialectical attribution above rests on the printed "Objection / Refutation" labels, not on inference from content.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — the primary source, read directly in published English translation.** *Complete Works of Yāmunācārya (English)*, Internet Archive item `yamunacharya`, containing the *Āgamaprāmāṇya* in English and **Śrī Yāmunācārya's *Siddhitraya*, with the Sanskrit commentary *Gūḍhaprakāśa* of Uttamūr T. Vīrarāghavācārya, English introduction by R. Ramanujachari, translation by R. Ramanujachari and K. Srinivasachariar, Ubhaya Vedanta Granthamala Book Trust, Madras, 1972** — a **critical edition with commentary and scholarly apparatus**, the strongest provenance available here. Supplies the pramāṇa argument, the non-Vedic-like-Buddhism inference and its answer, the brāhmaṇatva passage in its dialectical position, the *Ātmasiddhi* list of earlier Vedāntins, and the analytical outline of the three siddhis. **Signal 2 — Wikipedia, "Yamunacharya"**: dates 918–1038; the Āḷavandār etymology (the Pāṇḍya court scholar Akkiyāḻvāṉ's sarcastic "*Āḷavandhārā?*" — "has he come to rule me?" — turned into a title by the queen); the works with their verse-counts; the three dying wishes; Nāthamuni as grandfather; and its own citations to **Dasgupta (1940), S. M. Srinivasa Chari (1994), van Buitenen (1971) and Constance Jones (2007)**. **Signal 3 — a search-level aggregation** (sreenivasaraos.com and others): dates 918–1036, "Rāmānuja's guru's guru," and the *Āgamaprāmāṇya* as answering "the Mīmāṃsakas as well as the Advaitins." **Signal 4 — corpus-internal**: `gita.md` independently records the three-hexad division under his name; `ramanuja.md` and its IEP/Carman sources supply the never-met account.

**Independence.** Signal 1 is a **different order of evidence** from the rest — a 1972 critical edition read at first hand, not a web summary — and it is what lets this node describe the argument rather than the reputation. Signals 2 and 3 are weakly independent web sources that agree because they descend from the same handbook tradition; where they conflict with signal 1 or with `ramanuja.md`'s IEP/Carman pair, they lose. Signal 4 is a genuine internal check, written batches earlier without this node in view.

**Why contested.** The Rāmānuja relation splits three ways across sources of unequal weight, and the dates carry a structural defect (the 120-year figure) rather than a disagreement. **Why medium, not high**: the primary text was read in **translation only** and in **OCR with known page disorder**; no Sanskrit was consulted; van Buitenen's own 1971 edition of the *Āgamaprāmāṇya* was not obtained; and no source found argues for the dates rather than repeating them. **Not low** because the load-bearing claims come from a critical edition.
