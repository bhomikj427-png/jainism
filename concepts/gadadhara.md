## Gloss / Divergence map

**Gadādhara Bhaṭṭācārya** (17th century; writing **c. 1640–60**) is the last great systematiser of [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]] and the point at which the school's difficulty becomes a fact about the school. He wrote the ***Gādādharī***, a commentary not on [[gangesha|Gaṅgeśa]] but on [[raghunatha-siromani|Raghunātha]]'s *Dīdhiti* — **the third storey of the pyramid** — and his commentaries became so indispensable that they displaced their own predecessors: "Gadādhar's contributions **overshadowed** those of the earlier Nyāya scholars Jagadīśa Tarkālaṅkāra and Bhavānanda."

The most economical measure of what he is: **the standard beginners' manual of the whole tradition, Annaṃbhaṭṭa's *Tarkasaṅgraha*, was nicknamed *Bāla-gādādharī*** — "a sort of 'Beginner's Guide to Gadādhara'." A school whose introductory textbook is named after a third-tier commentator has made its own most recent master the thing one is introduced *to*.

### The works, and a number to hold loosely

- ***Gādādharī*** — on Raghunātha's *Dīdhiti*. Verified edition: Chowkhamba Sanskrit Series 42, Benares, **1913–1927**, printed together with the *Tattvacintāmaṇi* and the *Dīdhiti*.
- Banglapedia names his three most significant commentaries on Raghunātha as the ***Anumānadīdhitiṭīkā***, ***Nañvādavyākhyā*** and ***Bauddhādhikāradīdhitiṭīkā***, plus recovered commentaries on the *Tattvacintāmaṇi* and a *Kusumāñjaliṭīkā* (i.e. on [[udayana|Udayana]]'s *Nyāyakusumāñjali*).
- ***Vyutpattivāda*** — on *śabda*: **sentence**-meaning, and the role of *saṃsargamaryādā* in understanding it.
- ***Śaktivāda*** — on **word**-meaning: *pada*, *padārtha*, and *padavṛtti*, signification. Described as "the most fundamental work of Navya-nyāya logic on the theories of word, word-meaning, and word-relation."
- Also extant: *Muktivāda*, *Viṣayatāvāda*, *Vidhisvarūpa*.

⚠ **"He is believed to have written 64 more books on logic."** Reported by two sources. **Recorded as a tradition, not a count** — no source consulted lists them, and round-ish totals attached to prolific scholiasts are a genre convention. The *extant* works are the five or six named above.

⚠ **Note where his two independent treatises point: both are about language.** The *Vyutpattivāda* and *Śaktivāda* are on word- and sentence-meaning, not on inference. The school that narrowed for three centuries onto the *vyāpti* chapter produced, at its end, its most sustained original work on **semantics**. That is a shape worth recording, and it is consistent with [[navya-nyaya|the school's]] known export into grammar and poetics.

### ⭐ The divergence: a dating synchronism this node falsifies

**This is the finding, and it lands on a node the corpus wrote in the previous batch.**

`madhusudana-sarasvati.md` (Batch 45) adopted V. Rajagopalan's dating of Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, praising it as "**the only source consulted that argues for a date rather than stating one**." Rajagopalan fixes him by **four synchronisms**, and the third is: **"Gadādhara the logician was his contemporary."**

| the claim | the problem |
|---|---|
| Gadādhara was Madhusūdana's contemporary (Rajagopalan) | Madhusūdana is placed by that same argument at **the beginning of the 16th century**; Wikipedia gives **c. 1490–1580** |
| Gadādhara's date | **17th century**, writing **c. 1640–60** (Banglapedia); **c. 1650** (Hindupedia); 17th c. (search-level); and his own teacher **Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa** is placed **c. 1625** |

**The two cannot both stand. The gap is roughly 140 years.**

And the source of the synchronism appears to be identifiable. An Advaita-tradition site quotes a verse to the effect that **when Madhusūdana came to Navadvīpa, Mathurānātha Tarkavāgīśa trembled and Gadādhara became afraid** — and, to its credit, "presents this as quoted **praise poetry rather than historical record**." **A panegyric boasting that a champion frightened the great logicians of the day is a claim about his stature, not a chronological datum**; it is the same genre as the *digvijaya* stories this corpus discounts in `shankara.md` and `appayya-dikshita.md`.

⚠ **The same problem hits the teacher-list.** `madhusudana-sarasvati.md` also records (from Wikipedia) that Madhusūdana trained at Nabadwīp "under **Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa and Mathurānātha Tarkavāgīśa**." But Harirāma (c. 1625, author of a *Maṅgalavāda*) is **Gadādhara's** teacher, and Raghudeva's. **The whole Navya-Nyāya side of Madhusūdana's biography attaches him to a cluster of Navadvīpa names that belong a century after his adopted date.**

⚠⚠ **What this file does and does not conclude.**
- It **does** conclude that Rajagopalan's third synchronism is worthless as evidence, and that `madhusudana-sarasvati.md` must be amended to say so.
- It **does not** overturn that file's conclusion. The other three synchronisms are untouched, and the strongest of them — Śeṣagovinda studying with [[appayya-dikshita|Appayya Dīkṣita]], whose dates rest on a **dated 1580 inscription** — is external, epigraphic, and independent of the Navadvīpa material entirely.
- It **does** note, and refuse to resolve, an uncomfortable consequence: the outlier dating that file **argued against**, *c.* **1540–1647**, is the one that would make the Navadvīpa teacher-links chronologically possible. The inscription argument tells against it; the Nabadwīp biography tells for it. **Recorded as an open tension, not decided.** A batch that discovers a problem in its own earlier work should say so and stop there, not manufacture a resolution.

### Life

17th century. Born at **Lakṣmīcāpḍā village, Bogra district** (now Bangladesh). His father **Jīvācārya** was "a follower of tantricism." His teacher in Nyāya was **Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa** (c. 1625), who also taught **Raghudeva Bhaṭṭācārya**. He is styled ***mahāmahopādhyāya*** in the Sanskrit sources.

⚠ **Almost everything here comes from one regional encyclopedia** (Banglapedia). It is a serious and locally well-informed source, and its independence from the Sanskritist literature is exactly what makes it useful — but the biographical detail is **single-sourced** and is recorded as such.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Banglapedia, "Bhattacharya, Gadadhar."** The load-bearing biographical source, and independent of the Anglophone Sanskritist tradition: 17th c., writing 1640–60; Lakṣmīcāpḍā, Bogra; the father Jīvācārya; **Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa** as teacher; the three principal commentaries on Raghunātha by name; the *Kusumāñjaliṭīkā*; the "64 more books" tradition; and the judgement that he "overshadowed" Jagadīśa and Bhavānanda.

**Signal 2 — Jonardon Ganeri, SEP, "Analytic Philosophy in Early Modern India"** (= the article read here as the Columbia PDF *Navya-Nyāya*; see the ⚠ below). Places him with Raghunātha and Jagadīśa at the school's "heights," and supplies the *Bāla-gādādharī* nickname and what it implies.

**Signal 3 — B. K. Matilal, *The Navya-Nyāya Doctrine of Negation*, 1968, abbreviations list, read at first hand.** The verified edition datum: **TCDG**, *Gādādharī*, "a commentary on **TCD**" — i.e. on Raghunātha's *Dīdhiti*, **not** on Gaṅgeśa — ed. Bamacharan Bhattacharyya and others, Chowkhamba Sanskrit Series 42, Benares, 1913–1927.

**Signal 4 — search-level aggregation** on the *Vyutpattivāda* and *Śaktivāda*: the sentence-meaning/word-meaning division of labour, *saṃsargamaryādā*, *pada*/*padārtha*/*padavṛtti*; the extant-works list; Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa c. 1625 with his *Maṅgalavāda* and his two pupils; and the Navadvīpa verse.

**Signal 5 — advaita-vedanta.org, "Introduction to Advaita Siddhi."** An **Advaita-partisan** source, and it is cited here for something that cuts *against* its own tradition's hagiography: it quotes the trembling-logicians verse and treats it as praise poetry rather than record. A source conceding a point against interest is worth more than one asserting a point for it.

**Signal 6 — Hindupedia** (Gādādhara Bhaṭṭācārya, A.D. 1650) and a Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika historical study's **Appendix B**, which censuses **62 named Navya-Nyāya authors** and includes him. ⚠ The appendix is marked **not proofread**.

**Signal 7 — corpus-internal, and the reason this node matters beyond itself**: `madhusudana-sarasvati.md` (Rajagopalan's four synchronisms, and the Nabadwīp teacher-list), `appayya-dikshita.md` (the 1580 inscription that anchors the surviving argument), `raghunatha-siromani.md`, `tattvacintamani.md` (the commentary tiers).

⚠ **An independence correction that applies to this whole batch, discovered here.** The article cited throughout Batch 46 as *"Ganeri, Navya-Nyāya: Analytical Philosophy in Early Modern India"* (the Columbia/Pollock PDF) **is the Stanford Encyclopedia entry** now titled *"Analytic Philosophy in Early Modern India."* Fetching the live SEP entry returned the same sentences — the 11th-or-12th-century merger, the "Raghunātha, Jagadīśa and Gadādhara" formula, the completion-relation passage, *Bāla-gādādharī*. **They are one source at two addresses and must never be counted as two.** (This does **not** affect SEP's *"Gaṅgeśa"* entry, which is a **different** entry by a **different** author and remains genuinely independent.) The identification also *raises* the source's standing: it is a peer-reviewed encyclopedia article by a named specialist, not an unrefereed preprint.

**Independence.** Signals 1, 2, 3 and 5 are four different kinds of source — a Bengali regional encyclopedia, a philosophical encyclopedia entry, a Harvard monograph's bibliography, and a devotional-tradition website — with no visible dependence, and they agree on everything they overlap on. The *falsification* in this file rests on signal 1's date against signal 7's corpus record, with signal 4 supplying the independent Harirāma date and signal 5 the genre-identification of the verse. **Four routes to the same conclusion.**

**Why contested.** Not because his own dates are disputed — they are not, and 17th century / c. 1640–60 is agreed — but because **placing him falsifies a synchronism the corpus had already accepted and built on**, and because the resulting tension in `madhusudana-sarasvati.md` is left open rather than resolved. **Why medium.** Four independent sources, one of them read at first hand, and a verified edition. **Not high**: the biography is essentially single-sourced; **none of his works was read**, in Sanskrit or translation, though translations exist (see below); the "64 books" is a tradition, not a count; and the Devanāgarī, though verified, comes from a Sanskrit Wikipedia article rather than an edition's title page.
