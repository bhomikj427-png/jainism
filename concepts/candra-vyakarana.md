## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Cāndra-vyākaraṇa** (*Cāndrasūtra*) is the **Buddhist** non-Pāṇinian grammar of [[candragomin|Candragomin]], c. 450 CE, composed in Bengal — "the first great revised edition of the [[astadhyayi|Aṣṭādhyāyī]], embodying all the suggestions and corrections of [[katyayana|Kātyāyana]] and [[patanjali|Patañjali]]." Abhyankar: its author is "a famous Buddhist Sanskrit grammarian whose grammar, existing in the **Tibetan script**, is now available in the Devanāgarī script." It comes with the author's own ***Cāndravṛtti***.

⚠ **Contested, and the most consequential disagreement is one this corpus walked into and has had to correct itself over:**

| point | reading | who holds it |
|---|---|---|

| | ⭐⭐ the work had **eight**: chapters **7 (Vedic rules) and 8 (svara/pitch accent)**, placed **at the end** rather than distributed through the work as Pāṇini does — and they were **lost**. ## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐⭐ The correction: a transmission accident was being read as an authorial choice — by the tradition's leading lexicographer, and then by this corpus, one node ago.**

[[katantra]] was committed earlier in this same batch on the strength of Abhyankar's preface: "**The special feature of all these grammars was that they entirely omitted the Vedic peculiarities and accents**" — and the corpus built a ⭐⭐ finding on it, that what makes a grammar "a different system" is that it stops being a **vedāṅga**. **Timalsina falsifies the strong form of that for the Cāndra.** The Cāndravyākaraṇa did **not** omit the Veda. It **segregated** the Vedic and accent rules into two terminal chapters — "as the author placed these at the end of his work rather than following Pāṇini's practice of distributing them throughout" — and **those two chapters were subsequently lost.** Abhyankar's "six chapters… no section on Vedic Grammar and accents" is an accurate description of the **received** text and a false one about the **composed** text.

⭐ **And the architecture is the story.** Pāṇini interleaves Vedic rules through the Aṣṭādhyāyī; Candragomin collects them at the back. **Once collected, they are detachable — and they detached.** ⚠ The corpus does **not** claim the loss was caused by the arrangement, still less that it was intended: manuscripts lose their last leaves for entirely mechanical reasons, and that is if anything the *better* explanation. What is established is that the two facts are the same fact seen twice — the organisational choice that made the grammar teachable as classical Sanskrit is the choice that made its Vedic content separable. ⭐ **This is [[astadhyayi]]'s Batch 47 lesson in a new literature: the mechanism is not corrupting, it is invisible.** Here it was invisible to Abhyankar, and then to this corpus for exactly two committed nodes.

**⭐ What survives of the generalisation, stated precisely.** [[katantra]] has been **corrected in place**, and the surviving claim is weaker and better:

> Every named post-Pāṇinian system, **as received**, lacks Vedic and accentual material. In at least one case that is demonstrably a **transmission loss and not an authorial choice**, and the corpus therefore does not know, for any of the others, which it is.

⚠ **The Kātantra is specifically *not* known to have had Vedic chapters** — no source consulted says it did, and Abhyankar's *Sarvavarman* entry has the omission as deliberate and motivated ("with a view to making his grammar useful for beginners"). ⭐ **So the corrected position is not that Abhyankar was wrong, but that his class-level generalisation was assembled from received texts and cannot bear the weight of an authorial claim.** That is the [[vararuci]] defect a third time: **a reference work's general statement not checked against what its own entries would license.**

**⭐ Batch 47's flagged contradiction: narrowed to near-closure, and the corpus can now say why it arose.** [[mahabhashya]] records, as an explicit open question, that the secondary literature "flatly contradicts itself" on Candrācārya — blamed for the Mahābhāṣya's neglect, or credited with its rescue. **Two things now bear on it, and neither existed in Batch 47:**

1. **Abhyankar gives the address at first hand: *Vākyapadīya* II.489** is where Bhartṛhari refers to Candra.
2. The blame-verse is a **different verse with different names in it**: at **VP 2.484** Bhartṛhari criticises **Vaiji, Saubhava and Haryakṣa** for pursuing *śuṣka-tarka* — "dry logic" — and ignoring the rules of Pāṇini and Patañjali. **Candrācārya is not in that list.** Five verses later he is named in the other connection: recovering the Mahābhāṣya tradition **in Kashmir**, on a tradition that **Kalhaṇa** independently records in the twelfth century.

⭐ **The contradiction is very probably not a scholarly dispute at all but a single secondary writer attaching Candrācārya to the blame-list five verses too early.** ⚠ **Narrowed, NOT closed**, and the distinction matters: the verse *numbers* are first-hand from Abhyankar, but **neither VP 2.484 nor VP 2.489 has been read** in an edition or translation, and the 2.484 content reaches this node at search-summary level from pages of the **same weak provenance that generated the problem**. **The corpus does not resolve a weak source with itself.** ⭐ What *is* now solid and was not before: the Kashmir localisation, corroborated by Kalhaṇa — a Kashmiri chronicler — and consistent with Abhyankar's independent note that the *Cāndra* survived in **Tibetan** script, i.e. through the northern Buddhist transmission. Carried as a **sharpened** follow-up: read VP II.481–490 in Rau's or Aklujkar's edition.

### ✅✅ CLOSED IN BATCH 51 — the passage was read (depth read D-C), and the hypothesis above is **confirmed and corrected in the same move**

*(Amended, not rewritten: the Batch-49 reasoning is left standing above so the correction and its reason both stay visible. The Sanskrit was read at first hand in the **GRETIL** e-text of the *Vākyapadīya*, alongside **Ashok Aklujkar**'s article on this very verse. ⚠ The two translation routes the corpus had queued — Iyer's English kāṇḍa II on archive.org, twice — both returned **garbled Devanāgarī OCR with no Latin**; see Sources.)*

**(a) The hypothesis holds.** They are two different verses naming two different sets of people, and Cāndrācārya is in the second:
> *vaijisaubhavaharyakṣaiḥ **śuṣkatarkānusāribhiḥ*** — *ārṣe viplāvite granthe saṃgrahapratikañcuke*
> *parvatād āgamaṃ labdhvā **bhāṣyabījānusāribhiḥ*** — *sa nīto bahuśākhatvaṃ cāndrācāryādibhiḥ punaḥ*

**(b) ⭐⭐ But "five verses later" was wrong, and *how* it was wrong is the finding.** On any one scale the two verses are **two apart, not five**: the GRETIL e-text numbers them **2.483** and **2.485**; **Aklujkar** numbers the same two **2.484** and **2.486**; **Abhyankar's II.489 is a third scale again.** ⚠⚠ **The corpus subtracted a verse-number taken from one edition from a verse-number taken from another and reported the difference as a fact about the text.** The verse itself is stable — every witness quotes the same two lines — and **its number is not**. ⭐ **A citation that named its edition would have caught this; one that named only a number could not.** *(The mechanism is visible in the e-text's own apparatus: it carries a verse marked "this verse is only in Rau" at 2.329 — one editorial decision about one doubtful verse re-numbers everything after it.)*

**(c) ⭐⭐⭐ And the text supplies the positive reason the corpus had guessed at and could not supply.** The two verses are built as a **matched pair of compounds**: the blamed are ***śuṣka-tarkānusāribhiḥ***, and the restorers are ***bhāṣya-bījānusāribhiḥ*** — same ending, same case, two verses apart. ⭐ **The epilogue is not a list a name strayed into; it is a deliberate antithesis**, and a reader who takes it as one list collapses exactly the contrast it was built to make. **That is a better account of the secondary error than "five verses too early," and only the text could have supplied it.**

⚠⚠ **And a cost, recorded because reading the source produced it.** **Aklujkar argues that the ten epilogue verses (2.481–490) are not Bhartṛhari's at all, but a pupil's** — "the ten verses were not written by Bhartṛhari but by a student of his." So *"Bhartṛhari credits Candrācārya"* is now contested **at the level of who wrote the sentence**, a question the corpus did not have while it worked from verse-numbers alone. ⚠ Two further variants are **flagged and not adjudicated**: the e-text reads **`cāndrācārya`** (the vṛddhi form — "the teacher *of the Cāndra*"), where Aklujkar quotes **`candrācārya`**; and the verse says **`-ādibhiḥ`, "and others," in the plural**, whereas the Tibetan account Aklujkar cites has Candragomin come upon the Mahābhāṣya **alone**.

**⭐ It absorbs the commentaries into the sūtras — which is the opposite of what the Pāṇinian tradition did.** LinguIndic: the Cāndrasūtra is "believed to be based on Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī, with changes such as the **incorporation of a vārttika of Kātyāyana as a sūtra**"; the search-level account generalises this — "embodying all the suggestions and corrections of Kātyāyana and Patañjali." ⭐⭐ **Set that beside [[kaiyata]]'s ladder.** The Pāṇinian tradition's answer to nine centuries of accumulated correction was a **hierarchy of authority** over a fixed root text — sūtra, vārttika, bhāṣya, each tier commenting the last, with a maxim (*yathottaraṃ munitrayasya prāmāṇyam*) to rank them. Candragomin's answer was to **rewrite the root text** so the corrections are in it. ⚠ **The corpus asserts no causal or polemical relation** — no source consulted says Candragomin was reacting against the commentarial method — but the two responses to the same problem are worth holding side by side, and the second one is the one the corpus had never seen.

**⚠ Abhyankar's saṃjñā claim, recorded with its damage flagged.** His entry runs: six adhyāyas "in which **no technical terms or saṃjñās like** …" — and **the OCR loses the rest of the clause.** The claim being made is evidently that Candragomin dispenses with Pāṇini's artificial technical terms. ⚠ **It is not asserted here**: the sentence is incomplete in the only copy read, no independent source consulted confirmed it, and it is exactly the kind of striking structural claim §0 says to be slowest about. **Recorded as an unverified reading of a damaged line**, and carried as a follow-up.

**⚠ A name-collision, the ninth flagged since Batch 47 — and this one Abhyankar makes and marks in the same breath.** He has an entry for a **writer of a Prākṛt grammar** who "was known also as [Candra] and hence **identified by some with Candragomin**." ⭐ **The generator is the short-form name**: *Candra* is what both men were called, and the identification follows from nothing else. **This is the [[vararuci]] mechanism exactly** — and it is the second time in two batches that a Prākṛt grammarian is the party absorbed into a better-known Sanskrit one. Separately, Abhyankar records a **different** Buddhist grammarian as "the author of the work named ***Candra-Vyākaraṇa Paddhati***" — a further work in the school, by another hand, under a name one letter from the system's own.

**⭐ The corpus's first named European in this literature.** Abhyankar carries an entry for **[LIEBICH, BRUNO]**, "a European grammarian belonging to **Breslau**… He made a critical study of Sanskrit grammar and **edited the Candra Vyākaraṇa** and the *Kṣīratáraṅgiṇī*." Liebich brought out **two** editions — text alone, then text with the *Vṛtti* "which is attributed to Candra himself" — and is also the author of the standard monograph on the **[[katantra|Kātantra]]**. ⭐ **The two non-Pāṇinian systems this batch opens were both put into modern circulation by one man**, and the tradition's own leading lexicographer gives him a headword among the ācāryas. ⚠ Note the recursion this creates and that Abhyankar does not: Liebich's edition is the reason a "six-chapter" Cāndravyākaraṇa is the text everyone describes.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Abhyankar** at first hand (Buddhist authorship; Tibetan→Devanāgarī transmission; six adhyāyas; the saṃjñā claim, damaged; the *Vākyapadīya* II.489 reference; the 5th-c. date; the *Agni Purāṇa* ch. 248–258 summary; Liebich; the Prākṛt-grammarian collision; the *Candra-Vyākaraṇa Paddhati*). **LinguIndic**, independent (Cāndrasūtra; c. 450 CE; Bengal; the Kātyāyana-vārttika incorporation; the author's own Cāndravṛtti). **Ramhari Timalsina / Heidelberg**, independent and **decisive against both of the above** on the chapter question. ***Glimpses of the History of Sanskrit Literature***, independent (c. 470 CE with a 600 alternative; "three-fourth of the length" of the Aṣṭādhyāyī; Candragomin's own claim of "abrevity, lucidity, and comprehensiveness"). **CONTESTED**, confidence **medium** — the disagreements are real and one of them is a straight correction of the specialist source. ⚠ **No part of the Cāndrasūtra has been read**, and the node's most interesting single claim (the lost chapters) rests on **one** modern scholar reported through search rather than read.
