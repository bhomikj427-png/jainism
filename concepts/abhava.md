## Gloss / Divergence map

***Abhāva*** — **absence** — is [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]]'s **seventh category**, the one addition to [[kanada|Kaṇāda]]'s six. The claim is not the trivial one that things are sometimes missing. It is that **an absence is an entity**: a real, locatable object of the world, which can be perceived, can be a term in a relation, and can itself be a locus.

⚠ **This key is the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *padārtha* only.** The corpus already uses *abhāva* in a **different job** in `prabhakara.md`, where Kumārila's sixth *pramāṇa* is named "*abhāva*, non-apprehension." **Same Sanskrit word, two different offices**: for Mīmāṃsā *abhāva* is a **means of knowing**; for Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika it is a **kind of being**. The two schools are in fact opponents on precisely this — see below and the `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edge. **The map must teach the split; no second node is minted, but the distinction is asserted here and cross-linked.**

### Why it matters more than a list-entry should

Its editor states the stakes better than a summary can. **D. H. H. Ingalls**, introducing Matilal's monograph:

> "The doctrine of negation in Navya-nyāya is like a keystone that holds in place the interlocking voussoirs of an arch. It is essential to Navya-nyāya **metaphysics**, which hypostatizes 'absence' into a category. It is the peculiar mark of Nyāya **epistemology**, for the Nyāya insisted, **against the opposition of all other schools, that one can *see* the absence of an object in a given place.** And it is a keystone of Navya-nyāya **logic**, for in this system which presents a hierarchy of abstractions rather than a hierarchy of classes it is only by the use of negation that universal laws can be framed."

Note the middle clause. **Nyāya stood alone.** That one *perceives* an absence — walks into a room and sees that the pot is not there, rather than inferring it — is a minority position it held against everyone.

And the third clause is the point [[avacchedaka|`avacchedaka.md`]] develops: with no classes to quantify over, generality has to be built out of absences. Ingalls's own example: *"There is an absence of occurrence of humanity in such a locus as is not a locus of mortality. That is to say, all men are mortal."*

### The apparatus

Every absence is specified by what it is an absence **of**. That term is the ***pratiyogin***, the **counterpositive**; the locus is the ***anuyogin***. "There is no pot on the ground" analyses as: the ground is the locus of an absence whose counterpositive is pot. And per [[avacchedaka|the limitor]], the counterpositive-ness is **delimited** — *ghaṭatvāvacchinna-pratiyogitā*, "counterpositive-ness delimited by pot-ness" — which is how Nyāya says *all* pots without a quantifier, and how it scopes: add blue-colour to the limitor and the absence concerns only blue pots.

### The divergence I — **how many kinds of absence?**

| division | who | the kinds |
|---|---|---|
| **two** | **Jayanta** (*Nyāyamañjarī*) — who "mentioned six types of absence" but "tried to reduce all types to these two"; and this is the pair **Gautama** treats as fundamental (*NS* 2.2.12) | *prāgabhāva* (prior absence — no pot on the wheel before it is made) and *dhvaṃsa*/*pradhvaṃsa* (destruction, posterior absence — no pot on the ground after the hammer) |
| **four** | **Śrīdhara** (*Nyāyakandalī*, c. 991) — and this is the standard later list | the two above, plus *atyantābhāva* (constant/absolute absence — no colour in air) and *anyonyābhāva* (mutual absence, i.e. **difference** — "a cloth is not a pot") |
| **six** | Jayanta reports it and rejects it | — |

**The organising cut is not the number but *transient vs eternal*.** Prior absence and destruction are **temporal**: they begin or end. Constant and mutual absence are **eternal** (*nitya*, *sadātana*). And this is where the doctrine takes its heaviest fire — the objection is that the eternal absences do no work:

> "Water does not acquire any extra property by being the locus of the eternal absence of fire in it, nor does a pot change from being a pot by being not-cloth or not-stick."

The **Prābhākara** presses it into a *regress* charge, which Matilal translates from Gaṅgeśa's own text: "the Naiyāyika who allows the absence of trees on a mountain to be an independent entity **will not allow** the absence of pine trees in that absence to be a further independent entity" — i.e. Nyāya stops the regress by fiat, and the stopping-point is arbitrary.

⚠ **Śrīdhara saw the hardest internal problem and named it**: if constant absence and mutual absence are *both* eternal, what distinguishes them? His answer — reported by Matilal — is that in a **constant** absence what is denied "is in some way imaginary (*asat*), because it never occurs in the locus of denial but is imagined to do so," whereas a **mutual** absence "is a denial of the identity of two real objects." ⚠ Matilal elsewhere records that the "supposed distinction between a relational absence and a mutual absence does not stand the test" — **the internal dispute did not close.** Recorded open.

### The divergence II — ⭐ **the seventh category was retrofitted**

This is the finding, and it is the kind §0 exists to catch: a doctrine that the tradition attributes to its founders and that its founders did not state.

Matilal, on the *Vaiśeṣika* lineage:

> "the early Vaiśeṣikas (excluding, however, **Candramati's *Daśapadārthaśāstra***) did **not** speak of absence as a separate category. One might very well explain the *Vaiśeṣika-sūtras* 9.1.1–10 without assuming that **Kaṇāda** was speaking here of absence as a separate category. **Praśastapāda** also did not state whether absence should be considered a separate category. **Śrīdhara** (ca. A.D. 991), in his *Nyāyakandalī*, and **Udayana** (ca. A.D. 1050), in his *Kiraṇāvalī*, tried to argue that although absence had **not been mentioned** by Praśastapāda (or by Kaṇāda) as a separate *padārtha*, it was nevertheless **so approved by them implicitly**."

Three things follow, and the corpus should hold all three:

1. **The corpus's own Vaiśeṣika files are vindicated, not contradicted.** `vaiseshika-sutra.md` gives **six** *padārthas* from *VS* 1.1.4, read verbatim with Śaṅkara Miśra's *Upaskāra*; `dravya-vaisheshika.md` and `prashastapada.md` say six throughout. **Six is right for the root texts.** Ganeri's "the first six comprise the classical list ... while the seventh (absence) is a distinctive addition by the later school" says the same thing from the other end.
2. **The retrofit is explicit and was argued for.** Śrīdhara and Udayana did not smuggle the category in; they *argued* that Kaṇāda and Praśastapāda had approved it **implicitly**. That is a commentarial move with a name and a date, not a silent corruption — and the corpus should record it as an argument that can be assessed, not as a mistake.
3. ⚠⚠ **And note *who*, and *in which book*.** The retrofitter is **[[udayana|Udayana]]**, in the ***Kiraṇāvalī*** — the same man and the same work that `jati.md` runs on for the theory of universals, that `paramanu-vaisheshika.md` credits with making Īśvara the director of atomic combination, and whom **`navya-nyaya.md` records as one of the three candidate *starting points of Navya-Nyāya***. **The seventh category is installed by the man at whom, on one respectable periodisation, the "new" Nyāya begins.** This is not a coincidence constructed by this file: it is one man, one text, reached independently by four earlier nodes.

⚠ **One qualification, stated so the finding is not overstated.** Candramati's *Daśapadārthaśāstra* is expressly excluded from Matilal's generalisation — an early Vaiśeṣika work with **ten** categories that evidently did treat absence. So "the early Vaiśeṣikas had no seventh category" is a claim about the mainline *sūtra*-and-*bhāṣya* tradition, **not** about every early Vaiśeṣika text. The corpus has no node for Candramati and did not read the *Daśapadārthaśāstra*.

### The divergence III — the schools do not agree that absence is a thing

| position | who | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| Absence is a **real category of being**, and is **perceived** | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, from Śrīdhara/Udayana on | An ontology containing negative entities; the minority epistemology Ingalls flags |
| Absence is not a category but grounds a distinct **means of knowing** — *anupalabdhi* / *abhāva* as a sixth *pramāṇa* | **Kumārila** (*Mīmāṃsā-śloka-vārttika*: *pratyakṣāder anutpattiḥ pramāṇam ucyate*) | Non-apprehension yields knowledge directly; no negative entity is needed to do it |
| Neither — the whole apparatus is unnecessary | **Prabhākara** (*Bṛhatī*), with **Śālikanātha** (c. 825, *Bṛhatī-ṭīkā* and *Prakaraṇapañcikā*) | Five *pramāṇa*s; "absence" reduces to the essential nature of the locus |
| It is not a separate instrument of cognition, and whether it is a separate *prameya* is a further question | **Vallabha**, *Nyāyalīlāvatī* (c. 1150), who treats all three questions separately | That "is absence real?" and "is absence a way of knowing?" and "what does *na* mean?" are three questions, not one |

⚠ **This corroborates a corpus claim from primary sources.** `mimamsa-pramana.md` and `prabhakara.md` already record the Bhāṭṭa/Prābhākara split on *anupalabdhi* — Kumārila accepts a sixth *pramāṇa*, Prabhākara five — from Wikipedia and educational sites. Matilal reaches the same split **by page-citation to Kumārila's *Ślokavārttika* (pp. 473–492) and Prabhākara's *Bṛhatī* (pp. 118–122)**, with the Sanskrit. An encyclopedia claim in this corpus now has a critical-edition citation behind it.

### The texts, and what is within reach

Matilal's volume contains, in English translation with notes:
- **Gaṅgeśa, *Abhāva-vāda*** ("A Discourse on Absence"), *Tattvacintāmaṇi* Part I — pp. 109ff., Sanskrit text in Appendix A;
- **[[raghunatha-siromani|Raghunātha]], *Nañvāda*** ("A Discourse on the Significance of Negative Particles") — pp. 148ff., Sanskrit in Appendix B;
- and a chapter on **Gautama and Vātsyāyana on Absence** (p. 104).

Ingalls characterises the pair: the *Abhāva-vāda* is "the **orthodox** wing," the *Nañvāda* "the **radical** wing, of the Navya-Nyāya school."

⚠ **These translations were located and their framing read; the translated texts themselves were not worked through**, beyond the passages quoted here (passage 17 on eternal absences and the Prābhākara regress). **This is the corpus's nearest approach yet to a Navya-Nyāya primary text in a published scholarly translation, and it is not yet exhausted.**

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — B. K. Matilal, *The Navya-Nyāya Doctrine of Negation*, Harvard University Press, 1968.** **Read at first hand.** An entire monograph on this term, with a critical-edition apparatus. Supplies the retrofit finding (Kaṇāda, Praśastapāda, Śrīdhara, Udayana), the two/four/six divisions with Jayanta and Gautama *NS* 2.2.12, Śrīdhara's constant-vs-mutual problem and his answer, the transient/eternal cut, the Prābhākara regress objection in translation from Gaṅgeśa's own text, the Mīmāṃsā page-citations, Vallabha's three questions, and the limitor's role in generality.

**Signal 2 — D. H. H. Ingalls, Editor's Introduction to the same volume.** A different author making a different point: the keystone image, the three-way (metaphysics/epistemology/logic) statement of why absence matters, **the claim that Nyāya held the perceptibility of absence "against the opposition of all other schools,"** and the orthodox/radical characterisation of Gaṅgeśa vs Raghunātha.

**Signal 3 — Jonardon Ganeri, *Navya-Nyāya: Analytical Philosophy in Early Modern India*.** Fully independent: "the first six comprise the classical list of categories, found even in the *Vaiśeṣika-sūtra*, while the seventh (absence) is **a distinctive addition by the later school**" — the same conclusion as signal 1's retrofit argument, reached without the argument.

**Signal 4 — corpus-internal, and this is the strongest available check.** `vaiseshika-sutra.md` (six *padārthas*, from *VS* 1.1.4 read **verbatim** with Śaṅkara Miśra's *Upaskāra* — a primary-text reading, made batches before this node existed), `dravya-vaisheshika.md`, `prashastapada.md`, `kanada.md` — all six, none seven. `mimamsa-pramana.md` and `prabhakara.md` on the Bhāṭṭa/Prābhākara *anupalabdhi* split. `udayana.md`, `jati.md`, `paramanu-vaisheshika.md` on the *Kiraṇāvalī*.

**Independence.** Signals 1 and 3 are thirty-odd years and one continent apart and agree on the substantive claim. Signal 2 is bound in the same covers as signal 1 but is a distinct author on a distinct point. Signal 4 is the real test and it **passes**: the corpus's Vaiśeṣika layer, written from Wikipedia and a 1923 translation with no knowledge of Matilal, records exactly the six that Matilal says the root texts have — so the retrofit finding **explains** an agreement the corpus already had rather than disturbing it.

**Why contested, not converged.** Three live disputes, none of which the sources close: **how many** kinds of absence (2 / 4 / 6, and Matilal notes the relational-vs-mutual distinction "does not stand the test"); **whether** absence is a category at all (Nyāya yes, Prābhākara no, Bhāṭṭa a *pramāṇa* instead); and **whether the root texts support it** (they do not state it; Śrīdhara and Udayana argue they imply it). The disagreement is the finding.

**Why medium.** Two independent specialists plus a first-hand monograph, and a corpus-internal check that passes. **Not high**: the primary texts were **not** worked through — Gaṅgeśa's *Abhāva-vāda* and Raghunātha's *Nañvāda* are sitting in translation in the same volume and were sampled, not read; Śrīdhara, Jayanta, Vallabha, Candramati, Kumārila and Prabhākara are all reached **only through Matilal's citations**, none consulted directly; and *VS* 9.1.1–10, the passage the whole retrofit question turns on, was **not** read (`vaiseshika-sutra.md` read 1.1.1–1.1.4, not book 9).
