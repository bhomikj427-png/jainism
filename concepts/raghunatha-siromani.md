## Gloss / Divergence map

**Raghunātha Śiromaṇi** (c. 1477–1547, Navadvīpa) is [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]]'s second great figure and its most destructive one. He wrote the ***Dīdhiti***, the commentary on [[gangesha|Gaṅgeśa]]'s [[tattvacintamani|*Tattvacintāmaṇi*]] that displaced every earlier commentary and became **the object of commentary in its own right** — after him, Naiyāyikas commented on *him* rather than on Jayadeva's *Āloka*. And he wrote the ***Padārthatattvanirūpaṇa***, a short treatise that **takes apart the category-scheme his own school was built on**.

[[udayana|D. H. H. Ingalls]] fixes his position in one word. Introducing Matilal's translations of Gaṅgeśa's *Abhāva-vāda* alongside Raghunātha's *Nañvāda*, he calls the first "the **orthodox** wing" and the second "the **radical** wing, of the Navya-Nyāya school."

⚠ **This is the node that most complicates the corpus's Vaiśeṣika layer, and it should.** The corpus has carried Vaiśeṣika atomism, the seven-fold category scheme and Udayana's theory of universals as settled school doctrine. **The most powerful logician the school produced rejected all three.**

### What he did to the categories

The *Padārthatattvanirūpaṇa* ("Inquiry into the True Nature of Things") is described by every source consulted as a *short* treatise. Its programme, as reported:

| move | what it does |
|---|---|
| **identifies time, space and ether (*ākāśa*) with God** | three of the nine substances collapse into one |
| **eliminates *manas* (mind)** as a category, reducing it to matter | removes the ninth substance |
| ⭐ **denies atoms (*paramāṇu*) and dyads (*dvyaṇuka*)** | **rejects Vaiśeṣika atomism outright** |
| **dismisses *viśeṣa*, the "basic differentiator," as unproven** | removes the category the school is *named after* |
| **eliminates "number," "separateness," …** | strips items from the list of qualities |
| **adds eight new categories** | *śakti* (power), *svatva* (ownedness), *kṣaṇa* (moment), *kāraṇatva* (causehood), *kāryatva* (effecthood), *saṃkhyā* (number), *vaiśiṣṭya* (the qualifying relation pertaining to absence), *viṣayatā* (content-ness) |

⚠ **The two lists appear to contradict on "number": it is named among the things he *eliminates* and among the eight he *adds*.** This file does **not** smooth that over. But there is a reconciliation with independent support, and it is offered **as a hypothesis, labelled**: Ganeri, writing about a different problem entirely, reports that Raghunātha's whole treatment of number turns on the observation that number does **not** behave like an ordinary inhering quality —

> "(1) The table has wooden legs. (2) The table has four legs." — (1) entails "each leg is wooden"; (2) does **not** entail "each leg is four."

So he "postulates a new relation, **'completion' (*paryāpti*)**, which relates the property four-hood to the four legs **jointly**, but not to each leg individually," and remarks that this relation, "whose existence is indicated by constructions such as 'This is one pot' and 'These are two', is a special kind of self-linking relation." His commentator **Jagadīśa** adds that completion "relates the property two-hood **by delimiting it** as a property which resides in both pots. Otherwise, it would follow that there is no difference between saying 'These are two' and 'Each one possesses two-hood'."

**On this reading he removes number from the *guṇa*s — where it would have to inhere in each thing severally — and re-installs it as a *padārtha* in its own right, related jointly by *paryāpti*.** ⚠ **Recorded as a reconciliation, not as a sourced statement.** Neither the Britannica-derived list nor Ganeri says this; it is what makes the two consistent, and Potter's edition would settle it.

⚠ **⭐ The atomism finding, and what it costs the corpus.** `paramanu-vaisheshika.md`, `kanada.md` and `dravya-vaisheshika.md` all present Vaiśeṣika atomism as the school's ontology. **Raghunātha denies atoms and dyads.** Two consequences the corpus should hold: (a) the atomism is *Kaṇāda's and Praśastapāda's and Udayana's*, not "Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika's" without qualification, because the school's greatest late logician threw it out; (b) `kanada.md`'s standing warning that the *paramāṇu* doctrine is "routinely inflated into 'ancient Indian atomic theory = modern physics'" gains a **historical** argument to add to its conceptual one — **a doctrine that its own tradition's leading later thinker rejected on metaphysical grounds is not a stable empirical discovery being handed forward.** ⚠ *What he replaced it with is not established here* — whether the rejection is of atoms as such or only of the *dvyaṇuka*/*tryaṇuka* aggregation mechanism is exactly what Potter's translation would show, and it was not read.

### What he did to universals

`jati.md` runs on **Udayana's six exclusion criteria** — the *jāti-bādhakas*, conditions under which no genuine universal can exist. Ganeri reports that **Raghunātha rejects one of the central ones and threatens the rest**:

> "Some Naiyāyikas, especially **Bhāsarvajña and Raghunātha**, reject the 'cross-connection' [*saṅkara*] impediment, pointing out the problems encountered trying to apply it to non-biological natural kinds (e.g. redness and greenness overlap, as do pothood and goldhood). **Raghunātha suggests that consistent application of this principle would eliminate virtually all universals from the ontology.**"

That is an internal *reductio* against his own school's realism about universals: apply Udayana's rule honestly and the furniture disappears. ⚠ Ganeri adds his own assessment, which is not Raghunātha's and is marked as such: Udayana's criterion "is thus not a necessary one, and neither is it sufficient" — none of the impediments explains why *blue/green* is more natural than Goodman's *grue/bleen*.

### Life, and one claim held at arm's length

- **Dates**: **1477–1547**, given identically by Wikipedia and by a Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika historical study; Deb's independent chronology gives **c. 1475–1550**. Three sources, one date-range: **this is the least contested date in the whole Navya-Nyāya layer**, and the contrast with [[gangesha|Gaṅgeśa's]] four-way split is itself informative — a 15th–16th-c. Bengali is simply better documented than a 14th-c. Maithil.
- **Born in Navadvīpa** (Nadia, then in the Bengal Sultanate). His teacher was **Vāsudeva Sārvabhauma**, who founded the Bengal school. Through his mother he was grandson of **Śūlapāṇi**, a noted 14th-c. writer on *smṛti* — i.e. he comes out of a jurisprudential family, which fits Navya-Nyāya's known export into legal theory.
- ⚠ **Wikipedia says he "served as head (Chancellor) of the Ancient Mithila University," also called Mithila Vidyapeeth. No other source consulted mentions this, and it sits oddly with everything else** — he is the founding figure of the *Bengal* school, and the standard story of the period is that Navadvīpa **displaced** Mithilā. **Not asserted.** The kernel it may distort is the well-known tradition that Raghunātha studied at Mithilā under Pakṣadhara (Jayadeva) Miśra before returning to Bengal — but that tradition was **not** found in any source read here, and is not asserted either.

### His works

| work | what it is |
|---|---|
| ***Tattvacintāmaṇi-dīdhiti*** (TCD) | the commentary on Gaṅgeśa; **the most-commented-on text of the later school** — Mathurānātha, Jagadīśa (*Jāgadīśī*) and [[gadadhara|Gadādhara]] (*Gādādharī*) all wrote **on it**, not on Gaṅgeśa |
| ***Padārthatattvanirūpaṇa*** (PTN) | the category revision above. **Text and translation: Karl H. Potter, Harvard University Press, Harvard-Yenching Institute Studies vol. 17, 1957.** Sanskrit edition with Raghudeva's and Rāmabhadra's commentaries: ed. Vindheswariprasad Dvivedin, Benares, 1916 |
| ***Nañvāda*** | "A Discourse on the Significance of Negative Particles" — **translated into English by Matilal (1968), with the Sanskrit in his Appendix B**; the "radical wing" |
| ***Avacchedakatva-nirukti*** | "Definition of Limitorship" — printed in TCDJ pp. 257–283; per Matilal he "largely favored" two of the four rival accounts of [[avacchedaka|limitorship]], including the one that makes it **unanalysable** |

⚠ **Note the shape of that list. Two of his four works are available in published English translation** (Potter 1957, Matilal 1968), and **neither was read here.** This is the best primary-source access the corpus has to any Navya-Nyāya author, and it is unexploited.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Jonardon Ganeri, *Navya-Nyāya: Analytical Philosophy in Early Modern India*.** The *paryāpti*/"completion" relation with the four-legged-table argument, Raghunātha's own remark on it, and Jagadīśa's gloss; the rejection of the *saṅkara* impediment and the "would eliminate virtually all universals" *reductio*; his pairing with Bhāsarvajña; and the placement of Raghunātha with Jagadīśa and Gadādhara as the school's "heights."

**Signal 2 — B. K. Matilal, *The Navya-Nyāya Doctrine of Negation*, Harvard 1968, read at first hand.** The *Avacchedakatva-nirukti* and his preferences among the four accounts of limitorship (ch. 9 n. 11, TCDJ pp. 257–283); the *Nañvāda* translation and Sanskrit text; and the verified edition data for TCD, PTN, TCDJ and TCDG.

**Signal 3 — D. H. H. Ingalls, Editor's Introduction to the same volume.** The orthodox/radical characterisation — a separate author's judgement, and the single most compact statement of what this man is for.

**Signal 4 — Wikipedia, "Raghunatha Shiromani."** 1477–1547; Navadvīpa, Nadia, Bengal Sultanate; Vāsudeva Sārvabhauma; the Śūlapāṇi descent; the *Dīdhiti*; "brought ... Navya Nyāya ... to its zenith of analytic power." ⚠ **And the unsupported Mithilā chancellorship.**

**Signal 5 — a Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika critical and historical study**, "Authors of Nyāya (i)," Wisdomlib. Independently: 1477–1547; "the second great figure of the Navya-Nyaya school"; "an independent thinker"; the *Dīdhiti* and the *Padārtha-tattva-nirūpaṇa* as "a short treatise" which "refuted the Vaiśeṣika categories, **particularly the category of *viśeṣa***." ⚠ Page marked **not proofread**.

**Signal 6 — search-level rendering of Britannica's *Padārthatattvanirūpaṇa* entry** and of a second source on the eight new categories. The four-part revision programme (God-identification, mind, atoms, number/separateness) and the eight additions with their Sanskrit names. ⚠ **Britannica itself returned HTTP 403 on two attempts**, so this is Britannica **as reported by a search index**, not Britannica read. Weighted accordingly — but note that its *viśeṣa* claim is independently confirmed by signal 5, and its atomism claim by a second search route.

**Signal 7 — Deb, *The Navya-Nyāya Theory of Pakṣatā*.** c. 1475–1550; pupil of Vāsudeva Sārvabhauma (c. 1430–1540); and the observation that "after Raghunātha, many Navyanaiyāyikas commented on the *Dīdhiti* rather than Jayadeva's *Āloka*."

**Independence.** Signals 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7 are genuinely independent, and they converge on the essentials: the dates, the teacher, the two principal works, the *Dīdhiti*'s displacement of its rivals, and — from two directions that never mention each other — that he **attacked his own school's ontology** (Ganeri on universals; the historical study on *viśeṣa*). Signal 6 is the weakest link and carries the most specific content, which is why every claim taken from it is attributed to it by name.

⚠ **A contradiction inside signal 6, left standing.** "Number" appears in its list of eliminations *and* in its list of additions. The reconciliation proposed above is supported by signal 1 but is not stated by any source; it is marked as a hypothesis. **Potter 1957 would settle it in a page.**

**Why contested.** The reported content of the *Padārthatattvanirūpaṇa* is internally inconsistent as the corpus has it; one biographical claim (the Mithilā chancellorship) is single-sourced and implausible; and his positions **contradict doctrines this corpus has recorded as school doctrine** — atomism, the seven categories, *viśeṣa*, Udayana's universals — a contradiction that is real and belongs in the map rather than being resolved by preferring one side. **Why medium.** Five independent sources agree on the frame, and the two strongest are read at first hand. **Not high**: **his two translated works were not read** — Potter 1957 and Matilal's *Nañvāda* are both available and unexploited; the eight new categories rest on a search-rendered Britannica entry alone; and the *Dīdhiti*, the work he is most famous for, is described here only by its reception.
