## Gloss / Divergence map

**Candragomin** is the name attached to the [[candra-vyakarana|Cāndra-vyākaraṇa]], the first great non-Pāṇinian **Buddhist** grammar — and also to a body of Buddhist poetry, drama and vow-literature, and also to a celebrated multi-year debate at Nālandā against **Candrakīrti**. ⚠ **Whether that is one man or two is an open scholarly question with a named divider**, and the corpus's sources do not merely disagree — **they disagree about which works belong to whom.**

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Two men.** "The name of **two important figures** in classical Indian Buddhism: a Sanskrit **grammarian in the 5th century**, and a scholar and **poet in the 7th century**" — the latter "a lay practitioner (*upāsaka*), scholar, and poet" who "flourished in the latter three quarters of the seventh century," from **Eastern Bengal**, and it is **he** who debated Candrakīrti | ⭐ **Mark Tatz**, followed by the *Encyclopedia of Buddhism* | the grammarian **did not** debate Candrakīrti, and the *Śiṣyalekha* and vow-texts are **not** the grammarian's |
| **One man**, 5th–6th c., a teacher at Nālandā, author of the plays *and* the letters, whom "the Tibetan tradition believes… challenged Chandrakirti" | **Wikipedia** — which treats him as a single person throughout and cites Majumdar (1943) | the debate and the grammar belong to one biography |
| **One man**, "Candra or Candragomin," **5th century A.D.** | **Abhyankar**; **LinguIndic** (c. 450 CE, Bengal) | the grammarian is *the* Candragomin |
| the *Lokānanda* — "the **oldest Sanskrit Buddhist play in existence**" — is the **5th-century** figure's | *Encyclopedia of Buddhism* | ⚠ a **grammarian who wrote the first Buddhist drama** |
| the *Lokānanda* is by the same man as the *Śiṣyalekha* | Wikipedia | ⚠ the opposite division of the same works |

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐ Name-collision #10 — and it is the first in this corpus where a modern specialist has already made the split, so the corpus can watch the split fail to propagate.** Batches 47–48 flagged nine collisions and in every one the corpus was the party doing the separating, or Abhyankar was. Here **Mark Tatz** distinguished the fifth-century grammarian from the seventh-century *upāsaka* poet, and the *Encyclopedia of Buddhism* records it. ⭐ **And Wikipedia — incomparably the most-read of the three — still runs them together, and does something worse besides: its article on Chandragomin does not mention the grammar at all.** Asked directly, it gives the plays, the letters, the vow-verses and the Tārā hymns, and for grammar only this: "Tāranātha wanted to depict Chandragomin as someone who is **learned and cultured** while **Butön Rinchen Drub emphasised his work on grammar**." ⭐⭐ **The division of the man is visible inside the Tibetan historiography itself — two historians of one tradition writing up one name with different halves foregrounded — and the modern encyclopedia has inherited Tāranātha's half and dropped Butön's.** ⚠ **The corpus takes no position on one-or-two**; it records that the merge is the default and the split is the specialist's.

**⭐ The generator, and what it adds to the corpus's taxonomy.** [[candra-vyakarana]] records Abhyankar's *separate* collision: a **Prākṛt grammarian** "known also as [Candra] and **hence identified by some with Candragomin**." So the short form **Candra** absorbs at least two other men — a Prākṛt grammarian and (as *Candrācārya*) the reviver of Mahābhāṣya study in Kashmir. ⭐ **This name has now generated three separate identifications, and the mechanism in each is the same one [[vararuci]] diagnosed: a short form that several people answer to.** ⚠ *Gomin* appears to be a **title** rather than part of a personal name — the honorific of a lay Buddhist devotee, which would fit the *upāsaka* description exactly and would explain why the short form is the one that travels. **Not asserted**: no source consulted states it, and §3 makes etymology a clue and not a verdict. Recorded as the obvious hypothesis, unverified.

**⭐ What the debate story commits you to, and why it is not decorative.** On the Tatz division the man who debated Candrakīrti "advocated **Vijñānavāda**" against Candrakīrti's **Madhyamaka** — a lay [[yogacara|Yogācāra]] partisan against the great [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] commentator, at Nālandā, "over a period of seven years." ⚠ **The corpus holds this as Tibetan tradition, not as history** — the Encyclopedia of Buddhism attributes it to Tibetan sources and Wikipedia says only that "the Tibetan tradition believes" it. ⭐ But note the consequence for *this* node: **if Tatz is right, the grammar has nothing to do with the debate, and the Cāndra school's Buddhism is a fact about its author's affiliation and not about its doctrine.** ⚠⚠ That matters for §0 discipline: it is tempting to read a Yogācāra philosophy of language out of a "Buddhist grammar." **Nothing consulted supports that, and on the split reading the philosophical partisan is a different man two centuries later.** The Cāndra grammar's own stated virtues, per its author, are "**abrevity, lucidity, and comprehensiveness**" — pedagogical, not doctrinal.

**⭐ A lay author, and the corpus should notice what that is doing.** Both readings agree Candragomin was an ***upāsaka*** — a layman, not a monk — and the Tibetan tradition made the layman's victory over a great monastic scholar the point of the story. ⭐ Set that beside [[sarvavarman]], whose legend has a **layman teach a king in six months**, and beside Abhyankar's judgement that the Kātantra was written "for beginners and students of average intelligence." **Three of this batch's four opening nodes are about grammar leaving the institution that owned it** — the Veda in [[katantra]], the monastery here, and the seminary in both. ⚠ **This is a pattern the corpus is *noticing*, not a thesis it is arguing**: the sample is four nodes from one batch, chosen because they were the gap, and that is exactly the selection effect that manufactures patterns. Recorded with the caveat attached.

**⚠ What the divergence does to the date, and the corpus does not split the difference.** The candidate dates are **5th c.** (Abhyankar), **c. 450** (LinguIndic), **c. 470 with "some external evidence" for 600** (*Glimpses*), **5th–6th c.** (Wikipedia), and **latter three quarters of the 7th c.** for Tatz's second man. ⭐ **Note that the "600" alternative and the 7th-century figure are pointing at the same thing from two directions** — if the grammar is late, it is late because its author is the debater; if the grammar is c. 450, the debater is someone else. **The date question and the identity question are one question**, which is why the *Glimpses* alternative cannot be averaged away. The corpus records the range and the reason for it.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** **Encyclopedia of Buddhism** (Buddhist-studies reference; the two-figure split, Tatz, Eastern Bengal, the seven-year debate, the works divided between the two) — ⚠ the corpus already uses this site for [[pramana-samuccaya]] and [[dignaga-pramana]], so it is a *known* source, not a new one. **Wikipedia** (independent, fetched; the single-figure treatment, Majumdar 1943, the *Lokānanda* as "the oldest Sanskrit Buddhist play in existence," the Tāranātha/Butön contrast, and ⭐ **the complete absence of the grammar**). **Abhyankar** at first hand (Candra ≈ Candragomin, 5th c., the Buddhist authorship, the Tibetan-script transmission). **LinguIndic** (c. 450, Bengal). ⭐ **They are genuinely independent and they split cleanly along disciplinary lines: the two Buddhist-studies sources know the poet, the two grammar sources know the grammarian, and only one source of the four knows that both exist.** **CONTESTED**, confidence **medium** — ⚠ **Tatz has not been read** (his "Candragomin and the bodhisattva vow" is on academia.edu and was not fetched), and **no work of Candragomin's has been read in any genre.**
