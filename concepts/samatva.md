## Gloss

**Samatva** (Sanskrit, "evenness, equanimity, sameness") is the Bhagavad Gītā's own definition of **yoga**: *yogasthaḥ kuru karmāṇi… siddhy-asiddhyoḥ samo bhūtvā, samatvaṁ yoga ucyate* — "established in yoga, perform actions… being even-minded toward success and failure; this equanimity is called yoga" (BG 2.48). It is the disposition that performs one's duty (*karma*) while abandoning attachment (*saṅga*) to its fruits — neither elated by gain nor cast down by loss, neither by pleasure nor pain, honour nor dishonour.

Samatva is the affective core of **karma-yoga**: action is to be done *fully* but with the mind held even, releasing the result to the larger order. It is not indifference or inaction — Kṛṣṇa explicitly commands continued action (*kuru karmāṇi*) — but the inner steadiness that makes desireless action possible. The person who has perfected samatva is the **sthitaprajña** (BG 2.55–72), the sage of steady wisdom.

**Cross-tradition note (parallel, not identity).** Equanimity-ideals recur across Indian traditions — Buddhist *upekkhā* (the fourth brahmavihāra), Jain *mādhyasthya* (tolerance toward the incorrigible), Patañjali's *upekṣā* (Yoga-Sūtra 1.33). These occupy a parallel structural slot, but each is embedded in a different goal: samatva grounds *detached action within the world*, upekkhā grounds liberation-insight (*anattā*), mādhyasthya guards a vow within a karmic-physics soteriology. The Gītā's samatva notably defines yoga as equanimity centuries before Patañjali defines it as citta-vṛtti-nirodha.
