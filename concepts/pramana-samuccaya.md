## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Pramāṇasamuccaya** ("Compendium of Valid Cognition") of **Dignāga** (c. 480–540 CE) is the **founding text of Buddhist epistemology** (*pramāṇavāda*, the "Epistemological School") — the work that "laid the groundwork for the development of deductive logic in India." Dignāga, born near Kāñcīpuram and trained under **Vasubandhu**, composed it as **root verses with his own auto-commentary (Pramāṇasamuccayavṛtti)**; it is his mature, culminating statement. It anchors the project's files [[dignaga-pramana]], [[apoha]], and the Buddhist-logic tradition [[hetu-vidya]].

**Six chapters.** (1) perception (*pratyakṣa*); (2–4) inference and argumentation (*anumāna*, *parārthānumāna*); (5) language (the *apoha* theory); (6) fallacies and points of defeat (a critique of the Nyāya debate-categories).

**Two pramāṇas, two objects.** Dignāga's signature restriction: "there are only two instruments of knowledge — direct perception and inference," because there are exactly two kinds of object — the **svalakṣaṇa** (the unique, bare particular, grasped by perception) and the **sāmānyalakṣaṇa** (the generic/constructed universal, grasped by inference). **Perception is non-conceptual** — "knowledge free from all conceptual construction, including name and class concepts" (*kalpanāpoḍha*). Inference works through a middle term with **three characteristics** (the trairūpya: present in the subject, present in similar cases, absent in dissimilar cases). Chapter 5's **apoha** ("exclusion") holds that "a word indicates an object merely through the exclusion of other objects" — meaning by exclusion, not by positive reference to real universals (a nominalist move that dispenses with the Nyāya jāti).

**Transmission.** The Sanskrit original is **largely lost**; it is reconstructed from **two Tibetan translations** (by Vasudhararakṣita and Kanakavarman) and from **Jinendrabuddhi's commentary (Ṭīkā)**, an ongoing scholarly project. **Dharmakīrti's [[pramanavarttika]]** is the great later elaboration of this text.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1** (Encyclopedia of Buddhism, "Pramāṇasamuccaya"): the seminal pramāṇavāda text; verse + auto-commentary; the six-chapter structure (perception / inference 2–4 / language / fallacies); "only two instruments of knowledge"; svalakṣaṇa–perception and sāmānyalakṣaṇa–inference; perception as *kalpanāpoḍha*; the trairūpya; apoha as exclusion; Sanskrit lost, reconstructed from the two Tibetan translations + Jinendrabuddhi's Ṭīkā. **Signal 2** (Tibetan Buddhist Encyclopedia, via search): independently gives the same six-chapter breakdown (ch.1 perception, 2–4 inference/argumentation, 5 language, 6 fallacies) and the two-object/two-pramāṇa scheme. **Signal 3** (Encyclopedia of Buddhism, "Dignāga"): dates c. 480–540 CE; founder of Buddhist logic; student of Vasubandhu; the Pramāṇasamuccaya as his mature work restricting knowledge to perception and inference; apoha ("a word indicates an object merely through the exclusion of other objects"); Dharmakīrti's later elaboration.

Signals 1 and 2 are genuinely independent sites and agree precisely on structure and doctrine; Signal 3 (same site as 1, but a different article) supplies the biography and dating. Medium, not high: the Pramāṇasamuccaya verses were **not fetched verbatim** (and the Sanskrit is itself a scholarly reconstruction from Tibetan — a transmission ceiling §4 flags); the doctrinal content here overlaps with, and partly re-uses, the already-written [[dignaga-pramana]].
