## Gloss

**Upekkhā** (Pāli; Sanskrit **upekṣā**) is **equanimity** — "a perfect, unshakable balance of mind, rooted in insight" (Nyanaponika). It is the **fourth and culminating *brahmavihāra*** and fourth of the four ***appamaññā*** (immeasurables). Upekkhā holds the other three in balance: it is neither the warmth of mettā nor the ache of karuṇā nor the gladness of muditā, but the even regard that meets all beings — pleasant, unpleasant, neutral — without grasping or aversion, recognising that beings are heirs to their own *kamma*.

**Crucial distinction.** Upekkhā is explicitly **not** cold detachment. This is its near enemy:
- **Far enemy:** agitation / craving-and-aversion (reactivity) — the restless pull-and-push it transcends.
- **Near enemy:** **indifference**, apathy, or callous disengagement from others' welfare — a counterfeit calm that *looks* like equanimity but is really not caring. Genuine upekkhā is "rooted in insight" (into impermanence and not-self); its counterfeit is rooted in not bothering.

Because it rests on wisdom rather than suppression, upekkhā is more than a sublime social attitude: it is also a **factor of awakening** (*upekkhā-bojjhaṅga*) and the mature, non-reactive equanimity that the path develops toward liberation. Its grounding is the realization of *anattā* — when the "I" that would cling or recoil is seen through, balance follows.

**Cross-tradition note (parallel, not identity).** Equanimity-ideals recur across Indian traditions — Jain *mādhyasthya* (indifference toward the incorrigible), Patañjali's *upekṣā* (Yoga-Sūtra 1.33), the Bhagavad Gītā's *samatva* ("equanimity is called yoga," BG 2.48). These occupy a parallel structural slot, but each is embedded in a different goal (detached action / kaivalya / liberation from rebirth) and should not be flattened into one concept.
