## Gloss / Divergence map

**Sūrya** is the Hindu **sun deity** — among the oldest continuously-worshipped gods, present already in the **Ṛgveda** (composed ~1500–1000 BCE; RV 1.115 hymns the rising sun as dispeller of darkness and empowerer of knowledge). He is counted among the **Ādityas** (solar deities, children of Aditi) and named also **Savitṛ** ("the Impeller/Nourisher") and **Bhāskara** ("light-maker").

**Iconography.** Sūrya crosses the sky daily in a **golden chariot drawn by seven horses** (variously read as the seven colours of light or the days of the week), driven by the red charioteer **Aruṇa** (a personification of dawn). He typically holds lotuses and wears a crown; the *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* (Varāhamihira, ~6th c. CE) specifies two-handed forms, later art four-handed.

**Religious place.** In the **Smārta Pañcāyatana pūjā** Sūrya is one of the five deities (with Viṣṇu, Śiva, Devī, Gaṇeśa) held as **equivalent means to realising Brahman** — a key point: in this framing the sun-god is a chosen face of the one ultimate, not a competing absolute. The **Gāyatrī mantra** addresses Savitṛ, binding solar illumination to spiritual awakening. The apex of Saura architecture is the **Konārk Sun Temple** (Odisha, 13th c. CE) — the whole building a stone chariot with twelve wheel-pairs and seven horses.

**Decline.** Independent Sūrya worship peaked in the medieval period and waned after ~13th c.; the deity was increasingly absorbed into Vaiṣṇava (Sūrya-Nārāyaṇa) and Śaiva contexts rather than worshipped alone.
