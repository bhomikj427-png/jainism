## Gloss / Divergence map

**Cakrapāṇidatta** (**fl. c. 1040–1070 CE**), a Bengali physician of the Pāla period, is the author of the ***Āyurvedadīpikā***, "the most famous among the commentators of Caraka Saṃhitā" and the commentary through which the *Caraka Saṃhitā* has been read ever since — every modern edition of Caraka is printed *with* it. He also wrote the ***Bhānumatī*** on the [[sushruta|Suśruta Saṃhitā]], the ***Cikitsāsaṅgraha*** (better known as the ***Cakradatta***, a practical therapeutics compendium that became a standard text in its own right), a ***Dravyaguṇasaṅgraha***, and a lexicon, the *Śabdacandrikā*. His father was **Nārāyaṇa** and his elder brother **Bhānudatta**; his teacher was **Naradatta**.

### He is the best-dated person in this corpus, and the reason is instructive

Cakrapāṇi's father and brother were **in the service of the Gauḍa king Nayapāla**, whose reign historians fix at **1040–1070 CE**. That is an *external* anchor: a regnal chronology established from inscriptions and dynastic records, entirely independent of the medical tradition's own account of itself. Compare what the same session's other nodes had to work with — [[sarvajnatman]] dated by arithmetic on a discipleship, [[prakasatman]] swinging three centuries with no source arguing for either end, [[govinda-bhagavatpada]] attested only by the lineage that requires him.

The difference is not that Āyurvedic scholarship is more careful than Vedāntic scholarship. It is that **Cakrapāṇi's family worked at a court, and courts leave records.** A maṭha lineage documents itself; a dynasty is documented by others. Where a figure's life touches an institution with independent record-keeping, the dating problem largely dissolves — and where it does not, no amount of textual skill recovers it. The corpus keeps this node as the **positive control** for the dating problem it has been mapping all batch.

### The finding: what Ch 31 flagged, resolved — but not in the direction it expected

Ch 31 §4.2 recorded an honest caveat: the three-*nyāya* scheme of tissue-nourishment (*kṣīra-dadhi*, *kedārī-kulyā*, *khale-kapota*) was credited to Cakrapāṇidatta **on a single derivative practitioner-facing source** carrying no *śloka* citations. A peer-reviewed review in the Āyurvedic academic press (Sharma et al., *IJRAR* 2024), read in full with its endnotes, settles the matter — and corrects the picture in three ways at once.

**1. The scheme is not three theories but four**, and the fourth belongs to a different commentator entirely:

| nyāya | image | the source the review cites |
|---|---|---|
| **kṣīra-dadhi** — complete transformation | milk wholly becomes curd; each dhātu wholly becomes the next | Caraka with **Cakrapāṇi's Āyurvedadīpikā**, Cikitsāsthāna 15:20 |
| **kedārī-kulyā** — transmission | field-plots irrigated in sequence from one channel | **Suśruta**, who "utilized this Nyāya to describe *Sirās*"; Su. Sū. 14 |
| **khale-kapota** — selectivity | pigeons flying from all directions to one threshing-floor and each taking what it needs | Caraka with **Cakrapāṇi**, Sū./Ci. Grahaṇī-cikitsā |
| **eka-kāla dhātu-poṣaṇa** — simultaneity | all dhātus fed at once, *rasa* distributed by *vyāna-vāyu* with the heart's action | **Aruṇadatta**, the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* commentator (*Sarvāṅgasundarā*) — "Astanga Hridaya commentator, Arunadatta, advanced this view" |

So the scheme is **assembled across the commentarial traditions of all three saṃhitās**, not authored by one man. Cakrapāṇi is the anchor for the Caraka-side exposition — which is what the practitioner sources compress into "credited to Cakrapāṇidatta" — and that compression drops Suśruta and Aruṇadatta.

**2. The enumeration itself is modern.** The review's footnote for "there are four theories" (note iii) cites neither Caraka nor Cakrapāṇi but ***Āyurvedīya Kriyā Śārīra* by Vd. Ranajitrai Desai** (Baidyanath, 2017) — a contemporary physiology textbook. The *images* and their classical loci are ancient and well cited; **the numbered list of nyāyas as a set of rival theories is a modern pedagogical taxonomy** laid over them. This is a distinction the corpus is built to keep, and it is exactly the shape §0 warns about in reverse: not a modern concept read into an old text, but a **modern organising scheme mistaken for an old one**. Both errors have the same mechanism.

**3. And it puts a live dispute into the corpus.** [[dhatu]] records the three nyāyas as **incompatible mechanisms transmitted simultaneously** — "contested by design, not by scholarly disagreement." The IJRAR review says the opposite in as many words: "the Dhātu Poṣaṇa Nyāya function as a cohesive group… Every law is significant and equal to every other law… they do not conflict or interfere with one another," with *eka-kāla* named as the frame in which the other three run at once. The corpus does **not** adjudicate this, and does not quietly retire the older finding. Both readings are on the table, and which one is right depends on a question neither source asks: whether the classical texts ever presented these as *alternatives* at all, or whether that framing is part of the same modern taxonomy that numbered them.

### An objection, and the shape of a real commentarial argument

The review also preserves the internal debate rather than only the doctrine, which is worth recording because it shows the mechanism being *used*: some ācāryas object to *kṣīra-dadhi* that if transformation were total, a month's fasting would leave the body in *śukrakṣaya* with no intermediate dhātus left — all rasa having become rakta, all rakta māṃsa, and so on, with nothing held back. **[[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]]'s** answer is a three-way division of each dhātu's *pariṇāma*: a *sūkṣmabhāga* building the *upadhātu* and the next dhātu, a *malabhāga* becoming that tissue's waste, and a *sthūlabhāga* nourishing the dhātu itself — so transformation is **partial**, and the objection dissolves. The reporting source also records that the **time** for a full rasa-to-śukra cycle is itself unsettled across the classical citations — six days, twenty-four hours, or thirty days — and makes it depend on [[agni-ayurveda|agni]].

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Sharma, Sajeesh, Meena & Rai, "A Physiological Review on Dhātu Poṣaṇa Nyāya," *IJRAR* 11(1), Jan. 2024** — **read in full, including the endnote apparatus**, which is what made the finding possible: the four-nyāya enumeration and its *modern* textbook source; the per-nyāya classical citations naming Cakrapāṇi, Suśruta and Aruṇadatta separately; Ḍalhaṇa's three-part *pariṇāma* answer; the disputed transformation-time; the explicit "cohesive group" reading. Authors are National Institute of Ayurveda (Jaipur) faculty/scholars — an inside-the-tradition academic source, and read as such.
2. **"Commentators of Caraka Saṃhitā," in *History of Indian Medicine and Ayurveda*, via Wisdomlib** — the biography: 1040–1070 CE via Nayapāla; father Nārāyaṇa, brother Bhānudatta, teacher Naradatta; the Birbhum origin and the Cakrapāṇīśvara temple; the works-list; and the assessment that the *Āyurvedadīpikā* "superseded earlier commentators."
3. **Wikipedia, "Chakrapani Datta"; Hindupedia, "Cakrapāṇidatta"; Wisdomlib's definition entry** — independent restatements of the same 11th-century Bengali placement and the two-commentary pairing (*Āyurvedadīpikā* on Caraka, *Bhānumatī* on Suśruta). Consistent, and adding nothing the monograph does not.
4. **Bibliographic signals** — the *Āyurvedadīpikā* is in print in the standard Jādavjī Trikamjī Ācārya edition (Chaukhambha) and in the Sengupta five-volume Sanskrit edition alongside Gaṅgādhara's *Jalpakalpataru*; a 2024 critical English edition of the *Cakradatta* exists. **None was read**; they are recorded as the upgrade path.
5. **Corpus cross-check** — [[charaka]], [[sushruta]], [[dhatu]], [[mala]], [[agni-ayurveda]], [[vagbhata]] (Batch 42), and Ch 31 §4.2's caveat, which is what this node was written to answer.

**CONVERGED on the man** — date, place, family, patron-dynasty, teacher and works are stated identically by four independent sources and rest on an external regnal anchor. **The contested element is the *nyāya* scheme, not Cakrapāṇidatta**, and the file marks that boundary rather than letting one uncertainty colour the other. Confidence **medium**: no work of his was read in any edition, so everything doctrinal here is reported at second hand through a 2024 review that cites him by sthāna and verse — a real improvement on Ch 31's uncited practitioner site, but not the *Āyurvedadīpikā* itself. Confidence is **not** raised to high because the sources agreeing on the biography are reference works of a single type, and because the one substantive doctrinal source is a single paper.
