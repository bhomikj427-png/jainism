## Gloss / Divergence map

**Dharmakīrti** (fl. ~6th–7th c. CE; associated with **Nālandā**) is, with his predecessor **Dignāga**, the co-founder of the Buddhist **"Epistemological School"** (*pramāṇavāda*) — the most logically rigorous wing of Indian Buddhist philosophy and its principal weapon in debate against Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and Jain rivals. His **Pramāṇavārttika** ("Commentary on Valid Cognition"), formally a *vārttika* on Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, is the magnum opus of the tradition; his **seven treatises** became the core epistemology curriculum of Tibetan monastic education.

**Dates are genuinely contested** — this is itself a finding, not a gap:

| scholar | proposed dates | basis |
|---|---|---|
| Frauwallner | 600–660 CE | classic reference dating |
| Krasser | mid-6th c. | links with Bhāviveka and Kumārila |
| Balcerowicz | c. 550–610 CE | influence on the Jain Samantabhadra |

**Core doctrines (refinements of Dignāga, not departures):**
- **Two pramāṇas only** — *pratyakṣa* (non-conceptual perception of particulars) and *anumāna* (conceptual inference). No śabda, no upamāna (against Nyāya's four).
- **Radical nominalism**: only **svalakṣaṇa** — unique, momentary particulars with **causal efficacy** (*arthakriyā*) — are ultimately real; universals (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa*) are conceptual fictions. "Whatever has causal power, really exists."
- **Apoha re-grounded causally**: he reinterprets Dignāga's "exclusion" theory of meaning through a causal chain from particulars → perceptions → concepts, rather than mere double-negation.
- **Momentariness** defended by the *sattvānumāna* ("inference from existence"): whatever exists is momentary, since nothing produces new effects while remaining unchanged.
- **Svasaṃvedana**: all consciousness is reflexively self-aware ("auto-illuminated").
