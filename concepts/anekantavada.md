## Gloss / Divergence map

**Philological caution:** The term *anekāntavāda* does not appear in the earliest texts considered canonical by the Śvetāmbara tradition (Wikipedia, citing Jain sources). The *Tattvārthasūtra* provides the foundational naya (standpoint/partial perspective) doctrine — especially TS 1.6 "pramāṇanayaiḥ" (by valid cognitions and standpoints) — on which anekāntavāda is built; but the named, formulated doctrine emerges in post-TS authors: Samantabhadra (*Āptamīmāṃsā*, ~2nd c.), Siddhasena Divākar (~5th c., credited with coining the term), Mallavādin (~5th–6th c., first formulator of *saptabhaṅgī*), and Akalanka (~8th c., called "Master of Jain logic"). The divergence map below covers the contested readings scholars hold; the disagreements themselves are the finding.

**TS foundation — now independently triangulated (2026-06-10).** The sūtra grounding the doctrine, *arpitānarpitasiddheḥ*, is confirmed in **two genuinely independent critical editions**:
- **Vijay K. Jain 2018** (Digambara recension, numbered **5.32**, with Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi*): "The seemingly contradictory attributes are established from the points of view, whether primary or secondary." Pūjyapāda glosses *arpita* = the attribute under consideration (primary/emphasized), *anarpita* = attributes present but not currently emphasized (secondary), with the Devadatta example (simultaneously father, son, brother — different standpoints, no contradiction) and the sāmānya/viśeṣa application: substance permanent qua general qualities, impermanent qua specific modes.
- **Tatia 1994** (Śvetāmbara-aligned recension, numbered **5.31**, p. 184; draws on Umāsvāti's bhāṣya + Siddhasenagaṇi + Pūjyapāda): sūtra confirmed present via Archive.org full-text search; Tatia's exposition of the sevenfold predication follows at pp. 187–188 ("Now the three predicates of a real, (1) that it exists, (2) that it does not exist and (3) that it is inexpressible, become seven predicates…" — pen example). Tatia renders TS 1.6 as "The categories can be understood with greater accuracy through the approved means of knowledge and the philosophical standpoints" (p. 56).
- **Recension note:** the verse is 5.31 (Śvetāmbara/Tatia) vs 5.32 (Digambara/Jain). Tatia's edition also shows the naya-count recension split at 1.34/1.33: five nayas in the main Śvetāmbara reading ("common person's view, generic view, practical view, linear view and literal view"), seven in the variant — matching the Digambara seven of TS 1.33.

The two editions use different recensions, different commentarial bases, and independent translators 24 years apart; their convergence on "apparently contradictory attributes are established relative to standpoint/emphasis" is genuine independent agreement **for the TS-foundation layer**. The doctrine's *interpretation* (below) remains contested.

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-one-sidedness** (negative, polemical): the doctrine's work is *rejecting* any single ekānta (absolutist) view; what it affirms beyond that is implicit | Paul Dundas, *The Jains* (Routledge, 2002): "a fearsome weapon of philosophical polemic"; Mookerjee: "non-absolutism"; Sanghvi: "non-extremism" | Anekāntavāda is primarily a dialectical tool, not a positive metaphysical claim; the plurality is not in reality but in the inadequacy of single-perspective claims |
| **Many-sidedness** (positive, ontological): reality is genuinely multifaceted; the doctrine is a positive claim about the structure of existence | Most popular translations ("many-sidedness"); Charitrapragya; general Jain tradition presentation; TS 5.31/5.32 with Pūjyapāda's sāmānya/viśeṣa gloss supports an ontological ground | The doctrine asserts that substances (dravya) have genuinely simultaneous, contradictory-seeming attributes (permanent/impermanent, one/many) and philosophy must acknowledge all of them |
| **Epistemological locus**: anekāntavāda concerns the limits of finite knowing — no finite perspective grasps the whole; only kevala-jñāna (omniscience) can | Anil Mundra (U. Alabama, 2022, barefield.ua.edu): "epistemic respect for the view of others"; John Koller: "not relativism — it means epistemological respect"; Matilal: "syādvāda is not a theory of uncertainty, doubt or relative probabilities"; Tatia 1994 intro: "all knowledge, except omniscience, is only partial truth from a particular viewpoint" | The doctrine is about human cognitive limitation, not about metaphysical indeterminacy; omniscience is in principle attainable |
| **Methodological/hermeneutical locus**: a systematic framework for incorporating partial perspectives in philosophical argument | Samantabhadra's *Āptamīmāṃsā* v. 105 (via Mundra 2022): syādvāda "illuminates substances" indirectly where kevala-jñāna illuminates directly | Anekāntavāda is a procedure for philosophy, not a doctrine about reality's ultimate structure |
| **syāt = "maybe/perhaps"** (skeptical reading, based on general Sanskrit optative usage) | Early Western translators; **Monier-Williams** glosses *anekāntavāda* as "scepticism"; **Goldstücker**: "Scepticism, maintaining that nothing is certain" (both via wisdomlib lexicon aggregation, fetched 2026-06-10) | Anekāntavāda entails skepticism and relativism — no proposition unequivocally holds; this commits to the view that Jainism cannot make any definite truth-claims. **The 19th-c. lexicon glosses are themselves artifacts of this reading** — etymology (an-eka-anta = "not one-sided") supports non-onesidedness, not scepticism; lexicon is a clue, not a verdict |
| **syāt = "in a certain respect / conditionally"** (standpoint-relative reading) | Bimal K. Matilal (*The Central Philosophy of Jainism*, 1981): "conditional yes or conditional approval"; John Koller; Mundra 2022; most contemporary scholars; directly consonant with Pūjyapāda's arpita/anarpita gloss on TS 5.32 (fetched) | *Syāt* parameterizes an assertion to a defined standpoint (naya); the doctrine is perspectival, not skeptical; Jain omniscience claims remain coherent |
| **Tolerance / pluralism / intellectual ahiṃsā** (modern ethical-political reading) | Matilal (following Dhruva, Kapadia): "intellectual ahiṃsā [nonviolence]" and "toleration"; Ganeri: "syncretism" | The doctrine is an ethics of epistemic tolerance; widely criticized as importing modern liberal values into an ancient polemical tradition |
| **Quantum complementarity parallel** | D.S. Kothari, "The Complementarity Principle and Eastern Philosophy," *Niels Bohr Centenary Volume*, Harvard UP; various popularizers; ResearchGate (2023): "anekāntavāda = complementarity" | Bohr's complementarity (measurement-context-dependent quantum properties) is structurally mapped onto syāt-predication; **scholars consider this an anachronistic overlay** — see conflation link below |

### Saptabhaṅga formal structure (the seven bhaṅgas vs. modern logics)

The sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī) is the post-TS formalization of anekāntavāda's standpoint-qualified assertion (first systematized by Mallavādin, ~5th–6th c.; see saptabhangi.md). Tatia 1994 (pp. 187–188) presents it combinatorially: three base predicates of any real — exists, does not exist, inexpressible — generate seven forms. Modern logicians have proposed formal reconstructions: **Ganeri 2002** and **Priest 2008** (using many-valued and modal techniques) read it as supporting non-classical logic; **Balcerowicz contests the non-classical reading** (dispute documented in Rahlwes 2023). Per the project's edge-pairing rule, every row below is `structurally-parallel-to` **paired with** `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` — a real parallel, logged with its break. A forced fit logged as broken beats a clean one asserted.

| # | bhaṅga | proposed formal analog (who) | where the analogy breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *syād asty eva* — in some respect, it certainly is | designated value **T**; assertion of p (classical) or parameterized/modal assertion (Priest 2008) | classical T is unconditional; *syāt* indexes the assertion to a standpoint (substance/place/time/state). Strip the index and the bhaṅga is no longer what the texts assert — the *eva* ("certainly") shows full commitment *within* the parameter, not partial commitment without it |
| 2 | *syān nāsty eva* — in some respect, it certainly is not | falsity **F**; classical negation ¬p | the denial is exclusion relative to a *different* parameter (the pot does not exist qua clay-of-another-pot), not the contradictory of bhaṅga 1; classical ¬p cannot be co-asserted with p, while bhaṅgas 1+2 are jointly and fully assertible without revision |
| 3 | *syād asty eva syān nāsty eva* — both, successively | dialetheia **p ∧ ¬p**; paraconsistent logic (Priest 2008 reads the Jains as proto-paraconsistentists) | the two conjuncts carry different standpoint indices, so no *single* disambiguated proposition is both true and false; the Matilal line reads this as contradiction-*avoidance* by disambiguation — the opposite of dialetheism's contradiction-*acceptance*; Balcerowicz contests the paraconsistent reading (via Rahlwes 2023) |
| 4 | *syād avaktavyam* — in some respect, inexpressible (both at once) | third truth-value: Łukasiewicz **½**, Belnap glut **{T,F}** or gap **{ }** | *avaktavya* is not an intermediate degree, not "unknown," and not a value in an algebra — it marks an expressibility failure: simultaneous co-predication has no admissible sentence; Rahlwes 2023 reads it as enjoined silence; Tatia presents it as a third *predicate* generated combinatorially, not a third truth-value |
| 5 | *syād asty avaktavyam* — is, and inexpressible | composite value (e.g. ordered pair ⟨T, glut⟩) in a product algebra | the seven forms have no truth-functional connectives defined over them; the "combination" is sequential qualified predication, not value-composition under an operator |
| 6 | *syān nāsty avaktavyam* — is not, and inexpressible | composite ⟨F, glut⟩ | same break as #5 |
| 7 | *syād asty nāsty avaktavyam* — is, is not, and inexpressible | top of a 7-element value-lattice (Priest's seven-valued construction) | the seven are exhaustive *kinds of qualified assertion*, not a closed value-space: no consequence relation, no inference rules over the seven are defined in the classical texts — the scheme governs assertion, not entailment |

**Two cross-cutting breaks:** (a) **fuzzy logic** — reading *syāt* as a partial degree of truth fails because each bhaṅga is asserted as *fully and certainly* true from its standpoint (*eva*); Matilal: syādvāda is "not a theory of uncertainty, doubt or relative probabilities." (b) **many-valued logic generally** — a many-valued logic evaluates one proposition in one context against many values; saptabhaṅgī evaluates one sentence across many parameterized contexts with two classical outcomes plus an expressibility-failure marker (see many-valued-logic.md).

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Wikipedia (Anekāntavāda article):** Cites Matilal, Koller, Dundas, Charitrapragya, Mookerjee, Ganeri, Sanghvi, Balcerowicz. Summary: scholarly consensus firmly rejects the skeptical reading of *syāt* (standpoint-relative reading prevails); negative/positive formulation divide persists; no consensus on epistemological vs. ontological vs. methodological locus. Provenance: aggregated; individual citations need primary-source verification.

**Signal 2 — Anil Mundra, globalcritical.barefield.ua.edu (2022):** Independently presents the three-register analysis (ontological + semantic + epistemological); confirms standpoint-relative *syāt*; cites Samantabhadra's *Āptamīmāṃsā* v. 105 directly. Provenance: academic web essay, U. Alabama; not peer-reviewed — medium weight.

**Signal 3 — New World Encyclopedia entry:** Confirms *syāt* = "in some ways" / "from a perspective"; derivative — not independently sourced.

**Signal 4 — Philological signal (Śvetāmbara canonicity):** Term absent from early Śvetāmbara canon; attributed to Siddhasena Divākar. This weakens any reading that treats anekāntavāda as a single, unified TS doctrine rather than a post-TS systematisation of TS nayas. **Strengthened 2026-06-10:** Tatia's own edition shows the recension-level instability of the naya list itself (5 vs 7 at TS 1.33/1.34), confirming the doctrine-cluster was still in formation around the TS.

**Signal 5 — Quantum/physics comparison:** D.S. Kothari cited as academic originator. The critique stands on conceptual analysis (measurement-exclusivity ≠ standpoint-relative predication); see quantum-complementarity.md where the analogy is defused in detail. Flagged `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent`.

**Signal 6 — Tatia 1994 directly accessed (NEW, 2026-06-10):** Via Archive.org full-text search of the scanned edition: TS 1.6 rendering (p. 56), *arpita-nārpita-siddheḥ* present as 5.31 (p. 184), sevenfold predication exposition with pen example (pp. 187–188), naya recension split (pp. 71). This is the first direct access to Tatia's chapter-level text in the project (previous sessions only reached front matter).

**Signal 7 — Vijay K. Jain 2018, TS 5.32 fetched (NEW, 2026-06-10):** Full sūtra, translation, and Pūjyapāda's arpita/anarpita commentary with Devadatta example and sāmānya/viśeṣa application (WisdomLib).

**Independence assessment (updated):** Signals 6 and 7 are **genuinely independent** — different recensions (Śvetāmbara vs Digambara), different commentarial bases (Bhāṣya + Siddhasenagaṇi vs Sarvārthasiddhi), independent translators (1994/2018). They converge on the TS-foundation gloss: apparently contradictory attributes are established relative to standpoint/emphasis. The *interpretive* divergence map (rows above) still rests substantially on Wikipedia-aggregated attributions — Matilal 1981 and Dundas 2002 have not been fetched in their own text.

**Signal 8 — Lexicon/morphology (NEW, 2026-06-10):** Monier-Williams: *anekāntavāda* = "scepticism"; Goldstücker: "Scepticism, maintaining that nothing is certain." Morphology: an- (not) + eka (one) + anta (end/side) + vāda (doctrine) = "doctrine of non-one-sidedness." The MW gloss is evidence *about the 19th-c. Western reading*, not about the doctrine — it documents where the skeptical-reading row comes from. Etymology supports non-onesidedness; lexicon treated as clue, not verdict (§4.3).

**Signal 9 — Formalization dispute (NEW, 2026-06-10):** Ganeri 2002 and Priest 2008 (both *History and Philosophy of Logic*) propose non-classical formal reconstructions of saptabhaṅgī; Balcerowicz contests the non-classical reading (dispute summarized in Rahlwes 2023, *Journal of Indian Philosophy* — abstract-level access only, full text paywalled). The formalization itself is therefore *contested among logicians*, which independently supports keeping the cross-tradition logic links typed as parallel-NOT-equivalent rather than identity.

**Where this file still leans on a single source / inference:** (1) The divergence-map attributions to Matilal, Dundas, Koller, Mookerjee remain Wikipedia-mediated — not independently fetched. (2) The Samantabhadra *Āptamīmāṃsā* v. 105 citation rests on Mundra alone. (3) The per-bhaṅga break-analysis in the table is conceptual synthesis grounded in the cited formal literature but the Priest/Ganeri papers themselves were verified by title/abstract, not read in full. **Confidence therefore stays medium**; status stays contested — the interpretive split is the finding, and it is now better-evidenced, not resolved.
