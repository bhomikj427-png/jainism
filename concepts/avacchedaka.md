## Gloss

***Avacchedaka*** — "delimitor" or **limitor** — is the device on which [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]]'s technical language runs. It names a property that **restricts the scope of an occurrence**: not "fire is on the mountain" but "the mountain, *as delimited by the slope*, is a locus of fire."

**Morphology first, as a hard constraint (§4 signal 3).** Monier-Williams derives it from **ava-√chid**, "to cut off." Apte: "separating; determining, deciding; bounding, limiting; distinguishing, particularising," and as a noun "that which distinguishes; a predicate, characteristic property; boundary, limit." Böhtlingk–Roth: *scheidend, unterscheidend*. **The word is not a logician's coinage — it is the ordinary participle "cutting-off," pressed into service.** The correlative *avacchinna*, "delimited," is the passive of the same root: the limited is always limited *by* its limitor. This matters because the technical sense stays inside the etymology: a limitor **cuts a locus down** to the part where the claim holds.

### The problem it solves

Matilal sets it out as a failure of naïve formalisation, and the argument is worth having in full because it is the whole justification for the apparatus.

Take two ordinary sentences:

> (1) "There is oil in sesame seeds" (*tileṣu tailam*)
> (2) "There is fire on a mountain" (*parvate vahniḥ*)

Both rephrase as "x is a locus of y," so both look like instances of one open sentence. Their negation, by Nyāya convention, is "x is a locus of an **absence** of y." The formalisation therefore requires (5) and (6) to be **contradictory**. And for sesame and oil they are. But:

> "the mountain can be a locus of fire **as well as of the absence of fire**."

The mountain has fire on the slope and no fire on the summit. **Both statements are true of the same locus at the same time, and the formalisation makes them a contradiction.** Something has gone wrong.

⚠ **The diagnosis was not Nyāya's own — it was the grammarians'.** Matilal: "The Grammarians noted this distinction by naming the first a **pervasive (*abhivyāpaka*) locus** and the second a **partitive (*aikadeśika*) locus**," citing **Bhartṛhari, *Vākyapadīya* III, "Adhikaraṇādhikāra," verses 2–3, with Helārāja's commentary.** Navya-Nyāya's most characteristic technical device has its ancestry in **Sanskrit grammar**, not in logic. The corresponding property-side terms are ***vyāpya-vṛtti*** (pervasive occurrence — cow-ness is in every part of every cow) and ***avyāpya-vṛtti*** (non-pervasive, partial occurrence).

⚠ **A structural gap this exposes, and no edge is drawn to paper over it.** The corpus has **no node for Bhartṛhari and none for the *Vākyapadīya*** — no node for the Sanskrit grammatical tradition at all. The obvious nearby target, `sabda-pramana`, is about **testimony as a source of knowledge**, not about grammatical semantics, and an edge to it would misstate the relation. **Recorded as a gap rather than smoothed over with a loose link.**

**The repair.** Navya-Nyāya keeps the formal relation between the two schemata and changes the *rephrasing*:

> (7) "The mountain **as delimited by the slope** is a locus of a body of fire" — ***nitambāvacchedena parvate vahniḥ***

(7) is no longer structurally parallel to the sesame case, so the two do not collapse into one schema, and the contradiction never forms. **A limitor of this original kind is simply a spatial part of the locus.**

The canonical illustration is the **monkey and the tree** — and both of the corpus's independent specialist sources give it, one in Sanskrit:

> ***śākhāvacchedena vṛkṣaḥ kapisaṃyogī, na tu mūlāvacchedena*** — "the tree, as delimited by the branch, is in contact with the monkey; not as delimited by the root." (Matilal, quoting the standard usage and citing **Jagadīśa**'s *Tattvacintāmaṇi-dīdhiti* commentary)

SEP's Gaṅgeśa entry reaches the same case independently as "monkey-conjunction in a tree"; Ganeri gives it in English as *kapi-saṃyoga* and draws the consequence: "Nyāya avoids the threatened violation of the law of non-contradiction by **relativising the notion of occurrence**."

⚠⚠ **This is the point the map must teach, and the reason for this file's two `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edges.** Faced with "the tree both is and is not in contact with the monkey," Navya-Nyāya does **not** weaken the law of non-contradiction, and does **not** introduce a third truth-value or a degree of truth. It **denies that there is a contradiction**, on the ground that the two occurrences have different delimitors and therefore were never about the same thing. Same puzzle as motivates paraconsistent and many-valued logics; **opposite repair**.

### The three extensions

The device did not stay spatial.

1. **Temporal.** The later school treats a body as having a temporal stretch as it has a spatial one (a segment of *mahākāla*, called *kālopādhi*). A mango is green at one time and red at another; "x as limited by time t₁ is a locus of y" and "x as limited by time t₂ is a locus of ~y" are both true, without inconsistency. **The same move, applied to a second dimension.**
2. **Individuating cognitions.** This is what the limitor is really *for*, and it is where the device stops looking like a patch and starts looking like the point of the system. Matilal's example: a blue cup is in front of me, and I may cognise —
   *"The cup is blue" · "The utensil is blue" · "The cup is coloured" · "The substance is blue" · "The substance is coloured."*
   **The objects involved are identical in all five.** Nyāya nonetheless holds these to be five *distinct* cognitions, and can say why only by appeal to limitors: the qualificand-ness is limited by cup-ness in the first and third, by utensil-ness in the second, by substance-ness in the fourth and fifth; the qualifier-ness by blue-ness in the first, second and fourth, by colour-ness in the third and fifth. **Different combination, different cognition.** This confirms from a first-hand source what the secondary literature asserts — that the concept of limitor "was necessitated by Navya-Nyāya's goal of describing **cognitions**." The analysandum is a mental episode, not a sentence.
3. **Limiting relations.** Specifying the limiting *property* is not enough; one must also specify the **limiting relation** (conjunction, inherence, and the rest). Two relational abstracts of the same type with the same limiting property but different limiting relations "were also ruled to be distinct by Nyāya."

Matilal also distinguishes two limitor types by which side of the relation they qualify: ***niṣṭhatva-sambandhāvacchinna*** (a property of the locus) and ***nirūpitatva-sambandhāvacchinna*** (a property of the conditioner), citing **Ingalls, *Materials*, p. 50**.

### What "limitorship" is — four rival definitions, and Raghunātha's preference

*Avacchedakatva* (limitorship) and its converse *avacchedyatva* (limitedness) are themselves defined, and not uniquely. Matilal lists four accounts in circulation:

| # | account | Sanskrit tag |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | limitorship is a ***svarūpa*** relation — i.e. **unanalysable** | — |
| (ii) | a limitor is **not narrower** in extension than the limited property | *anyūna-vṛtti*, "occurring in no less" |
| (iii) | a limitor is **neither wider nor narrower** | *anyūnānatirikta-vṛtti* |
| (iv) | a limitor is **not wider** in extension than the limited property | *anatirikta-vṛtti*, "occurring in no more" |

**Alternatives (i) and (iv) "were largely favored in many contexts by [[raghunatha-siromani|Raghunātha]]"** — who wrote a dedicated treatment, the ***Avacchedakatva-nirukti***, "Definition of Limitorship." ⚠ Note that (i) and (iv) are of different kinds: (i) says the relation cannot be analysed, (iv) analyses it. That one man favoured both "in many contexts" is recorded as Matilal reports it and is **not** smoothed into a single position here.

### The honest comparison with quantification

Matilal's chapter is titled *"The Limitors and Quantificational Logic,"* so the comparison is his, not this file's — which is exactly why it can be stated precisely.

**What the limitor does that a quantifier does.** To say *all* pots are absent from a place, Nyāya does not quantify; it **delimits an abstract**: ***ghaṭatvāvacchinna-pratiyogitā***, "a counterpositive-ness delimited by pot-ness." Matilal: "In this way, generality of statements was maintained." And the delimitation **scopes**: if the limitor is pot-ness alone the absence concerns all pots; if it includes pot-ness *and* blue-colour, it concerns only blue pots. That is restricted quantification, performed by adding a limitor. Matilal adds that "the commentators upon Gaṅgeśa suggested expansion of his definition of pervasion along this line."

Matilal then does the translation explicitly, rendering (ii), (iii) and (iv) with quantifiers over an occurrence predicate *Oxy* ("x occurs in y") — (ii) as ∀z(Oyz ⊃ Oxz), (iii) as ∀z(Oyz ≡ Oxz), (iv) as ∀z(Oxz ⊃ Oyz) — and draws the conclusion that matters:

> account (i) "holds limitor-ship to be an **unanalyzable** relational property. But (ii), (iii), and (iv) hold that it is **definable in terms of the 'primitive' occurrence relation**."

⚠⚠ **An independent convergence, and the most valuable single result in this file.** Ganeri, writing decades later and about a different problem (Gaṅgeśa's definition of *vyāpti*), asks why the Naiyāyikas did not simply use universal quantification and answers: "they were in fact trying to **define** this notion, and to do so only in terms of certain other notions which they took to be primitive, especially the notion of **co-location and absence**." **Two specialists, independently, on two different technical problems, reach the same structural claim: Navya-Nyāya analyses generality into a primitive occurrence-in-a-locus relation.** This is what §4 calls convergence, and it is the corpus's warrant for saying anything at all about Navya-Nyāya and logic.

**And the limit, from the editor of the volume.** [[udayana|D. H. H. Ingalls]]'s introduction warns the expositor to "translate from system to system" — to render "arguments that are phrased in an **intensional logic of abstractions** into arguments phrased in an **extensional logic of classes and propositions**" — while insisting he "should warn the reader of **ultimate incompatibilities**." His own illustration is exact:

> "in this system which presents a **hierarchy of abstractions rather than a hierarchy of classes** it is only by the use of negation that universal laws can be framed. There is an absence of occurrence of humanity in such a locus as is not a locus of mortality. That is to say, all men are mortal."

**"All men are mortal" is not a sentence about a class of men. It is a claim about where a property fails to occur.** That is the whole difference, stated by the man who founded the Western study of the school.

**Signal 6 — search-level aggregation** on Navya-Nyāya technical language: "limitorship (*avacchedakatā*) ... crucial to understanding Navya-nyāya discussion of important relational abstracts, including content-ness (*viṣayatā*) and counterpositive-ness (*pratiyogitā*)"; the *pratiyogitāvacchedaka*; "the development of ... the concept of limitor (*avacchedaka*) was necessitated by Navya-Nyāya's goal of **describing cognitions**"; and that Raghunātha "made [limitorship] an important tool" — all four independently confirmed above by signal 1.

**Independence.** Signals 1, 3 and 4 are three authors, three decades apart, writing for three different purposes, and they agree on the problem, the canonical example, and the solution. Signal 2 is in the same physical volume as signal 1 but is a different author making a different point, and it is the only source that states the *limit* of the comparison. Signal 5 is a genuinely independent kind of evidence (lexicography) and constrains rather than confirms. Signal 6 turned out to be a weak restatement of what signals 1–4 give properly, and is credited with nothing on its own.

⚠ **One source deliberately not leaned on.** The *Cubical Type Theoretic Navya-Nyāya* paper glosses *avacchedaka* as "binding into the type of the relation it qualifies," which is a **modern reconstruction in a modern formalism**, not a report of what the school said. Its historical reporting is used in `navya-nyaya.md`; its type-theoretic gloss is **not** used here. Reading the limitor as a type-binder is precisely the kind of quiet modern mapping §0 forbids.

**Why converged.** Every signal agrees on what a limitor is, what problem it solves, which example is canonical, and how it relates to quantification — and the two independent statements of the *relation to quantification* (Matilal's "definable in terms of the primitive occurrence relation," Ganeri's "co-location and absence") were arrived at from different technical problems. **Why high** — the corpus's rule is `high` only on independent agreement, and this is the clearest case in the Navya-Nyāya layer: a **first-hand reading of the standard monograph**, published by Harvard, corroborated by two later specialists and constrained by six dictionaries. ⚠ **What `high` does not cover**, stated so it cannot be misread: the primary Sanskrit was **not** read (Raghunātha's *Avacchedakatva-nirukti* is known here only as a page-range in Matilal's footnote; the Bhartṛhari verses were not consulted); Ingalls's *Materials* itself was not read, only cited through Matilal; and the two-limitor-type distinction and the four definitions of limitorship rest on Matilal alone.
