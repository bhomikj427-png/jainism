## Gloss / Divergence map

**Pāṇini** is the author of the [[astadhyayi|Aṣṭādhyāyī]] and the first member of the ***munitraya***, the three-sage canon of [[vyakarana|Vyākaraṇa]]. He is the founder of the tradition in the only sense the evidence supports: **the earliest Sanskrit grammarian whose work survives** — not the earliest grammarian, since he himself names ten predecessors ([[vyakarana]]).

Almost nothing biographical is recoverable. Abhyankar is blunt: "Very little is known unfortunately about his native place, parentage or personal history. The account given about these in the *Kathāsaritsāgara* and other books is only **legendary** and hence, it has very little historical value."

### The one date, and how it moved 350 years

⚠ **This is the node's central finding, and it is a finding about method, not about Pāṇini.** The proposals span roughly the 7th to the mid-4th century BCE — and they do not scatter randomly. **They drift steadily later as the evidence used gets more external.**

| dating | who holds it (source) | what the argument rests on |
|---|---|---|
| **6th–7th c. BCE, "if not earlier"** | **Abhyankar**, *Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar* (read at first hand) | "The **internal evidence**, supplied by his work" — an impression of archaism |
| 5th or 6th c. BCE | Scharfe (1977), *per* Wikipedia | "proximity to the language of the Upanishads and Vedic sūtras" — again internal, again impressionistic |
| 6th or 5th c. BCE | Staal (1996), *per* Wikipedia | asserted in passing |
| **no earlier than 400 BCE** | Cardona (1997), *per* Wikipedia | "available evidence strongly supports" — a floor, not a date |
| **mid-4th c. BCE** | **von Hinüber (1989), Falk (1993)**, *per* Wikipedia | **Numismatic.** Pāṇini's rules mention coins — *rūpya*, *niṣka* — "which numismatists date in India to the 4th century BCE" |
| **c. 350 BCE** | Houben (2009), *per* Wikipedia | "c. 350 BCE for Pāṇini is thus based on **unrefuted concrete evidence**" |
| terminus post quem c. 350 BCE | Bronkhorst (2016), *per* Wikipedia | accepts the numismatic floor; places him "just before (or contemporaneously with?) the invasion by Alexander" |

**What commits you to which:** the early datings are *linguistic impressions* of how old the language feels; the late ones are *datable physical objects the text happens to name*. These are not two readings of one body of evidence — **they are two different kinds of evidence, and only one of them can be checked by someone outside the discipline.** The corpus records the numismatic argument as the stronger, without pretending it is settled: Cardona's own caution is that the evidence supports a *floor*.

⚠ **A cross-check the corpus can make on itself.** [[gangesha]] recorded "a date that drifted one way for a century, with every stage still in circulation." Pāṇini is the same pathology at ten times the scale and in the opposite direction — **later**, not earlier. In both cases the older estimate stays in print alongside the newer one, so a reader sampling several reference works gets the whole range and mistakes the spread for a consensus. **This is Batch 46's generalisation holding in a new literature.**

### The ambiguous witnesses

Two much-cited data points are weaker than they look, and both are recorded here as *not* decisive:
- ***yavanānī*** (A 4.1.49) — often read as "Greek script," which would date Pāṇini after Alexander (326 BCE). But Cardona notes it "has been interpreted as referring to either to Old Persian cuneiform, Greek script, or Ionian women." **A word that admits three readings dates nothing.**
- **The Achaemenid setting** — Pāṇini lived in Gandhāra when it was "nominally a satrapy of the Achaemenid Empire," which invites a Persian-contact argument. Wikipedia records the counter in the same breath: "Pāṇini's work shows **no influence from Old Persian**." The geography is real; the inference from it is not.

### ⚠ A name read out of the text — the same mechanism, twice in one batch

Abhyankar reports that **Jinendrabuddhi** (author of the *Nyāsa*) held that Pāṇini's *own name* is derived by Pāṇini in his own sūtra (A 4.3.94) from **Śalātura**, his native town — so that "Pāṇini" means "the man from Śalātura," and the grammarian is identified by an example inside his own grammar.

**Note what has happened.** A later commentator locates the author's biography by reading a proper name out of a rule in the text. This is *structurally identical* to the [[sphota|Sphoṭāyana]] case in this same batch, where Haradatta reads a whole doctrine out of a proper name in A 6.1.123. **Two different commentators, two different centuries, the same move** — and in both cases the corpus's judgement is that the name is evidence of very little. Recorded as a pattern of the commentarial literature, not as a fact about Pāṇini.

### What he actually did

Abhyankar's assessment is worth keeping because it states the *kind* of achievement without inflating it: the Aṣṭādhyāyī "gives an authoritative description of the Sanskrit language," and — the sharp phrase — **"The work is a linguist's and not a language teacher's."** It describes the language as a system; it is not trying to help anyone learn it. Its rules "mainly aim at the correct formation of words," covering "declension, conjugation, euphonic changes, verbal derivatives, noun derivatives and accents."

He worked from **two** bodies of data at once: the accentuation and word-formation of the Vedic corpus, *and* "the spoken Sanskrit language of his time in all its different aspects and shades, noticeable in the various provinces." **Pāṇini's grammar is a description of a living language, not only a scriptural one** — which is why the corpus files [[vyakarana]] as a technical śāstra with a conservative motive rather than as a purely liturgical appendix.

⚠ **On the formal-grammar comparisons — the prime-directive check.** Pāṇini's system has been compared to the Backus–Naur form, to Post rewrite systems, and to a Turing machine; Chomsky has said "the first generative grammar in the modern sense was Pāṇini's grammar." These comparisons are **structural and are labelled as such** ([[astadhyayi]] carries the detail and the honest limits). What must not be smuggled in is the inference that Pāṇini *had* the modern concepts. Cardona supplies the discipline's own brake, against the genealogical claim most often overstated — that Pāṇini shaped modern structural linguistics *through* Saussure. Of Saussure's work Cardona writes that "it shows **no direct influence** of Pāṇinian grammar. Indeed, on occasion, Saussure follows a path that is **contrary** to Pāṇinian procedure." **The resemblance is real and the transmission is not** — which is precisely the distinction §0 exists to keep.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand**, full scan): the 6th–7th c. BCE dating and that it rests on internal evidence; the legendary status of the *Kathāsaritsāgara* material; Jinendrabuddhi on the Śalātura etymology; the ten named predecessors; "a linguist's and not a language teacher's."
2. **Wikipedia, "Pāṇini"** (fetched, independent): the full modern dating literature with scholars attached — von Hinüber, Falk, Houben, Bronkhorst, Cardona, Scharfe, Staal; the *yavanānī* ambiguity; the Achaemenid counter-evidence; the formal-grammar comparisons **and** Cardona's caution.
3. **SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy"** (fetched, independent): Pāṇini c. 400 BCE, and the framing that he "composed his grammar of Sanskrit with a certain notion of Sanskrit as an atemporal language."
4. **Lexicon/morphology**: *pāṇini* is a patronymic/toponymic formation, which is exactly why the Śalātura reading is available — and exactly why it proves nothing.
5. **Internal consistency**: the dating-drift pattern here is checked against [[gangesha]] and [[candrata]], both of which the corpus established independently.

**Contested** because the dating genuinely splits and the split has a methodological cause worth stating. Confidence **medium**: the dating literature is reported at second hand through Wikipedia (the individual monographs were not fetched), though Abhyankar is first-hand and the disagreement between him and the numismatists is directly observed rather than reported.
