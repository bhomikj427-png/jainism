## Gloss / Divergence map

In Buddhist doctrine, the five *skandhas* (Pali: *khandhas*, "heaps/aggregates") are the **five constituent factors that together make up what appears to be a sentient being**. They are:

| # | Sanskrit | Pali | English rendering | nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | rūpa | rūpa | form / material | the four physical elements (solidity/earth, cohesion/water, heat/fire, motion/wind) and their derivatives |
| 2 | vedanā | vedanā | feeling-tone | pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral quality of any experience |
| 3 | saṃjñā | saññā | perception | recognition and labelling of sense objects |
| 4 | saṃskāra | saṅkhāra | mental formations / volitions | conditioned mental processes, including intention/will |
| 5 | vijñāna | viññāṇa | consciousness | discriminative awareness of objects through the six sense bases |

(Source: Wikipedia "Skandha," citing Rhys Davids/William Stede and the Pali Canon.)

**The foundational doctrinal claim:** All Buddhist traditions hold that "the aggregates do not constitute a 'self'" (Wikipedia). This is the *anātman* / *anattā* doctrine — the denial of an enduring personal essence. The five skandhas are impermanent, interdependent, and constantly arising and passing away. Their seeming unity is what we *mistake* for a self, but no stable "I" is found within or behind them.

This is in **direct structural opposition** to the Jain *jīva* doctrine. Jainism asserts a real, permanent individual soul (jīva) characterised by consciousness (*upayoga*), which persists through all rebirths and reaches liberation as a distinct individual. Buddhism denies any such permanent individual essence.

**What rūpa (the first skandha) is:** Buddhist rūpa covers material form broadly — the four elemental qualities (solidity, cohesion, heat, motion) and derived material phenomena. It is not a theory of physical atoms (paramāṇus). Buddhist Abhidharma traditions have their own fine-grained matter theories (e.g., *rūpakalāpa* in Theravāda Abhidharma), but these are not the same as Vaiśeṣika or Jain paramāṇu doctrine.

**Etymology:** Sanskrit *skandha* / Pali *khandha* = "heap, bulk, aggregate" (Rhys Davids & Stede, *Pali-English Dictionary*). The term denotes a cluster or collection, without implying unity or selfhood.

**Key texts:** The five khandhas are defined in the *Samyutta Nikāya* (*Khandha-saṃyutta*, SN 22) and appear throughout the *Majjhima Nikāya*. They are foundational to both Theravāda and Mahāyāna traditions.

## Critical distinctions: Buddhist skandha vs. Jain skandha

| dimension | Buddhist skandha (khandha) | Jain skandha |
|---|---|---|
| number | five | indefinite (any aggregate of ≥2 paramāṇus) |
| includes consciousness | yes (vijñāna is the fifth) | no — consciousness belongs to jīva, never to matter |
| includes mental factors | yes (saṃjñā, saṃskāra, vedanā) | no — strictly physical matter only |
| implies self? | no — their aggregation is what we mistake for self | irrelevant — Jain skandha is just a material category |
| metaphysical purpose | to show that "person" = impermanent aggregates, no abiding self | to classify grades of matter from gross to fine |
| liberation | dissolution/non-arising of the five aggregates | liberation of jīva from all physical karma/skandha |
