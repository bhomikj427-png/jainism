## Gloss / Divergence map

Plato's *Republic* presents the soul (*psychē*) as having **three parts** (*mérē*), each with a distinct function and capacity:

| part | Greek | location | function | corresponding city-class |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reason | *logistikon* | head | thinks, loves truth, governs | rulers (*philosophoi*) |
| Spirit / passion | *thymoeides* | chest | seat of anger, pride, honor | military (*phylakes*) |
| Appetite | *epithymētikon* | stomach | seeks food, sex, material pleasure | craftsmen/producers |

**The argument for tripartition (Republic 435c–441c)**

Plato argues from the *principle of opposites*: the same part of a thing cannot at the same time desire and refuse the same object (Republic 437b). Since we sometimes experience wanting to drink while a part of us refuses, these must be separate parts. Spirit (*thumos*) is introduced as a third part separate from both reason and appetite — it sides with reason against appetite in internal conflict (Republic 440b), but is not identical with reason.

**The *Phaedrus* chariot allegory (246a–249c)**

The soul is a charioteer (*logistikon*) driving two winged horses: a noble horse (*thymoeides*, obedient and honor-loving) and an unruly horse (*epithymētikon*, driven by base desires). The soul's ability to ascend toward truth and the Forms depends on the charioteer directing both horses harmoniously. This myth also presents the soul as immortal and pre-existing — it glimpsed the Forms in its prior disembodied state.

**Justice as internal harmony**

In the *Republic*, the *just soul* is one where each part performs its proper function without overstepping: reason rules, spirit supports reason, appetite complies. Injustice (unjust soul) occurs when appetite dominates. The soul's structure mirrors the city's three-class structure: philosophical rulers : military : producers = logistikon : thymoeides : epithymētikon.

**Immortality and metempsychosis**

The soul is immortal for Plato. After death, souls undergo judgment; guilty souls descend through animal forms as punishment; virtuous souls ascend. The *Timaeus* and *Laws* treat reincarnation as an educational process. The soul's wings grow when it perceives truth and beauty; they atrophy in lives dominated by appetite.

## Cross-tradition comparison

| tradition | soul structure | unity/plurality | agent/passive | liberation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plato | tripartite (reason/spirit/appetite); fundamentally reason | one soul per person | soul is agent; body is hindrance | return to the Forms; philosophical life |
| Jain jīva | single conscious entity; upayoga as mark | infinitely many distinct jīvas | active agent (kartā/bhoktā) | karma-free at lokāgra |
| Buddhist khandhas | five aggregates (no permanent soul) | no individual self; anattā | no permanent agent | nirvāṇa (cessation) |
| Sāṃkhya puruṣa | pure witness-consciousness; no parts | infinitely many puruṣas | passive witness; not an agent | discrimination from prakṛti |

Plato's soul is most similar to Jain jīva in that both posit individual, immortal souls that are agents and suffer from their entanglement with body/matter. But the Platonic soul has internal *parts* (reason can be dominated by appetite) — the Jain jīva is uniform in nature; its "corruption" is external (karma-matter), not internal division. The Platonic tripartite soul has no Jain equivalent.
