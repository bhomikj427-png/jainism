## Gloss

***Kāraka*** is [[panini|Pāṇini]]'s system of **participant roles in an action** — the layer of his grammar that says *what part a thing plays* in what is going on, as distinct from *what case-ending it takes*.

**Morphology first, as a hard constraint (§4 signal 3).** Abhyankar: "lit. **doer of an action**," from *√kṛ*, "to do" — but "the word is used in the technical sense of '**instrument of action**'." His gloss is the precise one and worth quoting whole:

> "The word '*kāraka*' in short, means '**the capacity in which a thing becomes instrumental in bringing about an action**'. This capacity is looked upon as **the sense of the case-affixes which express it**."

**There are six**, introduced under the governing rule at **A 1.4.23** and defined across **A 1.4.23–55**:

| kāraka | role |
|---|---|
| ***kartṛ*** | agent — the independent one |
| ***karman*** | object — what the agent most wants to reach |
| ***karaṇa*** | instrument — the most effective means |
| ***sampradāna*** | recipient — the one the object is destined for |
| ***apādāna*** | source — the fixed point from which departure occurs |
| ***adhikaraṇa*** | locus — where the action is located |

### Why this is the interesting part of the grammar

⚠ **Kārakas are not cases, and the whole point is that they are not.** There are **six** kārakas and **seven** *vibhaktis* (case-endings), and the mapping between them is neither one-to-one nor fixed: an agent surfaces in the nominative in an active sentence and in the instrumental in a passive one, while the instrumental also expresses *karaṇa*. **Kāraka is an intermediate level of representation** — Pāṇini "uses kārakas, or role types, **to mediate between semantic relation and morphosyntactic surface structure**," a "syntactico-semantic relation, serving as an **intermediary step** to express semantic relations through the usage of *vibhaktis*."

**So the Aṣṭādhyāyī does not go straight from meaning to ending. It goes meaning → role → ending**, and the role layer is where the generalisations live.

The tradition itself marked the distinction. Abhyankar: the endings that express kārakas are ***kārakavibhakti***, "as contrasted with ***upapadavibhakti***, which show a relation between two substantives and hence are looked upon as **weaker**" (*Paribhāṣenduśekhara* 94). **A case-ending assigned by a participant-role outranks one assigned by mere government by a neighbouring word** — a principled priority rule between two sources of the same surface form.

### ⚠ Pāṇini uses the term and does not define it

**A 1.4.23 is a heading, not a definition.** The term *kāraka* "is **not defined by Pāṇini**"; the definition the tradition works with — ***kriyānvayitvaṃ kārakatvam***, "**a kāraka is an item construed with an action**" — is accepted from "later grammarians and logicians." Abhyankar's own gloss is likewise built from the *Kāśikā* and the [[mahabhashya|Mahābhāṣya]] **on** I.4.23, not from I.4.23 itself.

⚠ **This is the batch's recurring mechanism for the third time in Pāṇini's own text**, alongside *pratyāhāra* ([[astadhyayi]]) and the biography read out of A 4.3.94 ([[panini]]). **Pāṇini states operations, not definitions.** He tells you what to *do* with a kāraka and never says what one *is*; the metalanguage that makes the system teachable is the commentators' contribution. **Whether that counts as recovering Pāṇini's meaning or supplying one is not decidable from the text**, which is exactly why the tradition's authority-maxim runs *upward* ([[vyakarana]]) — the definitions are downstream by construction.

**And this one spawned a genre.** Abhyankar: the kāraka chapter "is looked upon as a **very important and difficult** chapter in treatises of grammar and there are **several small compendiums** written by scholars dealing with kārakas **only**" — he names *Kārakacakra*, *Kārakakaumudī*, *Kārakavicāra*, *Kārakavāda* and more. **A topic the root text never defined became the tradition's densest sub-literature.**

⚠ **Name-collision flagged before it can be made.** One such *Kārakavāda* is by **Cakrapāṇi-Śeṣa**, "belonging to the famous Śeṣa family of grammarians, who lived in the **seventeenth century**." This is **not** [[cakrapanidatta|Cakrapāṇidatta]], the eleventh-century Bengali commentator on the *Caraka Saṃhitā* whom this corpus already holds. Different man, century, discipline and family. Recorded now because the corpus has met **five** silent name-merges in this batch alone, and this one was available to make.

### ⚠ The modern comparison, and where it stops

The kāraka system is the most-applied piece of Sanskrit grammar in present-day work: there is a "**Pāṇinian Grammar Framework**" used in computational linguistics, applied to English and to machine translation between Indian languages via "the Kāraka Model." The structural resemblance to **thematic roles** / **deep cases** in modern linguistics is real: an abstract layer of participant roles, mapped many-to-many onto surface case morphology.

**What that does not license.** That modern engineers find the scheme *usable* is a fact about the scheme's generality, **not evidence that Pāṇini was doing modern linguistics**, and not evidence of descent. The kārakas are **six**, fixed, and tuned to Sanskrit's morphology; they are not a theory of universal argument structure, and Pāṇini offers no account of *why* these six. **A framework that can be borrowed is not thereby the same theory as what borrowed it.** The corpus records the same brake on [[astadhyayi]]'s Post-system comparison, from Cardona.

⚠ **No edge is drawn for this comparison, deliberately.** The corpus has no node for modern linguistics or for thematic-role theory, and the nearest existing targets ([[modern-atom]], [[aristotle-logic]]) are about other things entirely — an edge to either would misstate the relation. **Recorded in prose rather than smoothed over with a loose link**, on the precedent [[avacchedaka]] set when it faced the same choice about the grammarians and refused it.

3. **Wikipedia, "Pāṇini"** (fetched): confirms the Aṣṭādhyāyī's rule-and-replacement architecture in which such a mediating layer sits; does **not** discuss kāraka, so it corroborates nothing here and is not counted.
4. **Internal consistency**: the "term used but not defined" pattern matches *pratyāhāra* in [[astadhyayi]] independently; the priority-rule between competing sources of one surface form is structurally the same problem [[avacchedaka]] solves for Navya-Nyāya — restricting the scope of an assignment so two rules stop colliding.
5. **Cross-check on the modern claim**: the "Pāṇinian Grammar Framework" is a live, checkable body of applied work, which is why the parallel is worth recording — and why it needed an explicit limit.

**CONVERGED**: Abhyankar and the linguistic literature agree on the definition, the six roles, the sūtra range and the mediating function, with no conflict.
