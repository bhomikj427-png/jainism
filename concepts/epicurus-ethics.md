## Gloss / Divergence map

**Epicurean ethics** makes **pleasure (*hēdonē*) the highest good (*telos*)** — but on a definition that overturns the popular sense of "hedonism." The foundational claim is empirical: all sentient creatures are by nature drawn to pleasure and repelled by pain, so pleasure and pain are "the fundamental guides to what is good and bad." Yet the **highest pleasure is not indulgence but the absence of disturbance**: Epicurus could be called a "tranquillist" rather than a hedonist.

**Katastematic vs kinetic pleasure.** *Kinetic* (moving) pleasures accompany the *process* of satisfying a want (fine food, scent) and "depend upon a lack that is painful." *Katastematic* (static) pleasure is the *state* of want already met — and this is the highest good, realised as **ataraxia** (freedom from mental disturbance) and **aponia** (absence of bodily pain). "The state of satiety, of no longer being in need... is itself pleasurable" and is the most pleasurable of all. The upshot is a surprising **asceticism of desire**: reduce your desires rather than multiply your goods.

**The classification of desires.** (i) *Natural and necessary* (food, shelter, security) — easily met, naturally limited, to be satisfied; (ii) *natural but not necessary* (luxuries) — breed dependency and anxiety; (iii) *vain/empty* (wealth, power, fame, immortality) — unlimited, socially constructed, never satisfiable, to be eliminated.

**The Tetrapharmakos (four-part cure).** Don't fear the gods; don't fear death; the good is easy to get; the bad is easy to endure. The gods exist but dwell in the *intermundia*, blessed and **indifferent to human affairs** — so religious dread is groundless. And **"death is nothing to us, since while we exist, our death is not, and when our death occurs, we do not exist"** (the no-subject-of-harm argument), reinforced by the symmetry of pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence — the soul is atomic and "disperses in the air" at death, so there is no afterlife to fear.

**Virtue and friendship.** The virtues are **instrumental**, not intrinsic: *phronēsis* (prudence) is "the chief of the virtues, on which all the rest depend," because "one cannot live pleasantly without living prudently, honourably, and justly." Friendship is prized as the greatest source of lifelong security; the sage practises *lathe biōsas* ("live unnoticed"), withdrawing from politics, which only imports anxiety.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1** (SEP, "Epicurus"): pleasure as telos grounded in sensation; katastematic (ataraxia + aponia) vs kinetic pleasure, with the highest pleasure being freedom from distress, not indulgence; the three-way desire classification; "death is nothing to us" (with the existence-quote verbatim); phronēsis as chief virtue with virtues instrumental to pleasure; friendship + *lathe biōsas*. **Signal 2** (IEP, "Epicurus"): Epicurus as "tranquillist," ataraxia as freedom from fear/disturbance; "satiety... the most pleasurable"; the same desire classification with the ascetic "reduce his desires" counsel; the gods in the *intermundia*, indifferent; the no-subject-of-harm and symmetry arguments with the atomic soul that "disperses in the air"; the tetrapharmakos; friendship for security.

The two are independent (scholarly encyclopedia vs teaching encyclopedia) and agree on every point. Medium, not high: the primary texts (*Letter to Menoeceus*; *Principal Doctrines*; the Tetrapharmakos as preserved by Philodemus) were not fetched verbatim — only the "death is nothing to us" line is given as a direct quote, on both sources' authority.
