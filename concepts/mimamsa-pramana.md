## Gloss

The Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā school — founded on Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā Sūtras* and developed by Śabara, Kumārila Bhaṭṭa, and Prabhākara — is primarily a school of Vedic hermeneutics, and its epistemological system is built around defending the unique authority of Vedic testimony (*śabda*). The school accepts more pramāṇas than Nyāya by adding *arthāpatti* (postulation) and, in the Bhāṭṭa sub-school, *anupalabdhi* (non-apprehension). Its distinctive doctrines — *apauruṣeyatva* (authorlessness of the Vedas) and *svataḥ-prāmāṇya* (self-validity of cognitions) — were built to respond to Buddhist epistemological challenges to Vedic authority.

**Arthāpatti (postulation):** Inferring an unstated fact from a stated incompatibility. Classical example: "Devadatta is fat but does not eat by day" → one *must* postulate he eats at night. This is not inference (*anumāna* = reasoning from observed sign); it is reasoning from practical necessity of explanation. Nyāya would subsume it under inference; Mīmāṃsā treats it as distinct.

**Anupalabdhi (non-apprehension):** Knowledge derived from the *absence* of perception. "There is no pot in this room" is not merely a negative inference — it is direct apprehension of absence (Kumārila). Prabhākara denies this is a distinct pramāṇa; knowledge of absence is reducible to perception.

## The two signature doctrines

**Apauruṣeyatva (authorlessness):** The Vedas were not composed by any person, god, or agent — they are eternal, beginningless. Their words and meanings have primordial connection. This removes the reliability problem that afflicts human testimony: an authorless text cannot have a biased or fallible author. This was Mīmāṃsā's response to the Buddhist argument that the Vedas, being of human origin, could be mistaken.

**Svataḥ-prāmāṇya (intrinsic validity):** Cognitions are valid by their own nature; they do not need external certification to be accepted as knowledge. When a cognition arises, it is presumed valid unless positively shown otherwise. This inverts the burden of proof. Nyāya requires positive validation of knowledge; Mīmāṃsā says validity is the default, invalidity needs to be proven.

## Historical context: defending the Vedas against Dignāga

Kumārila Bhaṭṭa's elaborate epistemological system (~7th c. CE) was specifically a response to Dignāga's (~5th–6th c. CE) Buddhist critique of Vedic testimony. Dignāga rejected śabda as an independent pramāṇa and argued that testimony reduces to inference (about the speaker's reliability). Kumārila's Ślokavārttika defended Vedic śabda's independence and eternal authority. The Mīmāṃsā-Buddhist epistemological debate drove much of medieval Indian philosophy.
