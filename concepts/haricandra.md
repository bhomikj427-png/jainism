## Gloss / Divergence map

**Haricandra** — also **Hariścandra**, **Bhaṭṭāra(ka)hari(ś)candra**, and sometimes named by his title of distinction alone, **Bhaṭṭāra(ka)** — was "**a very early, probably even the earliest, commentator on the *Carakasaṃhitā***" (Meulenbeld).

His commentary, the ***Carakanyāsa***, is **lost but for a small portion**. And he is quoted by very nearly every Āyurvedic author this corpus holds: **[[jejjata|Jejjaṭa]], [[candrata|Candraṭa]], [[cakrapanidatta|Cakrapāṇidatta]], [[arunadatta|Aruṇadatta]], [[dalhana|Ḍalhaṇa]], [[hemadri|Hemādri]], [[indu|Indu]]**, Candranandana, Niścalakara, Śivadāsasena, Śrīkaṇṭhadatta, Vijayarakṣita, Vopadeva and a dozen more.

**That combination is the node.** The earliest and most influential commentator on the great medical saṃhitā survives in a fragment — so his authority is established almost entirely by **other people's quotation of him**, and the reputation is recoverable in far more detail than the text.

### The reputation, in the words of the people who had the book

- **Candraṭa**, opening his own commentary on the *Cikitsākalikā*: "As there exist commentators like **Haricandra, Jejjaṭa, and Sudhīra**, it is presumptuous for anyone else to comment on the āyurveda." (This is the same sentence Meulenbeld uses to date Candraṭa — see `candrata.md`.)
- **Hemādri**, in the introduction to his *Āyurvedarasāyana*, calls him **the model of the commentators on the *Carakasaṃhitā***.
- An anonymous verdict Meulenbeld records: "The ignorant one who attempts to understand Caraka's thought without (consulting) Haricandra's commentary, is (like) a man who wants to drink up the ocean."
- **Śrīdāsapaṇḍita** makes him the foremost authority on internal medicine; **Narahari**, in his *Vāgbhaṭamaṇḍana*, gives him the epithets *munitulya* ("equal to a sage"), *nayavid* and *sakalavaidyamanyatama*.

⚠ **And the dissent, which matters more than the praise.** **Niścalakara and Śivadāsasena are sometimes critical of his opinions**, and **Indu often rejects his interpretations.** A tradition that only praised him would tell us he was a name; a tradition that argues with him tells us his positions were still live. Meulenbeld preserves two of them: Haricandra recognised **eighteen types of *kṣaya***, and distinguished a *gulma* in women caused by *ārtava* from a *gulma* caused by blood occurring in both sexes.

### The date

**Before A.D. 600**, on two independent grounds Meulenbeld reports:

1. **He is already quoted by Jejjaṭa.**
2. **Cakrapāṇidatta and Niścalakara were convinced he preceded [[vagbhata|Vāgbhaṭa]]** — "which would mean that he lived before A.D. 600."

⚠ **A negative argument worth marking**, because it shows the medieval commentators doing textual criticism. Some held that Bhaṭṭārakahariścandra *also* wrote a commentary on the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*. **Niścalakara contradicts this by observing that Vāgbhaṭa quotes Haricandra** — a man does not comment on an author who quotes him. The attribution is refused on a **chronological argument from citation direction**, which is exactly the method `dalhana.md` and `hemadri.md` are dated by.

⚠ The Batch 45 queue described him as "**Bhaṭṭāra Haricandra, 5th c.**, the earliest Caraka commentator named in this batch's sources." Meulenbeld supports *earliest* and *before 600*; **he does not give a 5th-century date**, and none is asserted here.

### An external, non-Indian witness

**Hariścandra is listed among the medical authorities in the *Mahāvyutpatti***, the Sanskrit–Tibetan lexicon compiled by order of **Khri-lde sroṅ-bcan (A.D. 816–838)**; his Tibetan name there is **Seṅ-ge zla-ba**. This is rare and valuable: a **datable, externally-controlled, non-Indian attestation** that a physician of this name was canonical enough to enter a state-sponsored Tibetan lexicon by the early 9th century. It does not date him — it dates his *fame*.

### ⚠ At least six other Haricandras

Meulenbeld separates them, and the corpus should keep them separate:

| who | where | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| a **prose writer** Haricandra | Bāṇa's *Harṣacarita*; Vākpati's *Gauḍavaho* | different person |
| an author quoted in Śrīdharadāsa's *Saduktikarṇāmṛta* and Vallabhadeva's *Subhāṣitāvalī* | | different |
| **Haricandravaidyatilaka**, one of the six sons of Śabarasvāmin | | different |
| a **Jain Digambara poet**, son of Ārdradeva and Rādhā | | "**undoubtedly different** from Bhaṭṭāraharicandra" |
| a **Bāhlīka physician** of the Kāṅkāyana gotra, son of Īśānacandra | Śyāmilaka's *Pādatāḍitaka* | different |
| a Haricandra who composed an **inscription in 1205** at the court of Bhillama III of Devagiri | | "much later than our commentator" |

Plus a **Vṛddhahariścandra** quoted in Anantakumāra, and an unidentified Hariścandra in the *Bheṣajjamañjūsāsannaya*. Compare `arunadatta.md`, where Gode found **three** men of one name merged by the popular literature into one polymath, and `vacaspati-mishra.md`'s two Vācaspatis. **This is the third such split in the corpus, and the pattern is now worth stating: in this literature, a distinguished name attracts unrelated bearers, and the merging is done by later summarisers rather than by the sources.**

### An observation, held deliberately loose

Haricandra's surviving *maṅgala* is addressed to **Sūrya**. Tisaṭa's *Cikitsākalikā* opens in praise of **Sūrya**, the Aśvins, Dhanvantari and Suśruta; and `candrata.md` records that **Candraṭa followed his father in being a sun-worshipper**, on the evidence of the *Yogaratnasamuccaya*'s first verse.

⚠ **That is three of these authors invoking Sūrya, and it is recorded as an observation, not a finding.** Sūrya is a conventional and unsurprising deity for a physician to invoke — the Aśvins and Dhanvantari are named alongside him — and **no source consulted draws any connection between these three**. Building a "solar tradition of Āyurvedic commentators" out of three *maṅgala*s would be precisely the inflation §0 forbids. It is logged so that a future reader with better sources can test it, and for no other purpose.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — G. Jan Meulenbeld, *A History of Indian Medical Literature*, vol. IA, "Haricandra," read directly.** The standard critical reference, and the sole substantive source: the identification and title-forms, "probably even the earliest" commentator, the *Carakanyāsa* and its partial survival, the Sūrya *maṅgala*, the *tantrayukti* competence, the revised *Kharaṇādasaṃhitā*, the refusal of the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* attribution on Niścalakara's argument, the ~25-author citation list, the encomia and the criticisms, the two preserved doctrines, the *Mahāvyutpatti* entry with the Tibetan name, the six homonyms, and the pre-600 dating. **Signal 2 — the medieval witnesses Meulenbeld quotes**, who are the real evidence and are not him: Candraṭa's introductory verses, Hemādri's introduction to the *Āyurvedarasāyana*, Maheśvara's *Viśvaprakāśa* (the Sahasāṅka court-physician verse), Niścalakara, Śrīdāsapaṇḍita, Narahari. **Signal 3 — corpus-internal**, written in Batch 44 from web-level sources and now testable: `jejjata.md`, `dalhana.md`, `hemadri.md`, `arunadatta.md`, `cakrapanidatta.md`, `vagbhata.md`.

⚠ **Single-scholar dependence, stated as in `candrata.md`.** One author, one work. What makes it usable is that Meulenbeld cites each witness individually so the claims trace to named medieval evidence rather than to his authority — but that is **not** independent confirmation, and no claim here was checked against a second modern scholar.

**Why converged.** Nothing is in dispute. The one contested attribution (the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* commentary) is resolved against, on a stated argument; the date is a **bracket** derived from two grounds, not a disagreement; and the homonym problem is a matter of separation, not of doubt. **Why medium, not high.** The surviving fragment of the *Carakanyāsa* was **not read**; the encomia are quoted from Meulenbeld's English; the queue's "5th c." could not be confirmed and was dropped; and "probably even the earliest" is Meulenbeld's own hedge, kept.
