## Gloss

*Anicca* (Pāli; Sanskrit: *anitya*) is the first of the three marks of existence (*ti-lakkhaṇa*): all *conditioned phenomena* (*saṅkhāra*) are impermanent — they arise from causes and conditions, change, and cease. The standard Pāli formula is *sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā* (Dhammapada 277: "all formations are impermanent"). Crucially, the claim is about *conditioned* things; the unconditioned (*asaṅkhata*) — nibbāna — is held to be beyond impermanence. The logical chain runs: anicca → dukkha (impermanent things cannot provide lasting satisfaction) → anattā (an impermanent, unsatisfying thing cannot be a permanent self).

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Dhammapada 277–279 (Pāli Canon; cited in Wikipedia "Three Marks of Existence"):** Dhp 277: *sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā*; Dhp 278: *sabbe saṅkhārā dukkhā*; Dhp 279: *sabbe dhammā anattā*. The three-verse sequence is the canonical compact statement.

**Signal 2 — SN 22.59 (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta; standard reference):** The Buddha analyses the five khandhas — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — and shows that each is impermanent, therefore suffering, therefore not-self. This is the direct application of anicca to the psychological constituents of persons.

**Signal 3 — Wikipedia "Three Marks of Existence" (citing Gombrich, Harvey):** Confirms the conditioned/unconditioned distinction; nibbāna as "the reality that is *nicca*, or knows no change, decay or death." Academic sources: Richard Gombrich, *Theravāda Buddhism*; Peter Harvey, *An Introduction to Buddhism*.

**Independence:** Signals 1 and 2 are canonical texts; Signal 3 is academic secondary. Confidence medium because Dhammapada verses cited via Wikipedia (not independently retrieved from critical Pāli edition).

## Three-tradition permanence comparison

| Tradition | Claim | Locus of permanence |
|---|---|---|
| **Buddhist anicca** | All conditioned things are impermanent; only the unconditioned (nibbāna) escapes | Nibbāna alone is beyond change |
| **Jain sat (TS 5.30)** | Substance has origination, destruction AND permanence simultaneously (*utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya*) | The substance (*dravya*) itself — permanent substrate WITH changing modes |
| **Advaita brahman** | Only Brahman is ultimately real and changeless; apparent change = māyā | Brahman alone; the world of change is illusory appearance |
| **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** | Substances (especially atoms) are eternal; qualities can change | Eternal atoms and ātman; qualities and relations change |

**The philosophical triangle:** Buddhism says there are *no* permanent substances — the five khandhas are always in flux. Jainism says substances are BOTH permanent AND changing — the permanence (*dhrauvya*) is as real as the change. Advaita says only the purely permanent is ultimately real — change is appearance. These three positions divide the ontological space on the permanence question and are mutually exclusive.

## The anattā extension and a contested edge

Note: Dhp 279 says *sabbe **dhammā** anattā* — "all *phenomena* are non-self," using *dhammā* rather than *saṅkhārā*. This raises the contested question of whether nibbāna itself is included in the anattā claim. Most Theravāda interpreters hold: anicca and dukkha apply to *saṅkhāra* only; anattā applies to all *dhammā* including nibbāna (but nibbāna is not-self in a different sense — it is beyond subject/object, not because it is impermanent). This is flagged but not resolved here: see anatta-buddhist.md.
