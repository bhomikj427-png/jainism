## Gloss / Divergence map

**Bhakti** (Sanskrit, root *bhaj* — service, participation, adoration): loving devotion to a personal deity as a path to liberation. The Bhagavad Gītā is the first text to explicitly use the word to designate a religious path (*bhakti-mārga*), alongside *jñāna-mārga* (knowledge) and *karma-mārga* (right action). The Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 6.23 contains one of the earliest usages: "He who has highest bhakti of Deva (God)... to him these teachings will be illuminating."

The three classical Hindu paths to *mokṣa*:

| path | means | associated text / school |
|---|---|---|
| bhakti-mārga | loving devotion to a personal deity | BG; Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja); Dvaita (Madhva); Vaiṣṇava *sant* tradition |
| jñāna-mārga | direct knowledge of Brahman/ātman | Advaita (Śaṅkara); principal Upaniṣads |
| karma-mārga | performance of right action without attachment | BG (Arjuna dilemma framing); dharmaśāstra |

**Rāmānuja vs Madhva on bhakti:**
- Rāmānuja: bhakti paired with *prapatti* (total self-surrender) as the two soteriological means; bhakti presupposes reason but transcends it; liberation = eternal communion retaining individuality.
- Madhva: bhakti rests entirely on God's *anugraha* (grace); the soul is utterly dependent (*paratantra*) on Viṣṇu; bhakti without God's grace cannot lead to liberation.

**Cross-tradition parallels** (all require NOT-equivalent caution):
- *Bhatti* (Pali) appears in Theravāda texts (Theragāthā) as devotion to the Buddha — structural parallel but without the liberation-via-devotion claim.
- Jain tradition includes devotional veneration of Tīrthaṅkaras (*pūjā*) — but the Tīrthaṅkara neither intervenes nor responds; liberation comes from the practitioner's own effort, making Jain "bhakti" soteriologically non-equivalent.
