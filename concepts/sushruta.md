## Gloss / Divergence map

"**Sushruta**" names both a physician-figure and the surgery-centered *Suśruta Saṃhitā* — Āyurveda's other foundational Saṃhitā alongside `charaka.md`. Like Charaka, the attribution is not straightforward:

**Contested: who wrote it, and to whom is it attributed?** Meulenbeld's assessment, as reported: "the text of the Suśrutasaṃhitā does not warrant that the one who composed it was a Sushruta." Two attribution frames coexist in the manuscript tradition itself: printed editions frame the work as **Dhanvantari's** teaching to a group of physicians including Sushruta, but "the oldest manuscripts... omit this frame, ascribing the work directly to King **Divodāsa**" (Wikipedia, "Ayurveda"). The proposed resolution (Meulenbeld, via secondary summary) is that "Sushruta" functions as a tradition-name, comparable to "Hippocrates," rather than a single historical author — the compiler organized earlier material and attributed it to Kāśirāja Divodāsa. `status: contested` is set for this authorship/attribution question specifically.

**Dating.** Wide scholarly disagreement, "ranging from 2000 BCE to the sixth century CE"; the most widely accepted modern consensus places the final recension in the **3rd–4th centuries CE**. Meulenbeld's layered-composition analysis (1999–2002) places the earliest layers in the last pre-Christian centuries, with a later redactor adding the closing *Uttaratantra* section — structurally the same multi-layer redaction pattern found in `charaka.md`, though the specific layers and redactor names differ.

**Structure and content.** 186 chapters across six sections: five core *sthānas* plus the *Uttaratantra* (covering surgery/*Śālākyatantra*, pediatrics, therapy, and supplementary material). Describes 1,120 diseases, 700 medicinal plants, 64 mineral preparations, and — its signature contribution — extensive surgical technique: cadaveric dissection practice (on gourds, dead animals, and per one secondary source, infant cadavers submerged in river water for controlled decomposition), 101 blunt and 20 sharp instruments, and reconstructive procedures including the forehead-flap and cheek-flap **rhinoplasty** methods still cited as ancestral to modern plastic surgery. Anatomically, Suśruta enumerates 300 bones against Charaka's 360 — a genuine internal-tradition numerical disagreement, not resolved in the sources consulted here.

**Transmission.** Translated into Arabic in early 8th-century Baghdad under Barmakid patronage (*Kitāb Shāh Shūn al-Hindī*); reached Tibetan medical literature and was known to the Cambodian king Yaśovarman I (r. 889–900 CE). First complete English translation: Kaviraj Kunja Lal Bhishagratna, three volumes, 1907–1916 — later recognized as limited in its medical-terminology choices; P. V. Sharma produced an alternative 1999 translation. Printed editions historically drew on only ~10% of the 230+ surviving manuscripts.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Sushruta Samhita"** — Meulenbeld attribution quote, Dhanvantari/Divodāsa manuscript-framing split, dating range and 3rd–4th-c. consensus, 186-chapter/six-section structure, disease/plant/mineral counts, rhinoplasty and cadaver-study details, Arabic/Tibetan/Cambodian transmission, Bhishagratna translation history.
2. **PMC article (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11000756)** — independently corroborates the rhinoplasty/otoplasty/cheiloplasty procedures and instrument counts, but places Sushruta flatly at "~600 BCE" with **no historiographical caveat** — i.e., this source is less careful than Wikipedia's Meulenbeld-sourced account and does not mention the person-vs-tradition-name question at all. This divergence in rigor between a popular-medical-history PMC piece and the Meulenbeld-sourced Wikipedia account is itself worth flagging: the "600 BCE, real individual" framing is common in medical/surgical literature and coexists uneasily with the philological consensus.
3. **Wikipedia, "Ayurveda"** — independently corroborates the Dhanvantari-vs-Divodāsa manuscript-framing detail.

Two independent sources (Wikipedia's Sushruta Samhita article and its Ayurveda article) converge on the manuscript-framing split and the broad dating band → those points **converged**. But a third source (the PMC article) flatly contradicts the philological consensus by asserting an unqualified historical date and person — a genuine split between medical-history popularization and Indological philology, not smoothed over → attribution/date specifics remain **contested**. Confidence **medium**: Meulenbeld's primary monograph not independently fetched; the PMC source's lower rigor was not simply discarded but recorded as evidence of how the popular medical-history literature diverges from specialist Indology.
