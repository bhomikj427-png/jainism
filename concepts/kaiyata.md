## Gloss / Divergence map

**Kaiyaṭa** is the Kashmirian author of the ***Mahābhāṣyapradīpa*** — the *Pradīpa*, "the Lamp" — **the oldest extant full commentary on the [[mahabhashya|Mahābhāṣya]]**, and tier 4 of the five-tier stack that node records. He is the son of **Jaiyaṭa**, and he is the man who formulated the sentence the corpus has been leaning on since Batch 47: ***yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam***.

Two things about him are contested, and they are contested for different reasons.

## Reasoning across signals

**T1 — the Batch 47 follow-up — is CONFIRMED, and confirmed with more precision than the corpus asked for.** Batch 47 recorded the maxim as "secondary report only, unverified at first hand," having been blocked by a 403. Deshpande's Gonda lecture supplies it directly, with wording and location:

> Kaiyaṭa's principle, *yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam* (**Pradīpa on Mahābhāṣya on P. 1.1.29; Vol. I: 217**), provides an ordered hierarchy between the three grammarian sages.

The wording [[vyakarana]] carried is **exactly right**, the attribution to Kaiyaṭa is **correct**, and the corpus can now cite the passage rather than the report. The Batch 47 caveat in `vyakarana.md` is discharged.

**⭐ But the same sentence that confirms it also bounds it — and the corpus had the scope wrong.** Deshpande continues: the authority of the sages "increases successively, and **it effectively peaks in Patañjali**." The maxim ranks **three men and then stops**. It is not a general principle that later commentators outrank earlier ones. [[mahabhashya]] had used it to supply "the doctrinal warrant that makes the climb respectable rather than decadent" for a **five-tier** stack — but tiers 4 and 5, Kaiyaṭa and Nāgeśa, fall **outside the scope of the maxim Kaiyaṭa himself wrote**. Corrected in that file this batch.

**⭐ The rule that stops, and the gloss that cannot explain the stopping.** Nāgeśa, commenting on the maxim at the same page, rationalises it: *uttarottarasya bahulakṣyadarśitvāt* — the later authority has observed a wider range of actual usage. Deshpande immediately notes the cost: "If this progressively wider vision is purely through chronological succession, **it does not explain why this process must end with Patañjali**." **Nāgeśa's justification proves more than the rule says.** By his own reasoning Nāgeśa — who has seen sixteen more centuries of usage — should outrank Patañjali, and no one in the tradition draws that conclusion.

**⭐ Deshpande's explanation of the stop, which is also the deepest link to the corpus's existing work.** He contrasts Kaiyaṭa with **Haradatta** (author of the *Padamañjarī* on the Kāśikā), a near-contemporary who produced a *complementary*, non-hierarchical view of the three sages. The difference tracks **which myth each man knew**:

- **Haradatta** — south Indian, Chola country, Śaivite (his name means "given by Śiva"; the *Padamañjarī* opens saluting Śiva) — gives the **first attestation in the Pāṇinian tradition** of Pāṇini receiving the Śiva-sūtras from Maheśvara, but shows no knowledge of Patañjali as Śeṣa. Result: three sages, complementary.
- **Kaiyaṭa** — Kashmiri, **Vaiṣṇava** (the *Pradīpa* opens saluting Nārāyaṇa; his teacher is named Maheśvara) — shows **no** awareness of the Śiva revelation, but calls Patañjali ***Nāganātha*** (ad M.Bh. ad P. 4.2.92, II: 430), which is first-hand evidence he knew the Ādiśeṣa-incarnation motif. Result: a hierarchy with **Patañjali at the apex**.

⚠ **This extends [[patanjali]]'s Batch 47 finding one step, and the step is load-bearing.** That node identified a **theological motor** behind the three-way Patañjali conflation — an avatar of Ādi Śeṣa, for whom mastering several sciences is a consequence of the premise. Deshpande's reading makes the *same motor* drive the tradition's **authority structure**: the *munitraya* ranking looks like text-critical judgement and may instead be a consequence of which incarnation story reached Kashmir. It also answers Nāgeśa's problem — **the ranking stops at Patañjali because the myth stops at Patañjali**; Nāgeśa's empirical gloss over-generates precisely because it has replaced a theological reason with a chronological one. ⚠ Deshpande states the causal claim with "perhaps," twice; recorded with his hedge, not firmer.

**⭐ The dating split is Batch 47's standing caution coming true on schedule.** That caution: *"read at first hand" and "specialist" are strong marks for what a discipline knows internally, and weak for anything depending on outside scholarship that has moved — chronology above all.* Here the specialist dictionary asserts a **century** with no argument, the lexica give **no date**, and careful modern usage gives only a **bound** ("pre-thirteenth century"). Abhyankar is not contradicted; he is **more confident than his evidence**. The caution predicted this before the source was opened.

**Independence check (§4 signal 1).** ⚠ **wisdomlib's Kaiyaṭa page is not an independent witness** — it reproduces Abhyankar's entry *verbatim* under an explicit attribution, alongside MW and Apte. Search summaries repeating "resident of Kashmir… his father's name was Jaiyaṭa… would have remained unintelligible" are quoting Abhyankar, not confirming him. **The genuinely independent pair here is Abhyankar and Deshpande**; the lexica add only the patronymic. Confidence held at **medium** on that count, not raised by the apparent abundance of agreeing pages.

**Not verified at first hand.** ⚠ Abhyankar's ***munitraya*** headword is quoted by wisdomlib as calling the three "the pioneers of that system" with **no ranking among them** — which would put Abhyankar's own dictionary at odds with the ranking on two counts (no *yathottara* entry, no hierarchy under *munitraya*). **That entry could not be located in the OCR scan** under any English phrase from the quotation, so it is reported as wisdomlib's quotation of Abhyankar and **not** as a first-hand reading. The negative check *was* done at first hand: **"yathottara" returns zero hits in the full scan**, replicating Batch 47's result.
