## Gloss

*Samayasāra* ("The Essence of the Self / the Doctrine") is Kundakunda's chief work and the foundational text of **Digambara Jain mysticism** — 439 Prākrit *gāthās* on the nature of the soul (*jīva*) and its relation to karma. Its single governing claim, pressed from the *niścaya* (absolute) standpoint: **the pure soul (*śuddhātma*) is utterly distinct from karmic matter — it is neither the doer of material karma nor the accumulator of merit and demerit (*puṇya/pāpa*).** Whatever binding, doing, and moral accounting the conventional (*vyavāhara*) standpoint describes belongs to matter's interaction, not to the soul's own essence; the soul's real nature is pure knowledge, perception, and bliss. Liberation, on this reading, is not *acquiring* something but *recognizing* what the soul always already is. The text is organized into ten chapters tracking the tattva sequence — jīva/ajīva, doer-and-deed (*kartā-karma*), merit-and-demerit (*puṇya-pāpa*), āsrava, saṃvara, nirjarā, bandha, mokṣa, and finally *sarva-viśuddhi-jñāna* (all-pure knowledge).

## Chapter arc — the tattvas read from the soul's side

| chapter | topic | the niścaya reframe |
|---|---|---|
| jīva / ajīva | soul vs. non-soul | the soul is *never* the matter it seems entangled with |
| kartā-karma | doer and deed | the soul does not *do* material karma; matter modifies matter |
| puṇya-pāpa | merit and demerit | *both* gold and iron chains bind — neither belongs to the pure soul |
| āsrava → bandha | influx → bondage | described as the soul's *apparent* condition (vyavāhara) |
| saṃvara → nirjarā → mokṣa | stoppage → shedding → liberation | recognizing the pure self *is* the turning |
| sarva-viśuddhi-jñāna | all-pure knowledge | the soul seen wholly as itself — the goal |

Note the **puṇya-pāpa chapter's signature image**: merit and demerit are a *gold* chain and an *iron* chain — both still chains. This is the Samayasāra's sharpest practical corollary and the doctrinal home of punya.md / papa.md: even good karma binds, so the goal is not to accumulate merit but to transcend the merit/demerit axis entirely.

## A two-standpoints text, not a contradiction of the Tattvārtha Sūtra

A naive reading sees the Samayasāra ("the soul never does karma") as *contradicting* the Tattvārtha Sūtra and Chapter 03 ("the soul binds karma, climbs the gunasthānas, is liberated"). It does not. The two describe the *same soul from the two standpoints* (nishchaya-vyavahara.md): the TS speaks *vyavāhara* (the soul as entangled and progressing), the Samayasāra speaks *niścaya* (the soul as essentially pure). Kundakunda's own framework requires *both* — the conventional account is the ladder, the absolute account is what stands at the top of it. Reading either standpoint as the *whole* truth is the error the niścaya/vyavāhara device exists to prevent.
