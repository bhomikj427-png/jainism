## Gloss / Divergence map

The modern atom is the **smallest unit of a chemical element** — but crucially, it is **not** the smallest unit of matter and it is **not** indivisible. Despite the name (Greek *atomos* = indivisible), the modern atom has known substructure and was discovered empirically.

**Structure:**
- **Nucleus:** protons (charge +1, made of 2 up quarks + 1 down quark) + neutrons (neutral, made of 1 up quark + 2 down quarks)
- **Electron cloud:** electrons (charge -1) in probability distributions around the nucleus (quantum mechanical orbitals, not fixed orbits)
- Protons and neutrons are themselves composite (quarks bound by the strong nuclear force)

**Key properties:**
- **Has parts** — protons, neutrons, electrons, quarks
- **Empirically discovered** — from Dalton's law of definite proportions (1803) → Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911) → Bohr model (1913) → quantum mechanical model
- **Divisible** — nuclear fission splits the nucleus; radioactive decay transforms atoms; particle accelerators probe substructure
- **No intrinsic philosophical qualities** — modern atoms have measurable physical properties (mass, charge, spin, energy levels) but not "colour" or "taste" or "smell" as intrinsic atomic properties (those are macroscopic/emergent)

## Why this is NOT equivalent to Jain or Vaiśeṣika paramāṇu

| dimension | Jain paramāṇu | Vaiśeṣika paramāṇu | modern atom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indivisible? | yes — by definition partless | yes — by definition partless | no — protons/neutrons/quarks inside |
| Argued or empirical? | argued a priori (infinite-regress) | argued a priori (measurement limit) | empirically discovered |
| Has taste/colour/smell? | yes — one each, intrinsic | varies by element type (tiered) | no — these are emergent macroscopic properties |
| Eternal/indestructible? | yes | yes | no — nuclear fission, radioactive decay |
| Smallest unit of matter? | yes — by definition | yes — by definition | no — quarks are smaller; leptons have no size |

**The mistaken parallel** that drives the conflation: both Jain tradition and modern physics use the word "atom" and both posit a smallest unit that combines into larger structures. The conceptual overlap goes no further. The Jain paramāṇu is a philosophical conclusion from a metaphysical argument (infinite regress of division is impossible); the modern atom is a physical entity found by experiment, and it has further substructure.

Equating them misreads the Jain tradition (which would need to be revised in the light of quarks — but that's not what the tradition claims to be about) and misreads modern physics (which makes no metaphysical claims about infinite-regress arguments).
