## Gloss / Divergence map

Aristotle's *Categories* presents **the ten highest genera of being** — the most fundamental ways a thing can be described. They are not categories of thought but categories of *what there is*: the basic ontological furniture of the world.

**The ten categories (Categories 1b25–2a4):**

| # | Greek | Latin | what it classifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ousia | substantia | substance (the primary bearer) |
| 2 | poson | quantitas | quantity (how large, how many) |
| 3 | poion | qualitas | quality (what kind, what character) |
| 4 | pros ti | relatio | relatives (in relation to what) |
| 5 | pou | locus | somewhere (where located) |
| 6 | pote | tempus | sometime (when) |
| 7 | keisthai | positio | being in a position (lying, sitting) |
| 8 | echein | habitus | having (being shod, being armed) |
| 9 | poiein | actio | acting (cutting, burning) |
| 10 | paschein | passio | being acted upon (being cut, being burned) |

Categories 2–10 are **accidents** — they cannot exist independently but only by inhering in a substance. The entire categorical system is substance-anchored: everything either *is* a substance or *inheres in* / *is said of* a substance.

**Primary vs. secondary substance (Categories 2a11–2a18)**

- **Primary substance** (*prōtē ousia*): particular concrete individuals — "this man," "this horse." They are neither said-of nor present-in anything else. They are the ontological bedrock.
- **Secondary substance** (*deutera ousia*): universals in the substance category — "man," "animal." These are *said-of* primary substances (Socrates is a man) but have no independent existence apart from the particulars they describe.

This yields a four-fold classification (Categories 1a20–1b9):
1. Said-of but not present-in: secondary substances (universals)
2. Present-in but not said-of: particular accidents (this white)
3. Both said-of and present-in: universal accidents (whiteness in general)
4. Neither said-of nor present-in: primary substances

**The tension with the *Metaphysics***

In *Metaphysics* Book VII (Zeta), Aristotle's account of substance shifts. The *Categories* account makes bare particulars (this individual) the primary substances. But *Metaphysics* Z argues that *form* (*morphē*) — the essence or definition of a thing — is substance in the most fundamental sense. This creates a tension: which is more fundamental, the individual (Categories) or the form (Metaphysics)? This is one of the most debated questions in Aristotle scholarship.

## Cross-tradition comparison

| system | substance analog | accidents analog | key difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aristotle Categories | ousia (primary/secondary) | 9 accident categories | substance is particular individual; universals are secondary |
| Aristotle Metaphysics | form (eidos) | matter + accidental properties | form is more basic than individual; tension with Categories |
| Jain dravya/guṇa/paryāya | dravya (substance) | guṇa (permanent attributes), paryāya (transient modes) | similar three-level structure; but Jain dravya is individual AND species at once — no primary/secondary split |
| Vaiśeṣika dravya | 9 dravyas (substances) | guṇa (quality), karma (action) | parallel 10-category structure; different list content |

The Jain guṇa/paryāya system is structurally similar to quality/relational-accidents in Aristotle: guṇas are permanent attributes (like Aristotelian qualities inhering in substance), paryāyas are transient modes. But the Jain dravya does not have a primary/secondary substance distinction — every dravya is equally a substance at one level of reality.
