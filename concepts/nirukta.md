## Gloss / Divergence map

***Nirukta*** — "explained, uttered out" — is the **etymological vedāṅga**: the discipline of establishing what Vedic words *mean*, as distinct from [[vyakarana|vyākaraṇa]], which establishes what form they correctly take. Wikipedia states the division of labour cleanly:

> "*Vyakarana* deals with linguistic analysis to establish the exact **form** of words to properly express ideas, while Nirukta focuses on linguistic analysis to help establish the proper **meaning**."

Its surviving monument is the ***Nirukta*** of **Yāska**, which the sources date to roughly the **7th–5th century BCE** — a critical edition placing it "between 700 and 500 BCE, i.e., **before Gautama Buddha**." ⚠ On the modern numismatic dating of [[panini]] (mid-4th c. BCE), that would make Yāska comfortably the earlier — but **no source consulted states the relation**, and the corpus does not infer a chronology by subtracting two independently uncertain dates. Left open.

### The divergence: did Yāska write the Nighaṇṭu?

| reading | source | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Yes** — Yāska wrote it | **Wikipedia**: "Yaska wrote the *Nighaṇṭu* (glossary), 'the first book on this field'" | Yāska both compiles the word-list and explains it; the *Nirukta* is his commentary on his own glossary |
| **No** — he inherited it | **Abhyankar** (read at first hand): the *Nighaṇṭu* is "a collection of words which are mainly Vedic. In ancient times such collections were possibly **very general and numerous**, and the works or treatises on derivation **such as the Nirukta of Yāska were based upon them**" | Yāska is a commentator on pre-existing lexical material; the glossary tradition is older than he is |

⚠ **Abhyankar's reading is the better-supported one**, and Wikipedia nearly refutes itself in the next breath by saying Yāska's *Nirukta* "extensively **refers to** the *Nighantu*" — which is what one does with a source, not with one's own preceding chapter. **The corpus records Abhyankar's position as the likelier and does not close the question**, having read neither text.

**The shape of this is now familiar from the whole batch.** [[vyakarana]] found that Pāṇini names ten lost predecessors; here the same pattern holds one discipline over — **the earliest surviving author is working on material already old**, and reference works quietly promote him from commentator to originator. Abhyankar's "very general and numerous" collections are precisely the lost stratum.

*(Durgācārya, Yāska's commentator, treats* nighaṇṭu *as synonymous with* nigama*.)*

### The two disputes Yāska preserves, and why they matter

**1. Do all nouns derive from verbs?** Yāska sides with **[[vyakarana|Śākaṭāyana]]** — one of the ten pre-Pāṇinian grammarians Pāṇini also names — that **every noun derives from a verbal root**, so that all naming is ultimately the naming of an *activity*. **Gārgya** objects, on the ground that the thesis over-generates: if nouns came solely from verbs, "every profession would share a name."

⚠ **This is a substantive and still-recognisable linguistic disagreement**, and the corpus should resist two temptations. It is *not* a debate about historical etymology in the modern comparative sense — neither party has a notion of sound-change or of related languages. And it is *not* settled by Yāska's siding with Śākaṭāyana: **Gārgya's objection is the better argument**, and the tradition preserved it. What is at stake is whether meaning is fundamentally about *processes* or about *things* — which is the same axis [[mahabhashya]] records Vyāḍi and Vājapyāyana dividing on (individual vs universal), one generation later and in the other discipline.

**2. Are the Vedic mantras meaningless?** Yāska records the objection of **Kautsa** that they are — *anarthakā mantrāḥ*, the verses carry no sense — and answers it. ⚠ **This is the most philosophically loaded thing in the vedāṅga literature**, and the corpus states plainly what it does and does not have: the objection and its author are attested, and **Yāska's actual response was not read.** The controversy is treated in the scholarly literature as "a **turning point** in the traditional interpretation of the Veda."

**Why it matters here.** [[vyakarana]] records that grammar exists to "prevent sloppy usage and transmission of the Vedic knowledge." **Kautsa's objection attacks the premise of that entire programme**: if the mantras mean nothing, preserving their exact form is preserving noise. **The two ancillary sciences of the Veda are, at their root, an argument about whether the Veda says anything** — and the discipline of meaning is where the objection had to be met.

⚠ **A source located and not obtained.** *"From Word Magic to Systematic Linguistic Inquiry: The Kautsa Controversy in Nirukta 1.15–16," Journal of Indian Philosophy* (Springer, 2021) — **paywalled; the abstract was not retrieved.** Recorded with its exact reference as a **follow-up**, because it is the one piece of current peer-reviewed scholarship the batch identified and could not read.

### ⚠ A false friend, caught in Abhyankar

Abhyankar, s.v. *dravya*:

> "The word *dravya* is used by **Yāska, Pāṇini and other grammarians** in a very general sense as **something in completed formation or existence**, as opposed to *bhāva* or *kriyā* or verbal activity."

**This is not [[dravya-vaisheshika|Vaiśeṣika's *dravya*]].** For the grammarians it is a *grammatical* contrast — the completed/nominal as against the processual/verbal, the very distinction the Śākaṭāyana–Gārgya dispute is about. For Vaiśeṣika it is the first ontological category, substance as the bearer of qualities and motions. **Same word, two disciplines, unrelated work being done** — and the confusion is easy to make because [[vakyapadiya]] shows a grammarian *deliberately* borrowing the Vaiśeṣika category list. **Flagged as a typed NOT-equivalent below**: without it, the map would suggest the grammarians were doing Vaiśeṣika ontology from the start, when the shared vocabulary post-dates the divergence.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Nirukta"** (fetched): the vedāṅga classification; Yāska c. 7th–5th c. BCE and the 700–500 BCE critical-edition dating; the form/meaning division against vyākaraṇa; ⚠ the Nighaṇṭu-authorship claim, here **under challenge**. The entry does **not** address Yāska's relation to Pāṇini, the Śākaṭāyana/Gārgya dispute, Kautsa, or the four word-classes.
2. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand**): the *Nighaṇṭu* as pre-existing lexical material that Yāska's work "was based upon," citing Nir. I.1; Durgācārya's *nighaṇṭu* ≈ *nigama*; the grammarians' *dravya* as "completed formation… as opposed to *bhāva* or *kriyā*"; Yāska cited throughout as a standard authority (`Nir.` is one of his abbreviations), which is itself evidence of how closely the two vedāṅgas were read together.
3. **Secondary literature via search**: Yāska siding with Śākaṭāyana that all nouns derive from a verbal stem; Gārgya's over-generation objection; Kautsa's *mantras are meaningless* and its status as "a turning point in the traditional interpretation of the Veda." ⚠ **Search-summary level**; the Springer article behind it was **not obtained**.
4. **Internal consistency**: Śākaṭāyana and Gārgya both appear in [[vyakarana]]'s list of the ten grammarians **Pāṇini** names — independently established there from Abhyankar. **The same two men are disputants in Yāska and names in Pāṇini**, which corroborates that the two disciplines share a single pre-Pāṇinian intellectual milieu, and is the firmest thing this node has.
5. **Lexicon** (§4 signal 3): *nir-ukta*, "spoken out, explained" — past participle of *nir-√vac*. The name describes the *act of glossing*, not a theory of word-origins; the modern sense "etymology" is a narrowing.

**CONTESTED**: the *Nighaṇṭu*'s authorship splits the sources, and the Yāska/Pāṇini chronology is unaddressed by any of them. Confidence **medium**: the discipline's definition and Yāska's rough date are agreed, and signal 4 is a genuine independent cross-check — but the *Nirukta* itself was not read, Wikipedia's coverage is unusually thin, and the two disputes that give the node its interest rest on search-summary sourcing.
