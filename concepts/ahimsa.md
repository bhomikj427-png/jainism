## Gloss / Divergence map

Ahiṃsā is **non-violence / non-harming** — the supreme ethical principle of Jainism, expressed in the maxim *ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ* ("non-violence is the highest dharma/virtue"). It is the first and most important of the five great vows (*mahāvratas*) detailed in TS Chapter 7.

**Metaphysical grounding in the jīva doctrine:** Ahiṃsā's scope is derived directly from jīva.md: every living being — from humans down to microscopic organisms — is a jīva (a conscious soul with upayoga). Because every jīva can experience suffering and has the potential for liberation, harming any jīva accumulates karma for the harmer. The scope of ahiṃsā is coextensive with the scope of jīva.

**Six categories of jīvas by sense-faculty (graduated scope of ahiṃsā):**

| category | Sanskrit | examples | sense faculties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earth-bodied | pṛthvīkāya | soil, stones, minerals | touch only |
| Water-bodied | jalakāya | water (in natural form) | touch only |
| Fire-bodied | agnikāya | fire, lightning | touch only |
| Air-bodied | vāyukāya | wind | touch only |
| Plant-bodied | vanaspatikāya | plants, trees | touch only |
| Mobile | trasa | animals, humans, insects | 2–5 senses |

One-sensed beings (touch only) are least able to defend themselves; harming them generates karma but less than harming a five-sensed being. Jain monks take extreme care even about one-sensed beings (hence the Digambara practice of sweeping the path, filtering water).

**The five mahāvratas (great vows)** — TS Chapter 7 (source: search-result aggregation):
1. *Ahiṃsā* — non-violence (first and most fundamental)
2. *Satya* — truthfulness
3. *Asteya* — non-stealing
4. *Brahmacarya* — celibacy
5. *Aparigraha* — non-possessiveness/non-attachment

Laypeople take the smaller *aṇuvratas* (minor vows) — the same five principles but with less stringent application.

**Three-fold scope of ahiṃsā:** The prohibition extends to violence by thought (*manas*), speech (*vāc*), and body (*kāya*) — the same three components of *yoga* that cause āsrava. Avoiding violence in all three dimensions is what actually stops karmic influx from violent thought/speech/action.

**Ahiṃsā and anekāntavāda — cautious note:** Some scholars (Matilal, Dhruva) describe anekāntavāda as "intellectual ahiṃsā" — epistemic non-violence towards other viewpoints. The project charter flags this as likely a modern political overlay. The classical connection is simpler and more direct: ahiṃsā applies to physical/verbal harm because physical and verbal actions cause karma; anekāntavāda is an epistemological doctrine about standpoints, not primarily an ethics of epistemic tolerance. The two are correlated (a Jain practitioner applies both) but the "intellectual ahiṃsā" equation is contested.
