## Gloss

**Buddha-nature**: every sentient being contains the *garbha* (embryo/womb/essence) of the *tathāgata* (the "thus-gone," i.e., a fully enlightened Buddha). The doctrine asserts that the potential for full awakening is universally present — not a gift, not an acquisition, but a natural characteristic of mind obscured by ignorance and defilements. The *Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra* is the earliest canonical source; the *Ratnagotravibhāga* (4th–5th c. CE, attributed to Maitreya/Asaṅga) systematised the doctrine.

**Cataphatic character** (positive predication): this is highly unusual in Buddhism. Where Madhyamaka and even Yogācāra characteristically operate apophatically (saying what reality is NOT), the tathāgatagarbha doctrine says "all sentient beings HAVE" buddha-nature — a positive affirmation. This makes it the most "Hindu-looking" Buddhist doctrine.

**Yogācāra connection**: the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* explicitly identifies tathāgatagarbha with the ālayavijñāna — the storehouse consciousness. Both are the latent, underlying substrate; both carry the karmic seeds of liberation. But basic Yogācāra (Asaṅga's school) holds that different sentient beings have different *gotra* (potentials) — not all have buddha-nature equally. Tathāgatagarbha corrects this: all have equal potential.

**Madhyamaka reading**: tathāgatagarbha is a provisional/pedagogical teaching — a *metaphor* for the potential inherent in emptiness itself. It is NOT a real entity. Even buddha-nature is śūnya (empty of svabhāva). This reconciles the doctrine with śūnyatā without positing a new ontological substrate.

**The ātman problem**: critics (Madhyamaka, Theravāda) argue that "all beings have buddha-nature" reintroduces the individual permanent self (*ātman*) that Buddhism defines itself by rejecting. The canonical defence: tathāgatagarbha is "not taught as an individual self" but as the "natural characteristic of mind, like wetness being the natural characteristic of water" — a property, not a substance.
