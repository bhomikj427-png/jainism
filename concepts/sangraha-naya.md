## Gloss

The *saṃgraha-naya* is the **generic or class standpoint** — the second of the seven nayas listed in TS 1.33. The word *saṃgraha* means collection or assembly. This standpoint comprehends different things sharing a common feature under one unified class-head, **ignoring their particular differences**. It attends to the universal, not the individual.

**Classic example — *sat* (being):** When it is claimed that "everything is *sat* (being/existent)," this is a valid saṃgraha-naya assertion. The universal property of existence groups all substances — jīva, pudgala, ākāśa, dharma, adharma, kāla — together, setting aside their specific characters. From this standpoint, the statement is correct. From the vyavahāra standpoint (which differentiates within the class), it omits too much.

**Second example — clay:** Pointing to a pot and saying "this is clay" attends to the material class (clay) rather than the particular form (pot). The shape, function, and particular history of this pot are ignored; the class-substance is what the standpoint registers.

**Third example — dravya:** When the word "substance" (dravya) is used, it groups jīva, ajīva and all their subdivisions together under the single definition of substance — ignoring their deep ontological differences. Valid from saṃgraha; incomplete from any more specific naya.

**Commentarial refinement:** WisdomLib (citing the Jain nayavāda tradition) notes that saṃgraha naya comes in two varieties — *parasaṃgraha* (treating all existents as one) and *aparasaṃgraha* (treating all of one category, e.g., all substances, as one). The two-level structure allows the standpoint to function at different degrees of generality without losing its defining characteristic: emphasis on unity over distinctness.

**Position in the naya hierarchy:** Each succeeding naya in the sequence is "purer" — more discriminating, narrower in scope. Saṃgraha is the most abstract of the three dravyārthika nayas; vyavahāra follows it with practical differentiation; naigama precedes it with its teleological breadth. Saṃgraha is the standpoint that, taken as absolute, would collapse into a monism — which is exactly why it is only a *naya* (partial perspective), not the whole truth.

## Why saṃgraha matters for anekāntavāda

Saṃgraha-naya is the standpoint from which "all is being" and "all is substance" are valid claims. But the Advaita claim "all is Brahman" or the Buddhist śūnyatā claim "all is empty" would each be saṃgraha-level truths (grouping all things under one property) that, from the Jain perspective, mistake a partial standpoint for the full picture. Anekāntavāda insists that the saṃgraha perspective must be balanced against the more discriminating nayas before reality is properly understood.
