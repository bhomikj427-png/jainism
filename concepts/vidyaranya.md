## Gloss / Divergence map

**Vidyāraṇya** (**1296–1391**), originally named **Mādhava**, was **jagadguru of the Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭham** from c. 1374–1380 until 1386 (the date of his ordination is itself given variously as 1331, 1377, or 1380). He is the figure through whom **Advaita became the dominant Hindu philosophical framework** — and the reason [[shankara|Śaṅkara]]'s modern stature exists at all.

**His central historical role, and the reason this corpus needed the node:** Vidyāraṇya had "a **central role in repositioning Śaṅkara** and his view on Advaita Vedānta." `shankara.md` records that Śaṅkara "was relatively unknown during his life-time, and probably for several centuries after," and that his towering fame is a **construction of the 14th-century Vijayanagara period** — Vidyāraṇya's promotion of him as a "divine folk-hero," pursued through works and institutional support while competing for royal patronage. This node is the other end of that claim.

**Securely his — the two Advaita works:**

- ***Pañcadaśī*** — a standard Advaita Vedānta text in **fifteen chapters**;
- ***Jīvanmukti-viveka*** — on liberation-while-living, notable for incorporating **yogic disciplines** alongside Advaita philosophy (already cited as a source-text in [[jivanmukti]]).

### Contested 1 — the *Sarva-darśana-saṅgraha* and who wrote it

The ***Sarva-darśana-saṅgraha*** ("Compendium of All Philosophies," 14th c.) is a doxography of **sixteen philosophical systems**, arranged **hierarchically from an Advaita standpoint**: Cārvāka and Buddhism lowest, as "furthest removed" from Vedānta; Sāṃkhya and Yoga highest among the non-Vedāntic systems, "approaching most nearly" to it. The **16th chapter, on Advaita Vedānta itself, is absent from the surviving text.**

| claim | who holds it |
|---|---|
| by **Mādhavācārya**, identified with Vidyāraṇya | the traditional attribution; "usually identified" |
| by **Cannibhaṭṭa / Channibhaṭṭa** (Chinna, Chennu), son of Sahajasarvajña Viṣṇu Bhaṭṭopādhyāya, working at the **Vijayanagara court** | the contesting scholarship |
| plan by Mādhava, **execution** by Cannibhaṭṭa | the compromise reading both sources report |

**A caution the arrangement itself demands.** The SDS is one of the very few surviving sources on **[[carvaka|Cārvāka]]** — `carvaka.md` records that the school's own *Bṛhaspati Sūtra* is lost and that it "survives almost entirely through hostile summaries in its opponents' works." The SDS is exactly such a channel: it quotes Cārvāka extensively **and ranks it lowest, by design, in a hierarchy built to end at Advaita**, and its own source notes the quotations may caricature. So one of the main windows onto Indian materialism is a doxography whose organising principle is the inferiority of what it is describing. (This is the fourth instance the corpus has recorded of a school surviving through an interested channel — with the Chaldean Oracles, Vindhyavāsin, and Makkhali Gosāla.)

### Contested 2 — the Vijayanagara foundation story

Tradition makes Vidyāraṇya the man who **helped establish the Vijayanagara Empire in 1336**, mentoring its founders Harihara and Bukka Rāya I and guiding three generations of kings.

> **Scholarship holds this to be largely legendary.** Contemporary inscriptions from the empire's founding make **no mention of Vidyāraṇya**; his role as advisor "was imagined probably at least 200 years afterward"; and the narrative is read as **"a political foundation myth, an ideological attempt to represent the authority of the Vijayanagara state."**

Note the shape of this, because it is unusually neat: **the man who (on the corpus's other record) built a philosopher's fame retrospectively had his own political role built retrospectively, by the same kind of process, roughly two centuries later.** The corpus records the coincidence without claiming a causal or ironic connection the sources do not draw.

### Contested 3 — how many Mādhavas, and did he write the *Digvijaya*?

**Identity.** "Some accounts conflate Vidyāraṇya with Mādhava, brother of **Sāyaṇa** (the Mīmāṃsā scholar and Veda-commentator)"; scholars distinguish **"Mādhava [A]" and "Mādhava [B]"** to keep multiple historical bearers of the name apart. Against the identification, the Śṛṅgeri accounts have the brothers **Mādhava and Sāyaṇa come *to* Vidyāraṇya** to receive his blessings and complete **his** unfinished Veda-bhāṣyas — which makes them separate people who collaborated, not one person.

**The *Mādhavīya Śaṅkara-digvijaya*** — the hagiography of Ādi Śaṅkara — is attributed to him, and the **attribution is disputed**.

> ⚠️ **An internal tension between two corpus records, flagged rather than smoothed.** `shankara.md` (and Ch 25 §3.2) state that "the most-cited [hagiography], **Mādhava's Śaṅkaradigvijaya, is 17th-century**." This node's subject died in **1391**. The two records are reconcilable **only if the attribution to this Mādhava is pseudepigraphic** — a later work travelling under a famous 14th-century name — which is precisely what "attribution disputed" would then mean. **Neither source states that reconciliation**, so it is recorded here as the likely explanation and not as a finding. Anyone upgrading either node should resolve this first; if instead the *Digvijaya* is genuinely Vidyāraṇya's, then `shankara.md`'s 17th-century dating is wrong and Ch 25 §3.2 needs correction.

3. **Search aggregation** (the Wikipedia "Madhavacharya of Sringeri" redirect; sanskritebooks.org; the Cowell & Gough translation on archive.org): independently repeats the Śṛṅgeri dates, the usual Mādhavācārya identification, the sixteen-system scope, and the "political foundation myth" verdict on the Vijayanagara story.
4. **Corpus cross-check** ([[shankara]], [[jivanmukti]], [[carvaka]]): `shankara.md` independently recorded the Vijayanagara-period fame-construction and named Vidyāraṇya for it, from SEP and Wikipedia's "Adi Shankara" in Batch 40; `jivanmukti.md` already cited the *Jīvanmukti-viveka*; `carvaka.md` independently recorded the survives-through-opponents problem this node now supplies a named channel for.

Status **contested**, because three separate identifications are open — the Mādhava/Vidyāraṇya identity, the SDS's authorship, and the *Digvijaya* attribution — and the single most famous biographical claim about him (Vijayanagara) is judged legendary by the scholarship. The **content** that is converged: his dates, the Śṛṅgeri headship, the two securely-attributed Advaita works, the SDS's structure and doxographical significance, and his repositioning of Śaṅkara. Confidence **medium**, and honestly capped: **signal 2 is a second article on the same platform** — related-but-separately-sourced, not a genuinely independent authority — and no work of his was read in any edition, though Cowell & Gough's SDS translation was surfaced and **not opened**.
