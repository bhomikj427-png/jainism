## Gloss / Divergence map

**Vərəθraγna** (Middle Persian **Warahrām**, whence **Bahrām**, **Behrām**) is the Zoroastrian *yazata* of **victory** — and the corpus's **first Iranian node**, added because he is the single most instructive comparandum for a Vedic figure the corpus already holds.

The name is built from Avestan ***vərəθra*** ("obstacle") plus the root of *\*gan-* ("to smite"), giving *vərəθragnan*, "victorious" — and the **Proto-Aryan adjective \*vṛtraghan has an exact Vedic Sanskrit cognate in *[[vritra|vṛtra]]* / *vṛtrahan*.** He "plainly enjoyed the greatest popularity of old"; beyond giving victory he "confers **virility**" and possesses the "**ability to heal**."

### The ten incarnations

The *Bahrām Yašt* has him appear to Zarathustra in **ten** successive forms — a list worth giving whole because it is unlike anything in the Vedic material:

> an impetuous **wind** · an armed **warrior** · an **adolescent of fifteen** · a gold-horned **bull** · a **white horse** with golden ears and muzzle · a **camel in heat** · a **boar** · a **bird of prey** · a **ram** · a **wild goat**

Note what the series does **not** contain: an opponent. Verethragna is not shown killing anything. He *is* the victory, taking whatever shape victory takes — which is the strongest single piece of evidence for the abstract reading below, and the reason this node earns its place beside [[vritra]] rather than merely echoing it.

⚠️ The ten forms invite comparison with the ten [[avatara-vedanta|avatāras]] of Viṣṇu, and the corpus draws that link **only** as a formal parallel. The lists share a count and a shape-shifting logic and nothing else: the avatāras are **successive descents into history** with narratives, opponents and a soteriology; the ten Vərəθraγna forms are **modes of a single appearance** to one prophet, with no chronology and no story attached to any of them. A comparison that slides from "ten forms each" to "the same doctrine" is exactly the failure this project exists to prevent.

### The dispute — the sharpest form of the *vṛtra* question

The Indo-Iranian evidence is one dataset read two ways, and the readings imply different prehistories:

| reading | who | the argument | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shared war-god** | the traditional comparative view | Verethragna corresponds to Vedic **Indra Vṛtrahan** as "descendants of a shared Proto-Indo-Aryan war-god" | a single inherited deity who kept his dragon-slaying character in India and lost it in Iran (where Zoroastrian reform demoted the *daēvas*) |
| **Abstract resistance-breaking came first** | **Benveniste and Renou** | the principal feature "wasn't slaying noxious creatures but **to overcome obstacles**" and to "**unblock the flow of *apas***" | there was no inherited dragon. Iran preserves the original — victory as *removal of resistance* — and **India's dragon is the innovation**, a personification grown around a neuter abstract noun |
| **Functional merger** | **Paul Thieme** | "Proto-Aryan *Indra* has assumed the functions of a Proto-Aryan god *\*Vṛtraghna*" — i.e. two originally distinct figures, one of whom absorbed the other | the Vedic Indra is a **composite**: an inherited god plus a separate inherited *function* that attached to him. Neither "same god" nor "same story," but a takeover |

Thieme's is the position most easily lost in summary and the most useful to hold, because it dissolves the question rather than answering it: **the Iranian *yazata* and the Vedic epithet can both be old and neither be derived from the other**, if what Iran kept as a god India kept as a job description. That would explain why *vṛtrahan* is an *epithet* in the Ṛgveda — something Indra *is called* — and a *name* in the Avesta.

The corpus records all three and adjudicates none. It notes only that the strongest datum on the table is the one in the *Bahrām Yašt* itself: **the Iranian figure has ten forms and no adversary.**

### Why the corpus keeps this node separate from *vṛtra*

Per §8, same word, genuinely different tradition → separate keys plus a typed edge. Here the split is unusually stark, because the two nodes stand on **opposite sides of the same fight**:

| | [[vritra|Vṛtra]] (Vedic) | Vərəθraγna (Avestan) |
|---|---|---|
| what the word names | the **obstruction** — the thing slain | the **smiting of obstruction** — personified as a divine being |
| status | *asura*, adversary; later a wronged avenger and a devotee of Viṣṇu | *yazata*, worshipped; "the greatest popularity of old" |
| relation to Indra | Indra's victim, and the source of Indra's epithet | on two of the three readings above, a figure Indra either **is** or has **absorbed** |
| morphology | neuter abstract *vṛtra*, "cover, obstacle" | compound *vərəθra-γna*, "obstacle-smiting" |

That is the map teaching a distinction, which is what the honesty layer is for: **an unwary reader meeting "Vṛtra ≈ Verethragna" will conclude the Zoroastrians worshipped Indra's dragon.** They worshipped its defeat.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Verethragna"** (fetched): the *vərəθra* etymology and the Warahrām → Bahrām/Behrām descent; the Proto-Aryan *\*vṛtraghan* with its Vedic cognate; **all ten incarnations** of the *Bahrām Yašt*; "plainly enjoyed the greatest popularity of old"; virility and healing; and **all three scholarly positions** — the traditional shared-war-god view, **Benveniste and Renou**, and **Paul Thieme** — stated with attribution.
2. **Wikipedia, "Vritra"** (fetched, separate article): the Vedic side of the cognate — *vṛtra* "cover, obstacle" from PIE *\*wer-*, *Ahi* as a serpent-name, the *Vṛtrahan* epithet and its "slayer of the first-born of dragons" gloss, and the Norse/Greek/Slavic dragon comparanda that the abstract reading has to explain away.
3. **Lexicon/morphology** (§4 signal 3, load-bearing here): the Avestan form is a **compound whose head is the smiting**, and the Vedic form a **simplex abstract noun** meaning the thing smitten. The two languages did different things with one root, and the grammatical difference *is* the divergence — which is why this is a case where §4's "etymology is a clue, not a verdict" cuts in favour of taking morphology seriously: the question at issue is precisely a question about word-history.
4. **Corpus cross-check** ([[vritra]], [[indra]], [[avatara-vedanta]]): [[indra]] independently records *Vṛtrahan* as an **epithet** rather than a name, and the corpus's Vaiṣṇava material supplies the ten-avatāra list against which the ten forms are compared and distinguished.

**CONTESTED** — a three-way scholarly dispute about Indo-Iranian prehistory. Converged on the etymology, the ten forms, the deity's functions and popularity, and the onward Bahrām name. Confidence **medium**, with a specific weakness: **both signals are Wikipedia articles**, so the independence is between *articles* rather than between *authorities*, and the three scholarly positions are known only through a summary of them. **No Avestan text was read**, in translation or otherwise — the *Bahrām Yašt* itself (Darmesteter's SBE translation is the obvious first fetch) was not consulted, and the ten forms are taken on the encyclopedia's word.
