## Gloss / Divergence map

**Vyāsatīrtha** (also **Vyāsarāya**, **Candrikācārya**), *rājaguru* of Vijayanagara, is "the **Prince of Dialecticians** of the Dvaita school" (B. N. K. Sharma) and the man who **started the longest-running philosophical controversy in Indian history**. His ***Nyāyāmṛta*** attacked Advaita; fifty years later Bengal answered with the *Advaitasiddhi*; and the exchange ran, in Sanskrit, **for nearly three centuries** — then reopened in English in 1964 and was answered again in 1994. **It is not obvious that it has ended.**

He entered this corpus sideways, twice, before he had a node: `madhva.md` cites Rao & Sharma's argument that the *Nyāyāmṛta* **quotes Madhva's supposedly fictitious sources as authorities**, so its silence about their authenticity is evidence; and `anandabodha.md` cites Dasgupta's remark that Vyāsatīrtha "collected standard Vedānta arguments from Ānandabodha and Prakāśātman for refutation."

### What the *Nyāyāmṛta* actually is: a collection

Two independent sources agree on the method, and it is not what "polemic" suggests.

**Dasgupta** (1932) says he *collected* the standard arguments of Ānandabodha and [[prakasatman|Prakāśātman]] in order to refute them. **Mm. Anantakrishna Sastri** — who edited the main works of *both* sides for the Calcutta Oriental Series IX, and so is not a partisan witness — says the same from the other end:

> "It was Vyāsatīrtha who, for the first time, took special pains to **collect together from the vast range of Advaita literature all the crucial points for discussion** and arrange them on a novel yet thoroughly scientific and systematic plan. He also exhibited in his work **more than a hundred points of departure**."

And Vyāsatīrtha describes his own aim in the same terms — gathering "the threads of doctrine and details of Madhva Siddhānta, found piecemeal and scattered here and there in the original works and their commentaries, and re-presenting them in a co-ordinated form."

**So the *Nyāyāmṛta* is a double compilation**: it assembles the Advaita case in order to have something worth refuting, and assembles the Dvaita case in order to have something to refute it with. That is the batch's recurring shape yet again — `dalhana.md`'s *Nibandhasaṅgraha* as archive, `nathamuni.md`'s Prabandham, [[anandabodha|Ānandabodha]] surviving because he was borrowed from. **Compilation is how positions survive.**

⚠ It also means the *Nyāyāmṛta* is a **source for Advaita**, not only an attack on it — which is exactly why `madhva.md` can use its quotations as evidence.

### Demolition precedes construction — the contrast with Śrīharṣa

Sharma is emphatic that the work's "object and purpose is **not purely negative or destructive. Demolition precedes construction.** The realistic metaphysics rises on the ashes of the Monistic Advaita." Its four *paricchedas* run through *pañcabheda*, bondage, the *sādhanas*, bhakti and grace, and the gradation of bliss in mokṣa — a positive system, not a clearing operation.

Set that beside [[sriharsa|Śrīharṣa]], written earlier this batch: a book of **pure** refutation, with no thesis to read off, whose author's affiliation is contested *because* he never states one. **Two great Indian works of demolition, and they differ on precisely the question of whether demolition is the whole job.** Śrīharṣa's silence made him claimable by the school he may not have joined; Vyāsatīrtha's constructive half makes his allegiance unmistakable. The methodological point is durable: *what a polemicist declines to assert determines who can later claim him.*

### The chain, and its dates

| round | work | author | side |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ***Nyāyāmṛta*** | **Vyāsatīrtha** | Dvaita |
| 2 | ***Advaitasiddhi*** — a line-by-line refutation, from **Bengal, fifty years later** | [[madhusudana-sarasvati|Madhusūdana Sarasvatī]] | Advaita |
| 3 | ***Nyāyāmṛtataraṅgiṇī***; ***Nyāyāmṛtakaṇṭakoddhāra*** | Vyāsa Rāmācārya; (Pāṇḍuraṅgi) Ānanda Bhaṭṭāraka | Dvaita |
| 4 | ***Guru-/Laghucandrikā*** | Brahmānanda Sarasvatī | Advaita |
| 5 | *Taraṅgiṇīsaurabha* / *Nyāyāmṛtasaugandhya* | Vanamāli Miśra | Dvaita |
| 6 | ***Vitthaleśī*** on the *Laghucandrikā* — after which the exchange "seemed to come to an end" | Vitthaleśa Upādhyāya | Advaita |
| 7 | *A Critique of Mādhva Refutation of the Śāṅkara School of Vedānta* (1964, repr. 1985) | **K. Narain**, Allahabad University | Advaita |
| 8 | *Advaitasiddhi vs Nyāyāmṛta: An Up To Date Critical Re-Appraisal* (1994) | **B. N. K. Sharma** | Dvaita |

Sharma dates the Sanskrit phase "from Vyāsatīrtha in the first quarter of the 16th century and ending with the 18th," and says Narain "reopened it in modern garb in English." **Rounds 7 and 8 are the same controversy in another language**, and Sharma says so.

⚠ **A divergence on his dates, and the corpus should note which way it leans.** Wikipedia gives **1447–1539**; B. N. K. Sharma gives **1460–1539** — and so, independently, do Rao & Sharma in *Asiatische Studien* (2003), already cited in `madhva.md`. The 1460 reading is better attested here and is used above; the thirteen-year discrepancy is unexplained by either source.

### The other two works, and a lost link to Śrīharṣa

The *Tātparyacandrikā* compares Śaṅkara's, Madhva's and Rāmānuja's readings of the Brahmasūtra — a **three-way** commentary, which is why he is called Candrikācārya. The *Tarkatāṇḍava* attacks **Nyāya** epistemology.

Sharma also records that Vyāsatīrtha "modelled his *Nyāyāmṛta* on the *Vādāvalī* and *Vādaratnāvalī* of his predecessors" — so even the founding move of the controversy is an enlargement of earlier work, not a beginning. And among those predecessors' works is **Viṣṇudāsācārya's *Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍana*** — literally "the refutation of the *Khaṇḍana*," a **Dvaita reply to Śrīharṣa**. It does **not survive**; it is known only because the *Vādaratnāvalī* names it and **Vyāsatīrtha quotes it in the *Nyāyāmṛta***.

⚠ That closes a circuit worth marking: `sriharsa.md` lists the Advaitin and Naiyāyika responses to the *Khaṇḍana* from SEP, and **no Dvaita response**. There was one; it is lost; and its only witness is the man who started the next war.

### ⭐ Batch 46: the non-partisan specialist, read at last

Batch 45 closed this file with a standing complaint — **"everything in `vyasatirtha.md` currently leans on a committed Dvaita partisan"** — and named **Valerie Stoker** and **Michael Williams** as the two modern specialists who could correct it. **Williams has now been read**, at first hand: *"The Impact of Navya-Nyāya on Mādhva Vedānta: Vyāsatīrtha and the Problem of Empty Terms," Journal of Indian Philosophy 49.2 (2020), 205–232* — peer-reviewed and open access. **Follow-up partly closed.** Four results:

**1. The dates.** Williams gives **1460–1539**, independently of Sharma. That is now **three** sources for 1460 (Sharma 1994; Rao & Sharma 2003 in *Asiatische Studien*; Williams 2020) against Wikipedia's 1447. **This file's lean is confirmed by a non-partisan specialist.**

**2. A Sharma citation that could not be verified.** On the question whether [[jayatirtha|Jayatīrtha]] already knew Gaṅgeśa, Williams writes: *"Sharma ([1961], p. 252) has claimed that Jayatīrtha was aware of Gaṅgeśa"* — and then: ***"I have not been able to locate this verse in any modern edition of the text."*** ⚠ **An unlocated verse is not a fabricated one**, and Williams claims only what he checked. But this is precisely the independent check Batch 45 asked for, and it landed on a citation rather than an interpretation. It does **not** touch the *Nyāyāmṛta* material this file rests on, which is doubly sourced through Anantakrishna Sastri and Dasgupta.

**3. He was the first Mādhva to take on Navya-Nyāya.** Williams: Vyāsatīrtha was **"the first member of the Mādhva tradition to engage in detail with the ideas of Gaṅgeśa and his followers in Mithila."** So the *Nyāyāmṛta* is not only the opening of the Dvaita–Advaita controversy; it is the **arrival of [[navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]] in Vedānta polemic**.

**4. ⚠⚠ And a finding that sharpens this file's own "collection" thesis into something stranger.** Williams shows the two great works use Gaṅgeśa in **opposite directions**:

| work | target | what he does with Navya-Nyāya |
|---|---|---|
| ***Nyāyāmṛta*** | Advaita | **"adopted Navya-Nyāya conventions and positions even though they conflicted with his own, Mādhva, views"** |
| ***Tarkatāṇḍava*** ("Death-Dance of Logic") | Nyāya itself | **critiqued those same positions directly** |

**He borrows a weapon to fight one opponent and then attacks the weapon when he turns to face its owner.** That is not inconsistency; it is the *collection* method this file already identifies, extended — the *Nyāyāmṛta* collects **whatever will defeat an Advaitin**, including positions its author does not hold. It also means **the *Nyāyāmṛta* cannot be read as a straightforward statement of Vyāsatīrtha's own views**, which is a real constraint on every use this corpus makes of it.

⚠ **Williams's methodological verdict, adopted here**: he distances himself from **Karl Potter**'s emphasis on Navya-Nyāya as "rigorous terminology," holding that "the available Sanskrit literature does **not** suggest that Mādhva philosophers regarded their encounter with Navya-Nyāya primarily as a matter of embracing an updated technical language." **Arguments crossed, not jargon.**

⚠ **Still open**: **Valerie Stoker, *Polemics and Patronage in the City of Victory*** remains unread, and it is the one that would test the Vijayanagara/patronage side of this file rather than the philosophical side.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — B. N. K. Sharma, *Advaitasiddhi vs Nyāyāmṛta: An Up To Date Critical Re-Appraisal*, Part I, Ānandatīrtha Pratiṣṭhāna, Bangalore, 1994 — read directly** (Internet Archive full text). A monograph by a named professor (Rupārel College, Bombay), and the single richest source: the 1460–1539 dates, "Prince of Dialecticians," sixth in succession from Jayatīrtha, the *Vādāvalī*/*Vādaratnāvalī* models, Viṣṇudāsācārya's three works of which only one survives, "demolition precedes construction," the four *paricchedas*, Vyāsatīrtha's self-statement of purpose, the fifty-year gap before the *Advaitasiddhi*, "nearly three centuries," Anantakrishna Sastri's assessment quoted at p. 36, Nṛsiṃhāśrama's *Tattvaviveka* citing the *Nyāyāmṛta*, and the Narain–Sharma modern round. ⚠ **Sharma is a committed Dvaita partisan** — the book is a reply on behalf of one side — and is used accordingly: for bibliography, chronology and quotation of others, not for verdicts. **Signal 2 — Mm. Anantakrishna Sastri**, quoted within signal 1: the editor of both sides' texts for the Calcutta Oriental Series IX, and therefore the least partisan voice available on what the *Nyāyāmṛta* is. **Signal 3 — Wikipedia, "Vyasatirtha"**: 1447–1539; Sāḷuva Narasiṃha, Tuḷuva Narasa Nāyaka and Kṛṣṇadevarāya; the three works' subjects; the reply-chain with named authors; and the modern scholars **B. N. K. Sharma, Valerie Stoker and Michael Williams**. **Signal 4 — corpus-internal**: `anandabodha.md` (Dasgupta's independent statement that Vyāsatīrtha collected Ānandabodha's and Prakāśātman's arguments) and `madhva.md` (Rao & Sharma 2003, the *Nyāyāmṛta*'s quotation of the disputed sources, and the 1460–1539 dating).

**Independence, and where it matters.** The load-bearing claim — that the *Nyāyāmṛta*'s method is **collection** — is carried by **Dasgupta (1932, via `anandabodha.md`)** and **Anantakrishna Sastri (Calcutta Oriental Series)** independently, from opposite ends and neither derivable from the other. That is why it is stated flatly. Everything resting on Sharma alone is attributed to Sharma.

**Why converged.** Who he was, what he wrote, what the *Nyāyāmṛta* does, whom it targets and what it provoked are agreed across a partisan monograph, a neutral editor, a modern encyclopedia and a 1932 history. The one divergence — 1447 vs 1460 — is bounded and reported. **Why medium, not high.** **No work of Vyāsatīrtha's was read**: the *Nyāyāmṛta* is known here only through Sharma's quotations and summary; the self-statement of purpose is quoted from Sharma's English rendering, not from the Sanskrit; Stoker and Williams, the two modern non-partisan specialists named, were **not consulted**; and the single most interesting item — the lost *Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍana* — rests on Sharma alone.
