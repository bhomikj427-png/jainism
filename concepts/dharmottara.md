## Gloss

An 8th-century (~740–800 CE) Buddhist philosopher from Kashmir, the most influential commentator on Dharmakīrti's epistemological works and the founder of the Kashmiri school of Buddhist logic. Dharmottara's significance is twofold: he transmitted the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti tradition into its most systematic and philosophically refined form in India, and he was the primary vehicle through which this tradition entered Tibetan Buddhist education. His Nyāyabinduṭīkā — the only one of his works to survive in Sanskrit — became a foundational text in the Tibetan monastic curriculum.

## Position in the hetu-vidyā inheritance chain

Dignāga (~480–540 CE) → Dharmakīrti (~625 CE) → **Dharmottara (~740–800 CE)** → Tibetan pramāṇa tradition

Dharmottara is the key node between Dharmakīrti's seven treatises and their Tibetan reception. Without his commentary, Dharmakīrti's terse texts would have been significantly harder to interpret.

## Philosophical innovations

**1. Definition of valid cognition as verification-capable**
Dharmottara defines pramāṇa (valid cognition) as knowledge "capable of verification" — cognition that "does not disagree with the objective reality represented in it." This adds an epistemic-success criterion not explicitly foregrounded in Dharmakīrti's own wording; valid cognition must be confirmable by subsequent experience.

**2. Niścaya-pratyaya (ascertaining cognition)**
Dharmottara introduces a three-stage cognitive model: (a) initial perceptual experience → (b) niścaya-pratyaya (ascertaining cognition) → (c) action/understanding. The ascertaining cognition bridges raw perception and propositional understanding through exclusion (anyāpoha). This was subsequently challenged by Prajñākaragupta, who rejected the intermediary stage — the debate marks the internal development of the hetu-vidyā tradition after Dharmakīrti.

**3. Kashmiri subjective idealism**
Dharmottara's school interpreted the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti tradition through a Yogācāra (mind-only) lens: cognizable objects are internal to consciousness. This contrasts with more realist readings. It is a live interpretive debate — the Sautrāntika reading of Dharmakīrti treats external objects as inferred causes of experience, while the Yogācāra reading (Dharmottara's) eliminates them entirely.

## Tibetan reception

The Nyāyabinduṭīkā is called *chos mchog chung* ("The small Dharmottara") in Tibetan. Dharmottara was instrumental in transmitting pramāṇa (Tib. *tshad ma*) studies to Tibet. Through Tibetan translations of his works (and of Dharmakīrti's works with his commentary context), the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti logical tradition became the backbone of Tibetan monastic philosophical training — a role it continues to hold.
