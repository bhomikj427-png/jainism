## Gloss / Divergence map

The ***Aṣṭādhyāyī*** ("the eight-chaptered") is [[panini|Pāṇini]]'s grammar of Sanskrit: a rule-system in **eight *adhyāyas*, each of four *pādas***, that derives correct Sanskrit forms from roots, stems and affixes. It is the root text of [[vyakarana|Vyākaraṇa]] and the object of every later commentary in the tradition.

Abhyankar's one-line characterisation is the most useful thing said about it, because it states the genre rather than praising the achievement: **"The work is a linguist's and not a language teacher's."** It is a *description of a system*, not an aid to learning the language. Its rules cover "declension, conjugation, euphonic changes, verbal derivatives, noun derivatives and accents."

⚠ It does not travel alone. The grammar as used depends on **ancillary lists** that are not part of the eight chapters: the **Śivasūtras** (fourteen sound-classes, see below), the **Dhātupāṭha** (a roster of verbal roots — Abhyankar counts "all the two thousand and two hundred roots"), and the **Gaṇapāṭha** (lists of words behaving alike). **A rule that says "the roots in list X take affix Y" is not self-contained**; the Aṣṭādhyāyī is the smaller half of its own apparatus.

### The divergence: nobody agrees how many rules it has

The Aṣṭādhyāyī is arguably the most precisely transmitted long text of world antiquity — memorised verbatim, checked syllable by syllable, recited in two hours end to end. **And its sources give at least five different totals.**

| count | who gives it (source) | what the number depends on |
|---|---|---|
| **3,983** | **Abhyankar** — the total "as commented upon by the writers of the Kāśikā and the Siddhāntakaumudī," and the number in "the traditional recital by Veda scholars who look upon the Aṣṭādhyāyī as a Vedāṅga" | The **commentarial** canon: what the standard commentaries treat as a sūtra |
| **3,996** | **Abhyankar**, reporting "a verse current among Vaiyākaraṇa schools" | A mnemonic verse — the tradition's own round figure |
| **3,981** | Wikipedia, "Mahābhāṣya" (the Mahābhāṣya covers "1228 of the 3981 sūtras") | Whichever recension that article's source counted |
| **3,959** | Wikipedia, "Pāṇini" | Unstated |
| **lower still** | **Patañjali**, *per* Abhyankar — because **nine** of the received sūtras "are described as Vārttikas and two as Gaṇasūtras by Patañjali" | A **doctrinal** decision: lines the tradition recites as Pāṇini's, Patañjali assigns to [[katyayana|Kātyāyana]] or to the word-lists |

⚠ **The disagreement is not sloppiness — it is the interesting part.** The count varies because *what counts as a sūtra of Pāṇini* is a **judgement**, not an observation: the received recitation includes lines the greatest commentator says are not Pāṇini's. **The text is stable and its boundary is not.** This is [[vyakarana]]'s *munitraya* finding in numerical form — the Aṣṭādhyāyī as transmitted is already the three-author composite, and counting it means deciding where one author stops.

⚠ **Arithmetic caveat, flagged rather than smoothed:** the scan gives Patañjali's figure as **3,979**, but 3,983 − 9 − 2 = 3,972. The two do not reconcile, and the discrepancy may be an OCR digit error in a two-column scan. **The exact figure is therefore not asserted here** — only the reason it is lower.

### The devices, and one that has no name in the text

**Anubandha / *it*-markers.** Abhyankar, at first hand: these are "a letter or letters added to a word before or after it, **only to signify some specific purpose**" — and Pāṇini calls them ***it***, "lit. going or disappearing," because "they do **not** form an essential part of the word to which they are attached, the word in usage being always found **without** the *it* letter."

**This is a genuine metalanguage, and it is worth being exact about why.** The *it*-letters exist in the rule-language and are absent from the object-language. Pāṇini marks affixes with symbols that are guaranteed never to surface in any Sanskrit word — a diacritic layer that the derivation consumes and deletes. That is a real formal property of the text, statable without any modern comparison.

**Pratyāhāra** — the abbreviation device. The fourteen **Śivasūtras** (also *Akṣarasamāmnāya* or *Māheśvara Sūtras*) list the sounds of Sanskrit in a contrived order, each group closed by a mute marker; a pratyāhāra then names any contiguous run by pairing its first sound with a closing marker, so that whole phonological classes get two-syllable names. Abhyankar: it "include[s] all letters beginning with the letter uttered and ending with the letter which precedes the (mute) letter."

⚠ **And here the corpus's batch-long mechanism appears in a technical guise.** Abhyankar, directly:

> "The term *pratyāhāra* is **not actually used by Pāṇini**; it is found in the Ṛk Tantra… The term appears to have come into use **after** Pāṇini. **Pāṇini has not given any definition of the term.** He has simply given the *method* of forming the Pratyāhāras and he has profusely used them."

**The single most famous device in the Aṣṭādhyāyī is one its author uses constantly, never names, and never defines.** The name, the definition, and the doctrine of pratyāhāra are the tradition's, supplied afterwards over a working practice. Abhyankar adds that the practice itself predates Pāṇini — "possibly in the grammar attributed to Indra," a work he elsewhere doubts Pāṇini ever had.

⚠ This is the **third** instance in one batch of a blank in the text being filled from outside it: Haradatta reading the sphoṭa doctrine out of the name *Sphoṭāyana* ([[sphota]]); Jinendrabuddhi reading Pāṇini's biography out of A 4.3.94 ([[panini]]); and here, a whole technical vocabulary supplied for a method the text performs but does not describe. The first two are the mechanism producing *false* history. **This third one produces a perfectly good analysis** — the pratyāhāra account is correct, and the practice is genuinely there. **That contrast is the honest lesson: the mechanism is not inherently corrupting; it is inherently invisible.** The corpus's job is to notice when it has operated, not to assume the result is wrong.

### ⚠ The formal-grammar comparison, and its exact limits

The Aṣṭādhyāyī attracts modern comparison more than any other text in this corpus, and §0 applies with full force. What is *fairly* said, all of it **structural**:
- It has been "described as 'the first context-sensitive formal model of language', showing 'many features of a formal, computationally implementable system' **comparable to** the modern **Backus–Naur form**."
- Its derivational technique "has been compared to the **rewrite systems** developed in the 1920s–1930s by the logician **Emil Post**"; Wikipedia reports that "Sanskritists now accept that Pāṇini's linguistic apparatus is well-described as an **'applied' Post system**."
- It has been compared to a **Turing machine** as an idealised reduction of a computing device.
- **Chomsky**: "the first generative grammar in the modern sense was Pāṇini's grammar."

**What none of this licenses.** These are descriptions of the Aṣṭādhyāyī *in modern terms by modern readers*; they are not claims that Pāṇini possessed the concepts of a formal language, a rewrite system, or computability. The text has no notion of a machine, of decidability, or of a grammar as one member of a hierarchy of formal power. **"Well-described as an applied Post system" is a statement about the describer's vocabulary, not about Pāṇini's.** The corpus records the parallel as `structurally-parallel-to` and never as identity.

And the brake is supplied from inside the discipline. **Cardona**, against the genealogical version of the claim — that Pāṇini shaped modern linguistics *through* Saussure — writes of Saussure's work that "it shows **no direct influence** of Pāṇinian grammar. Indeed, on occasion, Saussure follows a path that is **contrary** to Pāṇinian procedure." **The resemblance is real; the transmission is not.**

### Composed for the ear

The text "was composed in an era when oral composition and transmission was the norm, [and] is staunchly embedded in that oral tradition" — which is why it is compressed to the point of unreadability without a commentary: "Pāṇini is said to have preferred brevity over clarity." Whether he *wrote* it is disputed: he demonstrably knew of writing (the words *lipi*, "script," and *lipikara*, "scribe," occur at A 3.2), and **Vergiani** argues the grammar's structure and complexity "offer evidence for its composition and transmission through **written** means." **Knowing of script and using it are different claims**, and only the first is settled.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Abhyankar, *A Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (**read at first hand**, full scan): the 3,983 / 3,996 counts and their sources; Patañjali's reassignment of 9 sūtras to vārttikas and 2 to gaṇasūtras; the *anubandha*/*it* definition; the pratyāhāra entry including the decisive statement that Pāṇini neither names nor defines the device; the Śivasūtras as *Akṣarasamāmnāya*; the 2,200 roots of the Dhātupāṭha; "a linguist's and not a language teacher's."
2. **Wikipedia, "Pāṇini"** (fetched, independent): 3,959 sūtras; eight adhyāyas of four pādas; the four ancillary texts; the formal-grammar comparisons with attributions (Post, BNF, Turing, Chomsky) **and** Cardona's Saussure caution; oral composition; the *lipi*/*lipikara* evidence and Vergiani's written-composition argument.
3. **Wikipedia, "Mahābhāṣya"** (fetched, independent): 3,981 sūtras, of which the Mahābhāṣya treats 1,228 — a third count, and a measure of how *little* of Pāṇini the great commentary actually covers.
4. **SEP, "Language and Testimony in Classical Indian Philosophy"** (fetched): Pāṇini's treatment of Sanskrit "as an atemporal language," with regional and scriptural usage as sub-domains of one system.
5. **Internal consistency**: the counting problem is the same shape as [[vyakarana]]'s *munitraya* sense-2 (the text identified with its commentators) and as [[tattvacintamani]]'s migration of the object of study upward into the commentaries.

**Contested**: the sūtra count genuinely differs across sources, and the difference has a substantive cause rather than a clerical one. Confidence **medium** — the structure, devices and ancillaries are firmly established across independent sources and one first-hand specialist lexicon, but no critical edition of the Aṣṭādhyāyī itself was consulted, which is exactly what would settle the count.
