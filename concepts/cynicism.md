## Gloss / Divergence map

**Cynicism** (Greek *Kynismos*, "dog-like") is the radically ascetic Greek school descending from Socrates through **Antisthenes** (c. 445–365 BCE), made famous by **Diogenes of Sinope** (c. 412–323 BCE), and carried by **Crates of Thebes** (c. 365–285 BCE) — who **taught Zeno of Citium**, the founder of Stoicism. There is no founding text; the school is a way of life transmitted through anecdote.

**The one demand: virtue, by nature, against convention.** The Cynics held **virtue (aretē) to be the only good** and the sole route to happiness (**eudaimonia**) — and that virtue is achieved by **living "according to nature" (kata physin)** in deliberate defiance of social **convention (nomos)**. Everything convention prizes — wealth, power, reputation, comfort — is rejected as worthless and enslaving. The practical programme:

- **autarkeia** — self-sufficiency: needing as little as possible (Diogenes' barrel, his single cloak), so that nothing external can be taken from you;
- **askēsis** — rigorous training in hardship, toughening the self against fortune;
- **anaideia** — "shamelessness": doing in public what convention deems private, to expose convention as arbitrary;
- **parrhēsia** — frank, fearless truth-telling, even to the powerful;
- **eleutheria** and **kosmopolitēs** — freedom from civic constraint; Diogenes called himself a "citizen of the world."

**Relation to Stoicism.** Cynicism is the direct ancestor of [[stoicism]]: Zeno was Crates' student, and the Stoics later called Cynicism "the short path to virtue." But the Stoics softened it — see the NOT-equivalent edge.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1** (IEP, "Cynics"): Antisthenes → Diogenes (c. 412–323) → Crates → (Hipparchia, Zeno); virtue the only good, achieved by living *kata physin* against *nomos*; the cluster autarkeia / askēsis / anaideia / parrhēsia / eleutheria; Diogenes the *kosmopolitēs*; rejection of wealth and institutions; direct influence on Stoicism (shared virtue-sufficiency, ethical primacy, askēsis). **Signal 2** (Wikipedia, "Cynicism (philosophy)"): Antisthenes (c. 445–365), Diogenes (c. 412–323), Crates (c. 365–285) who taught Zeno; virtue in agreement with nature; rejection of wealth/power/pleasure/glory/recognition; autarkeia, askēsis, anaideia, parrhēsia, atuphia; profound influence on Stoicism (Apollodorus: "Cynicism is the short path to virtue").

The two are independent (a peer-reviewed encyclopedia and a reference article) and agree on the lineage, dates, the virtue-and-nature core, the ascetic vocabulary, and the line to Stoicism. **Medium, not high**: there is no primary Cynic text to anchor against (the school is anecdotal, transmitted by the later doxographer Diogenes Laërtius); the Greek terms are cross-checked between the two summaries but not against a critical edition.
