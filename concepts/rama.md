## Gloss

**Rāma** (Sanskrit *rāma*, "pleasing, charming"; also "dark") is the **seventh avatāra of Viṣṇu** and one of the two most popular deities of Hindu *bhakti* (with Kṛṣṇa). Prince of Ayodhyā, son of Daśaratha and Kausalyā, husband of Sītā — his life is narrated in the **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa**: birth, marriage, fourteen-year forest exile, the abduction of Sītā by Rāvaṇa, the war at Laṅkā with the aid of Hanumān, and the return to establish the ideal reign (*Rāma-rājya*).

**Maryādā Puruṣottama** — "the supreme upholder of *maryādā* (proper bounds / dharma)." Rāma is the paradigm of dharmic conduct: he embodies duty, filial obedience, and rule-bound righteousness, often at great personal cost. This makes him the structural counterpart to Kṛṣṇa, whose divinity is expressed through transgressive *līlā* (play) rather than rule-keeping. The Rāmāyaṇa repeatedly stages *dharma-saṅkaṭa* — situations where right action is genuinely ambiguous (the killing of Vālin; the banishment of Sītā) — and Rāma's choices are the tradition's case-law in ethics.

**Devotional and literary afterlife.** Rāma-*bhakti* flowered in the **Rāmānandī Sampradāya** (founded by Rāmānanda, 14th c. — one of Hinduism's largest monastic orders, egalitarian across caste and gender) and in **Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas*** (16th c. Awadhi), which carried Rāma-devotion to the masses and grounds the annual *Rāmlīlā* performances. The **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** rereads the whole story through Advaita Vedānta — Rāma as Brahman, Sītā as māyā/prakṛti.

Avatāra-status note: in the Vālmīki text the explicit divine identification (Rāma = Viṣṇu) is concentrated in the framing Bāla and Uttara Kāṇḍas, generally held to be later additions; the older core (Books 2–6) presents Rāma chiefly as an ideal human hero. The full avatāra theology is most developed in the later devotional retellings.
