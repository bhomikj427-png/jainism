## Gloss

Non-violence as a Vedic/Hindu virtue — ethically required across traditions but contextualised within the framework of *dharma*, which permits exceptions: warrior duty (kṣatriya-dharma), criminal punishment, and medical necessity. Hindu ahiṃsā is not absolute; it is one virtue among a network of dharmic obligations. This distinguishes it sharply from Jain ahiṃsā (absolute prohibition grounded in every jīva having a soul) and situates it closer to Buddhist ahiṃsā (intention-based, gradient rather than absolute).

## Historical development

| period | text | development |
|---|---|---|
| Early Vedic (~1500 BCE) | Rigveda; Kapisthala Katha Saṃhitā (Yajurveda) | Rigveda records ritual animal sacrifice; Kapisthala Katha Saṃhitā contains earliest reference to pashu-ahiṃsā (non-harm to animals) in a moral sense |
| Upanishadic (~800–600 BCE) | Chāndogya Upanishad | Lists ahiṃsā among five essential virtues alongside satya (truthfulness), ārjava (sincerity), dāna (charity), and tapas; links its observance to escape from the rebirth cycle |
| Epic (~400 BCE–400 CE) | Mahābhārata (Anuśāsana Parva) | "Ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ" — "non-violence is the highest moral virtue"; yet warriors may kill in battle without sin; hunting permitted for kṣatriyas, forbidden for hermits |
| Classical Yoga (~400 CE) | Yoga Sūtra II.30 (Patañjali) | Ahiṃsā is the first of five yamas (moral restraints): ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha; foundational to all other yamas; applies to thought, word, and deed |
| Medical (~600 CE) | Suśruta Saṃhitā | Recommends various meats for specific ailments and for pregnant women — within Hindu ethical framework, medical necessity overrides the general principle |

## Key difference from Jain ahiṃsā: dharmic exceptions

Hindu tradition explicitly permits violence in specific roles and contexts:
- **Kṣatriya dharma:** warriors who fight and kill in just war commit no moral injury
- **Capital punishment:** just, proportional, non-cruel punishment is permitted by dharmic texts
- **Sacrifice:** Vedic ritual sacrifice of animals predates and exists alongside ahiṃsā as a parallel obligation — later Hindu reform movements (e.g., Vaiṣṇava bhakti) increasingly rejected this
- **Medicine:** harm in medical treatment is permitted as necessity

The Jain position explicitly rejects all these exceptions. Jain ascetics may not farm (risk of killing soil-bodied jīvas), may not eat after dark, and must sweep the ground before sitting — no dharmic exception overrides the soul-protection principle.

## Cross-tradition comparison

| feature | Hindu ahiṃsā | Jain ahiṃsā | Buddhist ahiṃsā |
|---|---|---|---|
| metaphysical ground | all life is sacred (ātman/brahman); souls interconnected | every jīva has a soul (upayoga); harm = karma bondage | all sentient beings suffer; intentional harm generates negative karma |
| scope of prohibition | graduated: applies to all, but exceptions for dharma-roles | near-absolute for monks; strict for laypeople; no dharmic exceptions | intention-based: unintentional harm is less grave; mettā extends to all sentient beings |
| exceptions | warrior duty, punishment, medicine, ritual | essentially none for ascetics; laypeople: 1-sensed jīva harm unavoidable but minimised | no formal exceptions; but unintentional harm treated as less grave |
| primary obligation type | one virtue among networked dharmic duties | first and supreme mahāvrata | first precept (pāṇātipātā veramaṇī); also a brahma-vihāra (mettā) |
| textual authority | Vedas, Upanishads, Mahābhārata, Yoga Sūtra | Ācārāṅga Sūtra; TS Chapter 7; 6-jīva-category ontology | Dhammapada 10.129; Sutta Piṭaka |
