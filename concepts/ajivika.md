## Gloss

The *Ājīvika* school was a major **śramaṇa** (renouncer) movement of the 5th century BCE — a direct contemporary and rival of Jainism and Buddhism — founded by **Makkhali Gosāla**. It belongs to the *nāstika* (heterodox, non-Vedic) cluster alongside Jainism, Buddhism, and Cārvāka. Its defining doctrine is **niyati** ("fate," "destiny"): *absolute determinism*. Everything that has happened, is happening, or will happen is entirely preordained; there is **no free will**; and — the point that made it Jainism's mirror-opposite — **human effort and karma have no power to alter one's destiny**. The soul does transmigrate, but through a *fixed* number of rebirths (a vast preset span) that ascetic striving can neither shorten nor lengthen. The school flourished under the Mauryas (especially Bindusāra), declined in the north after the 2nd century BCE, and survived in South India until roughly the 14th century. Its texts are lost; it is known almost entirely through *hostile* Jain and Buddhist polemic — a serious source-limitation the corpus flags rather than hides.

**Signal 2 — Britannica/UPSC philosophy sources + Buddhist *Sāmaññaphala Sutta*:** Independently confirm Gosāla, *niyati* as absolute fatalism, the explicit denial of free will and the efficacy of effort, and the contrast with Jain personal-effort soteriology; confirm decline via "extreme doctrine, lack of institutionalization, overshadowed by Buddhism/Jainism."

**Independence:** Encyclopedic, philosophy-survey, and (indirectly) the Buddhist canonical account converge on the doctrine and the history. Confidence medium — but with a *structural* caveat sharper than the corpus norm: **every source is downstream of the movement's enemies.** No Ājīvika self-statement survives, so the doctrine as we have it is the *Jain/Buddhist caricature* of it. The convergence is real; the underlying portrait may be unfair.

## The exact contrast with Jainism — niyati alone vs. niyati as one of five

This is why Ājīvika earns a place in a *Jain* corpus: it is the **precise inversion of the Jain account of agency**, and the contrast sharpens both.

| | Ājīvika | Jainism |
|---|---|---|
| destiny (*niyati*) | the *sole* determinant — absolute | *one* causal factor among several |
| human effort (*puruṣārtha*) | **powerless** — cannot change one's fate | **decisive** — asceticism directly sheds karma → mokṣa |
| karma | exists, but inert — alters nothing | physical matter whose influx/shedding the soul *controls* (Ch 03) |
| liberation | reached by *all* souls eventually, on a fixed schedule, effort or not | reached *only* by deliberate effort up the gunasthāna ladder |

Jain doctrine recognizes **five co-operating factors (*samavāya*)** in any event — *svabhāva* (nature), *niyati* (destiny), *kāla* (time), *pūrva-karma* (prior karma), and *puruṣārtha* (effort). The Ājīvika error, from the Jain side, is **absolutizing one of these five** (niyati) and annihilating the rest — above all *puruṣārtha*, the effort on which the entire Jain path depends. The whole apparatus of the vows, tapas, saṃvara, and nirjarā (Ch 08, Ch 03) presupposes that effort *works*; if niyati were total, Jain soteriology would be pointless. So the Ājīvika is the negative space that defines Jain free-will realism.

## Gosāla and Mahāvīra

Jain sources record that Gosāla accompanied Mahāvīra as a companion/disciple for about **six years** before a bitter split and the founding of his own determinist doctrine — making the Ājīvika not a distant rival but a *schism-adjacent* movement born in the same ascetic milieu (mahavira.md). The personal rupture is dramatized (and surely sharpened) in the Jain *Bhagavatī Sūtra* — which is itself the warning: the most detailed account of Gosāla comes from the tradition with the strongest motive to discredit him.
