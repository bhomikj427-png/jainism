## Gloss / Divergence map

**Vṛtra** is [[indra|Indra]]'s adversary in the Ṛgveda's central myth: he "kept the waters of the world captive until he was killed by Indra, who destroyed all the **99 fortresses** of Vṛtra before liberating the imprisoned rivers." The fullest telling is **RV 1.32**, where "Vṛtra broke Indra's two jaws during the battle, but was then thrown down by Indra and, in falling, crushed the fortresses that had already been shattered." From this Indra takes his standing epithet ***Vṛtrahan*** — "**Slayer of Vṛtra**," and also "slayer of the first-born of dragons."

### The contested question: is Vṛtra a dragon or an obstruction?

This is not a quibble about imagery. It decides what the Ṛgveda's central myth *is about*, and the split has named holders.

| reading | evidence | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Vṛtra is a serpent-dragon** — the demon-slaying reading | Vṛtra is "known in the Vedas as ***Ahi***" (अहि, 'snake'); *Vṛtrahan* glossed as "slayer of the first-born of dragons"; the comparative pull of Jörmungandr, Typhon and the Slavic dragon | the Vedic myth is an instance of the widespread Indo-European **combat myth**, and Indra is a dragon-slayer of the standard type |
| **Vṛtra is "the obstruction"** — the abstract reading, **Benveniste and Renou** | *vṛtra* "literally means '**cover, obstacle**'," from Proto-Indo-Iranian *\*wr̥trás*, from PIE *\*wer-* "to cover, to obstruct." On this reading the principal feature of the myth "wasn't slaying noxious creatures but **to overcome obstacles**" and to "**unblock the flow of *apas***," the waters | the myth is about **resistance-breaking**, and the dragon is a personification that grew around an abstract noun rather than a monster that acquired a name. **The demon is downstream of the word** |

The etymological point is the hinge, and it is genuinely awkward for the dragon reading: *vṛtra* is a **neuter abstract** meaning "obstruction," and its personification as a masculine adversary is a development *within* the language, not a starting point. But it is also true that the Ṛgveda already calls him *Ahi* — so if the abstraction came first, the personification was complete before the hymns we have.

The corpus records this as contested and does not adjudicate. What it will say is that **which reading you take changes what [[indra]] is**: on reading A he is a monster-killer; on reading B he is the power that makes flow possible, and the Ṛgveda's obsessive interest in released waters is the point rather than the scenery. The link to [[rta]] is worth holding in view — a cosmos whose central problem is *blockage* is a cosmos ordered by flow.

### The Purāṇic Vṛtra: an inversion

The later tradition rewrites him twice over, and each rewrite costs Indra something.

- **Mahābhārata**: "Vṛtra was an asura created by the artisan god **Tvaṣṭṛ** to avenge the killing of his son known as **Triśiras** or **Viśvarūpa** by Indra." Vṛtra now has a *motive*, and it is Indra's own prior crime. Since Viśvarūpa is a brahmin, Indra's act incurs ***brahmahatyā*** — brahmin-murder — and the slayer becomes the guilty party.
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa**: recognises Vṛtra "as a ***bhakta*** (devotee) of Viṣṇu who was slain only due to his failure to live piously and without aggression."

So the figure travels from **cosmic obstruction** → **wronged avenger** → **flawed devotee of Viṣṇu**. The corpus notes that this is the *same diachronic movement* [[indra]] already documents from the other side: Indra's Vedic supremacy declines as Vaiṣṇavism rises, and one of the mechanisms of that decline is precisely **the moral rehabilitation of the things he killed**. Compare [[bali]], the asura king whose righteousness is the point of his story. A tradition that reframes the enemy as a devotee has thereby reframed the hero as a problem.

### The Indo-Iranian split

"The Indo-Iranian word is also found in Avestan as ***vərəθraγna*** (Vedic *vṛtraghná*), literally '(one who) slays obstacles'." The same compound is a **demon's name's derivative** in India and a **god's name** in Iran — see [[verethragna]], where the dispute above recurs in its sharpest form.

## Reasoning across signals

1. **Wikipedia, "Vritra"** (fetched): the captive waters and **99 fortresses**; **RV 1.32** with the broken-jaws detail; *Vṛtrahan* and "slayer of the first-born of dragons"; the *\*wr̥trás* < PIE *\*wer-* etymology with the "cover, obstacle" gloss; ***Ahi*** as a Vedic name for him; the **Mahābhārata** Tvaṣṭṛ/Triśiras-Viśvarūpa revenge account; the **Bhāgavata**'s bhakta reading; the Avestan cognate; and the Jörmungandr/Typhon/Slavic comparanda.
2. **Wikipedia, "Verethragna"** (fetched, separate article): the **Benveniste and Renou** position stated explicitly — the principal feature "wasn't slaying noxious creatures but to overcome obstacles" and to "unblock the flow of *apas*" — and **Paul Thieme**'s alternative, that "Proto-Aryan *Indra* has assumed the functions of a Proto-Aryan god *Vṛtraghna*." This is the signal that turns a Wikipedia gloss into a mapped scholarly dispute with named holders.
3. **Lexicon/morphology** (§4 signal 3, and here decisive rather than decorative): *vṛtra* is a **neuter abstract noun** from *\*wer-* "to cover, obstruct." Etymology is a clue and not a verdict — but when the disputed question is precisely *whether the referent was ever a creature*, the morphology is evidence about the thing at issue, not background. Recorded as the strongest point in favour of reading B, and explicitly not treated as settling it.
4. **Corpus cross-check** ([[indra]], [[bali]], [[vishnu]], [[rta]]): [[indra]] was written in an earlier batch from a different source-set and independently carries *Vṛtrahan*, the vajra forged by **Tvaṣṭṛ** (the same god who fathers Vṛtra in the epic account), the release of the waters at **RV 4.18**, and — centrally — the documented **decline of Indra's standing** as Vaiṣṇavism rises. The Purāṇic rehabilitation of Vṛtra recorded here is the same process observed from the enemy's side, which is corroboration of a pattern rather than of a fact.

**CONTESTED**, on what Vṛtra fundamentally is — with the disagreement running between named scholars and turning on morphology against imagery. Converged on the narrative, the epithet, the etymology, and the Purāṇic transformations. Confidence **medium**: no Ṛgveda translation was fetched for **RV 1.32** in this session (the corpus's Vedic verse citations elsewhere rest on earlier directly-fetched work), the Benveniste/Renou and Thieme positions are known **only through a Wikipedia summary of them**, and neither scholar's actual argument was read.
