## Gloss

*Saṃsāra* literally means "wandering" or "flowing together" — the beginningless cycle of birth, existence, death, and rebirth that characterises conditioned existence in all three major Indian traditions (Buddhist, Jain, Hindu). All three traditions agree on the basic structure: beings cycle through realms of existence driven by karma; the soteriological goal is to escape this cycling. Where they disagree fundamentally is on **what cycles** and **what karma is**.

## The central question: what wanders?

| tradition | what cycles | what karma is | liberation means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buddhist** | no permanent self — a stream of consciousness driven by karmic momentum; "continual repetitive cycle arising from grasping at a self" (Enc. Buddhism) | intentional mental action generating volitional formations (not a substance) | nirvāṇa: craving ceases; no more grasping; the stream stops |
| **Jain** | the jīva — a real, permanent, individual soul-substance | physical ultra-fine matter (karma-varganā) adhering to jīva due to passions | mokṣa: jīva freed of all karma; ascends to siddhāśilā; individual identity persists |
| **Hindu/Vedic** | the ātman — a real, permanent, unchanging self | a moral/cosmic force (not physical matter); ripens as life-circumstances | mokṣa (Advaita: ātman recognises identity with Brahman); or blissful union with personal God |

**The Buddhist paradox**: if there is no permanent self (anattā), what wanders? The canonical answer (SN 15.3, Assu Sutta): "From an inconstruable beginning comes transmigration. A beginning point is not evident, though beings hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving are transmigrating and wandering on." The wandering is beginningless; what wanders is a stream of conditioned processes, not a substance — like a flame that passes from candle to candle without a "fire-essence" that transfers.

## The realms

**Buddhist six realms**: gods (*deva*), jealous gods (*asura*), humans, animals, hungry ghosts (*preta*), hell beings (*naraka*). Function both cosmologically and psychologically (states of mind).

**Jain four gatis**: human beings (*manuṣya-gati*), heavenly beings (*deva-gati*), infernal beings (*nāraka-gati*), animals and plants (*tiryag-gati*). Notably: liberation (*mokṣa*) is possible **only** from human birth. Other realms can last very long but not lead to liberation directly.

**Hindu**: similarly structured realms in most Purāṇic accounts; Sāṃkhya / Vedānta focus on the three guṇa-states rather than realm-cosmology.

## Beginninglessness (and ending)

All three traditions agree samsāra is *anādi* (beginningless) — there is no "first moment" when the cycle began. They disagree on ending:
- Buddhist: can be definitively ended for an individual by attaining nirvāṇa; the cycle is not cosmically fated to continue
- Jain: can be definitively ended by individual jīva attaining mokṣa; siddhāśilā is permanent
- Hindu: individual ātman can be liberated; cosmic samsāra (the play of the universe) may repeat in vast cycles

## Why human birth is privileged (Jain and Buddhist)

Both Buddhist and Jain traditions consider human birth uniquely valuable: it is the only realm from which liberation is directly achievable. Gods live too long in comfort; hell beings suffer too intensely; animals lack the cognitive capacity. The Jain text explicitly: "liberation is impossible for any other kind of being." This is not chauvinism but soteriological logic: liberation requires a specific combination of reflective capacity and freedom from extremes.

## Reasoning across signals

Encyclopedia of Buddhism (encyclopediaofbuddhism.org) confirms the Buddhist position on what wanders (consciousness stream, not permanent self) and cites SN 15.3. Jainpedia (jainpedia.org) independently confirms the Jain four-gati system, jīva cycling, and siddhāśilā destination. New World Encyclopedia (newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Samsara) provides the three-tradition comparison including the Buddhist/Jain/Hindu divergence on soul-existence.
