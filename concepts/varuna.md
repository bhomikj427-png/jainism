## Gloss / Divergence map

**Varuṇa** is the Ṛgveda's **moral sovereign** — and the corpus's clearest case of a god losing an entire job description. He is portrayed "in four principal aspects: as a **universal monarch and sovereign of the sky**, the **upholder of [[rta|ṛta]]**, a deity associated with **water (*āpaḥ*)**, and a **wielder of *māyā***." By the epic period only the third survives.

### The Vedic Varuṇa: a god you can sin against

What distinguishes him from every other major Vedic deity is that his hymns are **juridical and penitential** rather than laudatory. He is "the enforcer of *ṛta*, the cosmic and moral order that governs both the natural world and human conduct," "a vigilant upholder of truth, who punishes transgressors while showing mercy to the penitent," and his ordinances are "unshakable, akin to a mountain." Despite the punishing role, "Varuṇa is often invoked for **forgiveness**, protection from evil, and relief from fear."

The enforcement apparatus is concrete:

- the ***pāśa***, the **noose**, with which he binds transgressors — the god's iconic weapon is a **restraint**, not a blade;
- **spies**. His omniscience runs through "celestial spies," and the Atharvaveda gives him "spies with a thousand eyes" who "descend to observe all human actions." He sits in a thousand-gated palace on a golden throne.

**RV 7.86–7.88**, attributed to the Vasiṣṭha family, are the crux: hymns of **supplication and confession of sin** to avert his wrath. And the ritual follows the theology — in the ***Varuṇapraghāsa*** rite, a seasonal sacrifice, "the sacrificer's wife was required to **confess her sin** (i.e. conjugal infidelity) before the officiating priest."

That is worth pausing on. The corpus's Vedic material is overwhelmingly about **[[yajna|sacrifice as exchange and maintenance]]** — the mechanism by which *ṛta* is kept running. Varuṇa's hymns are about something else: **guilt, surveillance, confession and pardon**, with a personal deity who *notices*. It is the nearest thing in the Ṛgveda to a conscience-religion, and the corpus records that it did not become the mainline. What Indian traditions later built their ethics on was **[[karma-vedic|karma]] — an impersonal mechanism with no one to confess to.** The displacement of Varuṇa is therefore not just a god's demotion; it is a road not taken.

### *Asura* — the word before it meant demon

Varuṇa is "mentioned as the **king of asuras**, who gained the status of a *deva*, serving as chief of the **Ādityas**." He and Mitra are "referred to as **Asuras** (e.g. **RV 5.63.3**), though also addressed as **Devas** (e.g. **RV 7.60.12**), reflecting fluid theological roles."

⚠️ The original sense of *asura* is "**possessor of *māyā***" or of vital force, "lacking later demonic connotations." A reader who brings the Purāṇic *asura* = demon back to these verses gets the Ṛgveda exactly backwards: **the highest moral god in the collection is called an asura, approvingly.** This is the single most common misreading of early Vedic vocabulary and the map flags it.

The same caution applies to ***māyā*** here. Varuṇa's *māyā* is **cosmic creative and measuring power** — "through *māyā*, Varuṇa becomes not only the enforcer of law but also a **cosmic architect**," embracing night and measuring the earth with the sun (Maṇḍala VIII). It is **not** [[maya-advaita|Advaita's world-concealing illusion]]. Same word, ~1500 years, and a reversal from *power to make* to *appearance to see through*.

### Mitra–Varuṇa

He is paired with Mitra in the dual compound *Mitrā-Varuṇā*: together they "preside over moral and cosmic law," oversee ***[[satya|satya]]*** (truth) and "mete out justice by punishing violations of *ṛta*, such as falsehood or moral transgression." They are "youthful monarchs clad in glistening garments, residing in a golden palace with a thousand pillars and gates," and the sages **Vasiṣṭha and Agastya** are born from their shared semen, deposited in a pitcher at the sight of Urvaśī.

### The decline

| era | what he is |
|---|---|
| **Ṛgveda** | supreme sky-sovereign; enforcer of *ṛta*; moral judge; wielder of *māyā*; lord of the *āpaḥ* |
| **later Vedic** | *Sindhu-pati*, "lord of the ocean"; the waters called *Virāj* become his consorts, "representing *prakṛti*" |
| **Mahābhārata / Purāṇas** | "lord of all waters, ruling over oceans, rivers, streams and lakes." "**He is no longer depicted as the supreme sky god or an omnipotent sovereign administering cosmic order and morality**, though his pairing with Mitra remains. The epic **forgets his earlier associations with *ṛta*** and ethical oversight" |
| final position | a ***Lokapāla*** / *Dikpāla* — guardian of the **western** quarter, "a symbolic alignment with the setting sun and perhaps with darkness and night" |

Set beside [[indra]], whose decline the corpus already documents, the two cases differ in kind and the difference is instructive. **Indra was displaced** — outranked by Viṣṇu, humbled by Kṛṣṇa at Govardhana, his enemies rehabilitated. **Varuṇa was not displaced; he was narrowed.** Nobody took his throne in a story. His moral-juridical function simply **stopped being the kind of thing Indian religion needed a god for**, and what was left — the waters — was enough to keep him in the pantheon at reduced size. Ending as guardian of the *west* is almost too apt.

### A famous comparison, now withdrawn

Georges **Dumézil** proposed that Varuṇa and the Greek **Ouranos** belong to "the earliest Indo-European cultural level," both names from a PIE root *\*ŭer-* with a sense of "**binding**" — which would have made the noose the etymology. The corpus records that this identification "**is now widely rejected** in favour of derivation from the root *\*wers-* 'to moisten, drip' (Sanskrit *vṛṣ* 'to rain, pour')."

This is worth keeping precisely because it is a **retraction**. The Varuṇa–Ouranos equation is still repeated in general literature; the etymology it rested on has been abandoned; and on the current derivation his name means something like *the rainer* — which fits the water-god he ended as rather than the moral sovereign he began as. **A comparison can be famous, elegant, and wrong**, and this project's §0 exists for exactly that failure mode.

### The Iranian comparison, which survives

By contrast the **Ahura Mazdā** parallel is doing real work. The Avestan name would be rendered in Vedic as *Asura Medhya*; the Vedas repeatedly call Varuṇa an *asura*; and "even as Ahura Mazdā rules by and upholds ***aša***, the cosmic moral order, in the Avesta, so too do Varuṇa and the Asuras uphold the analogous concept of ***ṛta*** in the Veda" — *aša* and *ṛta* being the same inherited word. So the pair *asura/ahura* + *ṛta/aša* is a **two-term correspondence in vocabulary and in function at once**, which is a far stronger comparative datum than a single name-match. It is still a **structural parallel and not an identity**: Ahura Mazdā is the sole uncreated creator of a dualist system, Varuṇa the chief of the Ādityas in a polytheistic one. The corpus draws it as a parallel and says so. See [[verethragna]] for the corpus's other Indo-Iranian pair — and note that these two cases pull in **opposite directions**: there, Iran keeps as a god what India keeps as an epithet; here, both keep the god, and it is *India* that lets the moral function lapse.
