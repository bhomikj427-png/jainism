## Gloss / Divergence map

**Yajña** (Sanskrit root *yaj* — "to worship, adore, honour, revere"): Vedic ritual sacrifice performed in or before a sacred fire (*agni*), involving the offering of oblations (clarified butter/*ghee*, grain, soma, etc.) with accompanying mantras. The tradition spans from Ṛgvedic fire-rituals through elaborate Śrauta rites to symbolic internalization in the Upaniṣads and Bhagavad Gītā. Its Mīmāṃsā formalisation makes yajña the paradigm case for the entire *karma-kāṇḍa* (ritual-action section) of Vedic injunctive authority.

**Three interpretive layers (historically stratified but co-existing):**

| layer | source | yajña = |
|---|---|---|
| Brāhmaṇa / Śrauta | Brāhmaṇa texts, Yajurveda | External fire-ritual; priest-performed; cosmic mechanism for maintaining order |
| Upaniṣadic internalization | Chāndogya Upaniṣad (~700 BCE) | Chaste student life; inner discipline; "What is called yajna is really the chaste life of the student" |
| Bhagavad Gītā expansion | BG 3.11; BG 4.24 | Any selfless action done without attachment as offering; * brahmaṇy ādhāya karmāṇi* |

**The cosmic-exchange theology:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.11 formulates the reciprocal structure explicitly: *devān bhāvayatānena te devā bhāvayantu vaḥ / parasparam bhāvayantaḥ śreyaḥ param avapsyatha* — "By this [yajña], nourish the gods; may the gods nourish you; mutually nourishing each other, you will attain the supreme good." Agni carries the human offering heavenward; the devas return rain, crops, and prosperity. This is a *structured exchange*, not merely prayer.

**Pancha Mahā-Yajña (Five Great Daily Sacrifices):** required of householders: (1) *Deva-yajña* (ghee to gods in fire); (2) *Pitr-yajña* (water libations to ancestors); (3) *Brahma-yajña* (Vedic recitation); (4) *Bhūta-yajña* (food offerings to living beings); (5) *Manuṣya-yajña* (alms to humans). These ritualise the cosmic-exchange theology at daily scale.

**Mīmāṃsā formalisation:** Pūrva Mīmāṃsā takes yajña as its central case for deriving binding Vedic injunctions (*vidhi*). The *Mīmāṃsā Sūtra* opens: *athāto dharma-jijñāsā* ("now, therefore, the inquiry into dharma") — where dharma means the injunctive force of Vedic ritual sentences. Yajña is *apauruṣeya* (not authored by any person) and *nitya* (eternal): its authority derives not from a divine creator's command but from the Veda's own self-validating injunctive force (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*). The sacrifice does not worship a god who mandated it; the sacrifice *is itself* the dharmic obligation. This is why Mīmāṃsā is often characterised as atheistic despite being centrally concerned with ritual.

**Connection to ṛta:** In the Ṛgvedic worldview, yajña maintains *ṛta* (cosmic order). The Puruṣa Sūkta portrays the cosmos itself as arising from a primordial sacrifice (the dismemberment of the cosmic Puruṣa). Ritual sacrifice thus re-enacts the originary cosmic event, keeping the universe in proper order.

**The ahiṃsā problem:** Vedic animal sacrifice (*paśu-yajña*) directly conflicts with Jain absolute ahiṃsā and Buddhist compassion ethics. The Brāhmaṇical response is that Vedic ritual killing is *dharmic*, not *hiṃsā*, because it fulfils an eternal injunction. Jain and Buddhist traditions reject this justification entirely.
