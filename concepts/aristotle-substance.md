## Gloss

Ousia ("substance," "being," "essence") — the central ontological category in Aristotle's metaphysics: what exists independently and is the primary bearer of properties. All other things (qualities, quantities, relations) exist *in* or *of* substances. Aristotle's account shifts between two texts: in the *Categories*, individual particular things (this horse, this man) are primary substances; in the *Metaphysics*, the **substantial form** (the organising principle that makes a thing the kind of thing it is) emerges as the deepest sense of ousia. Underlying both accounts is **hylomorphism**: every physical substance is a compound of matter (*hylē* — pure potential, no fixed identity) and form (*morphē/eidos* — the actualising principle that gives matter its structure and function).

## Two-level account across the texts

| text | primary substance | secondary substance | key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Categories* | individual particulars (Socrates, this horse) | species and genera to which particulars belong ("human," "animal") | substance is what cannot be predicated of anything else as a subject |
| *Metaphysics* | **form** (the substantial form) | matter (pure potential) | substance is "most properly" the form that makes a compound *what it is* — not the matter, not the whole compound |

The development between *Categories* and *Metaphysics* is contested: some scholars read it as a genuine revision; others as a deepening of the same insight. Both levels are in the tradition.

## Hylomorphism

Every physical thing is a **synolon** (compound) of:
- **Matter (hylē):** the indeterminate "stuff" — pure potentiality with no fixed identity by itself. Wood is the matter of a table; bronze is the matter of a statue.
- **Form (morphē/eidos):** the organising principle that gives matter its determinate structure, function, and identity. "Horse-ness" is the form of a horse; being a table-shape is the form of a table.

Neither is encountered without the other: formless matter and matter-less form are conceptual limits, not real things. This is Aristotle's explicit rejection of Platonic Forms as separately existing entities.

## Cross-tradition comparison: ousia, dravya, pudgala

| feature | Aristotle (ousia) | Jain (dravya) | Vaiśeṣika (dravya) |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary substances | individual particulars (this Socrates) | all six dravyas are real; no primary/secondary distinction | nine substance types including earth, water, fire, air, ether, time, space, soul, mind |
| matter/form structure | hylomorphic compound — form is the "primary" substance | no form/matter duality — dravya has guṇas (qualities) and paryāyas (modes) but is not a form-matter composite | substance has qualities (guṇa) that inhere via samavāya; no hylomorphic structure |
| universals | secondary substances — species/genera are real but not primary; universals are immanent in particulars | universals contested; Jain realism treats sāmānya (universal) as real but dependent on individuals | viśeṣa (ultimate particular) is a separate category; universals (sāmānya) also real |
| persistence through change | substance persists through accidental change; substantial change requires form-replacement | dravya persists as permanent substrate; modes (paryāya) change — similar to Aristotle's accidental change model | composite dravyas do NOT persist through substantial change (unlike Aristotle) |
| non-physical substances | soul (psyche) debated — form of a living body, not a separate substance | jīva, dharma, adharma, ākāśa, kāla — all non-physical dravyas with no parallel in Aristotle | soul and mind are substances; ether, time, space also substances — more overlap |
| key category not in others | substantial form (eidos as organising principle) | samavāya absent: Jain dravya-guṇa-paryāya unity is intrinsic, not via "inseparable inherence" | samavāya (inseparable inherence) is a distinct 6th category in Vaiśeṣika — Aristotle has no equivalent |

## Aristotle vs. Plato on substance

Aristotle's hylomorphism is explicitly designed against Platonic Forms: for Plato, the Form of Horse (universally existing independently) is more real than any particular horse; for Aristotle, the form of a horse exists only *in* particular horses and *is* their substance. This makes Aristotelian metaphysics immanentist (universals exist in particulars) rather than transcendentalist (universals exist independently). The Jain position is closest to Aristotle here: both reject separately existing Platonic Forms.
