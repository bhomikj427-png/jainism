## Gloss / Divergence map

**Sarvavarman** (Abhyankar: "called Sarvavarman or Śarva or Sarva") is the attributed author of the [[katantra|Kātantra]], the oldest surviving non-Pāṇinian Sanskrit grammar — and, outside that attribution, ⚠ **a figure this node could find in no source that is not the same story.**

| what is claimed | who holds it | what it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| "a reputed grammarian… believed to have been a **contemporary of the poet Guṇāḍhya in the court of Sātavāhana**"; wrote rules "mostly based on the Sūtras of Pāṇini," omitting the Vedic section "and all the intricacies and difficult elements… for **beginners and students of average intelligence**" | **Abhyankar**, s.v. | ⚠ the same court legend, stated as biography |
| he "**revised and redacted** the Kātantra Sūtras **already existing**" for his patron | Abhyankar (as reported), s.v. | ⚠ a **redactor**, not an author — which the Kātantra's own antiquity claim needs |
| he wrote only **adhyāyas I–III**; the fourth is [[vararuci|Vararuci]]'s | **Abhyankar**, s.vv. *Kātantra* and *Vararuci* | a composite text with two named hands |
| he received the grammar from **Kārttikeya** after austerities; the god named it | ***Kathāsaritsāgara*** I.7 (Tawney tr.) | ⚠ **revelation, in a tale-collection** |
| he won a **six-month wager** against Guṇāḍhya before King Sātavāhana | *Kathāsaritsāgara*; *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* | ⚠ the same story, in two retellings |

⭐ **The divergence that matters is not between the sources. It is between "author" and "redactor," and Abhyankar holds both.**

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐⭐⭐ The finding: the sole witness to Sarvavarman's date is a text whose own existence that same episode explains.** This is worth setting out as a chain, because each link is separately sourced and the shape only appears when they are put in order:

1. King **Sātavāhana** is humiliated by a Sanskrit error before his queens. **Śarvavarman** promises to make him proficient **in six months**; **Guṇāḍhya** wagers that if he does, Guṇāḍhya will "abandon his knowledge of **Sanskrit, Prākṛt and local language**." Sarvavarman's counter-stake is to wear Guṇāḍhya's sandals on his head for twelve years. *(Purāṇic Encyclopaedia, s.v. Guṇāḍhya; Kathāsaritsāgara I.6.)*
2. Sarvavarman performs austerities, and **Kārttikeya** ([[kartikeya]]) gives him the grammar. *(KSS I.7.)*
3. Guṇāḍhya loses, **abandons all three languages**, goes to the Vindhyas, learns **Paiśācī** from the *piśācas*, and composes the ***Bṛhatkathā*** in it. *(Purāṇic Encyclopaedia.)*
4. The ***Kathāsaritsāgara*** — Somadeva's 11th-century Kashmiri Sanskrit rendering — presents itself as an abridged translation **of that lost Paiśācī Bṛhatkathā**.
5. And the *Kathāsaritsāgara* is **the narrative source for 1–3**.

⭐⭐ **The composition of the Kātantra is, inside the story, the cause of the existence of the story's own source-text.** Every dating of Sarvavarman that runs through "contemporary of Guṇāḍhya at the Sātavāhana court" — which is all of them — runs through a closed loop. ⚠ **This does not make the date false**, and the node does not claim it does: there may well have been a Sātavāhana-era grammarian, and legends do accrete around real people. What it establishes is that the corpus has **one** witness where it looked like it had a tradition, and that the witness is self-referential. [[vararuci]] and [[panini]] already carry **Abhyankar's own verdict on that witness** — "only legendary and hence, it has very little historical value." ⭐ He nonetheless prints the Guṇāḍhya-and-Sātavāhana connection as biography in this entry, without the caveat he applies to it elsewhere. **That is the missing-cross-reference defect [[vararuci]] diagnosed in Batch 48, recurring in the same book on a neighbouring question** — and this time the corpus found it by walking the story rather than by comparing two headwords.

**⭐ The legend and the text agree about the text's design brief — and that is the interesting part, not evidence.** The Kātantra is short (1,412 sūtras, "the shortest extant grammar"), drops the Vedic section and the accents, reorders everything topic-wise, and is aimed at "beginners and students of average intelligence." The legend says it was produced to teach an adult non-specialist Sanskrit **in six months**. **Those are the same specification.** ⚠ Two readings, and the corpus asserts **neither**:
- the legend preserves a real commission, and the text's shape is the trace of it; or
- ⭐ the legend was **built backwards** out of the text's obvious character — anyone who opened the Kātantra could see it was a crash course, and the story supplies a reason.
The second is the cheaper hypothesis and the corpus's default suspicion, but nothing here decides it. **What is recorded is the fit, flagged as a fit.**

**⭐ The god names the grammar — read at the primary level.** In Tawney's translation, Skanda recites a sūtra "beginning with **the traditional doctrine of letters**" and declares: "**it shall be called Kātantra, and Kālāpaka, from the tail (*kalāpa*) of the peacock on which I ride.**" ⭐ Note what this does that Pāṇini's legend does not: the Śivasūtras' myth gives Pāṇini his **alphabet** from Śiva's drum; here the deity supplies the sūtras, the **title**, and the **etymology of the title**, in one speech. And before it, "**Sarasvatī in visible shape entered my mouth**" ([[sarasvati]]) — so the account stacks two deities. ⚠ [[katantra]] recorded the peacock-tail etymology at search-summary level; **it is upgraded here to a published translation of the primary text, and that node has been corrected accordingly.**

**⚠ Author or redactor? — and the corpus does not resolve it.** Abhyankar's *Sarvavarman* material, as reported, has him "revise and redact the Kātantra Sūtras **already existing** for the benefit of his patron"; his *Kātantra* entry has the work "based on Pāṇini but composed on the models of ancient grammarians." Those pull in different directions — the first makes Sarvavarman an editor of a pre-existing Kātantra, the second makes him a composer working from models. ⚠ **And Abhyankar separately calls it "rather risky" to say Kalāpa was an ancient system referred to by Pāṇini** — i.e. he refuses the strongest version of the pre-existence claim while leaving a weaker one standing. **The corpus records the tension and does not smooth it.** ⭐ Note this is the *Vararuci* pattern once more: an entry that carries material sitting badly with its own claim.

**⚠ The name.** *Sarvavarman* / *Śarvavarman* is not a stable spelling in the literature — and the variation is not cosmetic. **Śarva** is a name of **[[shiva|Śiva]]**; *sarva-* is "all." Abhyankar's own alternates are "Sarvavarman or Śarva or Sarva." ⚠ **The corpus does not build on this**: a *Śarva*-form would make the author "armoured-by-Śiva," which would sit very neatly with a legend in which Śiva delegates the teaching to his son — **and that neatness is exactly the reason to distrust the inference.** §3 lexicon-as-clue-not-verdict. Recorded as an observation about the spellings, with no claim attached.

**Independence (§4 signal 1).** ⚠ **Weak, and the weakness is the finding.** Four sources were consulted and **three of them are one story**: the *Kathāsaritsāgara* (Tawney's published translation, read), the *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* (Vettam Mani 1975 — an independent retelling of the same narrative, valuable precisely because it agrees on the wager's terms without depending on Tawney's wording), and Abhyankar, whose biographical sentence is the story compressed. **LinguIndic** supplies the attribution and a date and nothing about the man. ⚠ **wisdomlib's "Vyakarana glossary" is Abhyankar reprinted** and is not counted — verified at first hand for this very entry (see [[katantra]]). **No inscription, no independent chronicle, no contemporary reference was found.** **CONTESTED**, confidence **low** — the corpus's second `low`-confidence node, and the reason is not that sources disagree but that **there is effectively one source and it is a fairy tale.**
