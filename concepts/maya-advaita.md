## Gloss / Divergence map

In Advaita Vedānta, māyā is **the power by which the one Brahman appears as the manifest, apparently plural world**. It is the mechanism that accounts for the phenomenal world without granting it ultimate reality — and without saying the world is absolutely nothing.

**Two functions of māyā** (Śaṅkara-school formulation):
- *Āvaraṇa* (veiling): conceals the true nature of Brahman/ātman from the individual
- *Vikṣepa* (projection): projects the apparent plurality and multiplicity of the phenomenal world onto the non-dual Brahman

**Avidyā** (ignorance) is related but distinct: *māyā* operates on the cosmic level (the power of Brahman itself that generates the appearance of plurality); *avidyā* operates at the individual level (the individual soul's ignorance of its identity with Brahman). Both veil and project; both are dissolved when *jñāna* (liberating knowledge) arises.

**Adhyāsa (superimposition):** The world and the individual self are "superimposed" onto Brahman due to avidyā — like seeing a snake in a rope in dim light. When the light of knowledge falls, the snake-appearance (the world's apparent independence) is dissolved; but the rope (Brahman) remains, and it was always only that.

## Contested: Śaṅkara's actual position

**Śaṅkara's Adhyāsa definition (Brahmasūtra Bhāṣya, introductory section; Swami Gambhirananda tr., WisdomLib):**
> "The apparent presentation to consciousness, by way of remembrance, of something previously observed in some other thing."

This superimposition (*adhyāsa*) is, Śaṅkara states, what is meant by "ignorance (*avidyā*) — metaphorically, the effect being put for the cause." The world's status: "neither real nor unreal, nor both — they are unspeakable (*anirvachanīya*)." The world has "a relative existence; it is true for the time being, but disappears when true knowledge dawns."

Crucially, the WisdomLib presentation of Śaṅkara's Bhāṣya does **not** explicitly articulate the āvaraṇa/vikṣepa two-function scheme in this passage — those terms are more prominent in post-Śaṅkara commentators (Sureśvara, Padmapāda). This cautions against treating āvaraṇa/vikṣepa as Śaṅkara's own canonical formulation vs. the tradition's elaboration.

| reading | who holds it | what it commits to |
|---|---|---|
| **Realistic Advaita**: the world is real at the conventional (*vyāvahārika*) level; "illusion" means lack of *ultimate* (*pāramārthika*) reality, not total unreality | Śaṅkara's own Brahmasūtra Bhāṣya (per Gambhirananda tr.): world is *anirvachanīya* (unspeakable as real or unreal), not "not real" | The world is causally efficacious, practically real; only its status as ultimately independent is denied |
| **Illusory-world Advaita** (*vivartavāda*): the world is literally like a dream or mirage — not just ultimately unreal but experientially unreal-in-nature | Post-Śaṅkara tradition, particularly Prakāśātman (~13th c.) and later *vivartavāda* school | The world has no more reality than a dream; extreme idealism |

The second reading is the popular conception of "māyā = illusion"; the *anirvachanīya* formulation in the primary source better supports the first. This distinction matters enormously for cross-tradition comparison: conflating them produces mistaken equations between Advaita and Buddhist śūnyatā or Berkeley's idealism.

## Māyā vs. Jain ontology

Jainism explicitly holds all six dravyas as co-equal, fully real entities — neither hierarchically ranked nor derived from a single ground. Advaita Vedānta posits:
- *Pāramārthika satya* (ultimate reality): Brahman alone
- *Vyāvahārika satya* (conventional reality): the world of plurality, karma, persons, liberation
- *(Prātibhāsika satya)* (apparent/illusory, e.g. the dream or mirage)

Jainism collapses this hierarchy: dravya is dravya at only one level of reality. The world of pudgala and jīva is not a lower-level appearance of something more ultimately real — it simply IS real. This is precisely what made Śaṅkara engage critically with Jain (and Buddhist) realism in his Brahmasūtra commentary.
