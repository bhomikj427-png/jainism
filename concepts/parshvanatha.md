## Gloss

*Pārśvanātha* (Pārśva) is the **23rd tīrthaṅkara** of the present cosmic half-cycle, and the **earliest tīrthaṅkara whom modern scholarship accepts as a historical person**. Tradition and historians place him roughly in the **9th–8th century BCE — about 250 years before Mahāvīra** (the 24th). He is said to have been born in Vārāṇasī to King Aśvasena and Queen Vāmādevī, renounced at 30, and attained omniscience after meditation; his iconographic emblem is the **multi-hooded serpent** (recalling the legend of the snake-deity Dharaṇendra sheltering him in meditation). His doctrinal significance for the corpus is precise: he taught a **four-fold restraint (*cāturyāma*)** — ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, aparigraha — and **Mahāvīra later added the fifth vow, brahmacarya (celibacy)**, producing the five mahāvratas of Ch 08. Mahāvīra was thus a *reformer of an existing tradition*, not its founder: Mahāvīra's own parents are recorded as followers of Pārśva's order.

Confidence medium: the *dating* (9th–8th c. BCE) is traditional/scholarly reconstruction, not documentary; the biographical details are "interwoven with legend" (Britannica's own caveat).

## The four vows vs. the five — the doctrinal hinge

| | Pārśva's *cāturyāma* (4) | Mahāvīra's mahāvratas (5) |
|---|---|---|
| ahiṃsā (non-violence) | ✓ | ✓ |
| satya (truthfulness) | ✓ | ✓ |
| asteya (non-stealing) | ✓ | ✓ |
| aparigraha (non-possession) | ✓ | ✓ |
| **brahmacarya (celibacy)** | — (subsumed under aparigraha) | **✓ — separated out as its own vow** |

The Śvetāmbara reading: Pārśva folded celibacy *into* aparigraha (a woman being treated as a "possession"), and Mahāvīra, facing lax practice, made it an explicit fifth vow. The **Digambara reading disputes that this is a real change** — holding that the content was always five and Pārśva's "four" is only a count of emphasis. Either way, the *cāturyāma* → *pañca-mahāvrata* transition is the single most-cited piece of internal evidence that Jainism is a *lineage older than Mahāvīra* — which is exactly what the tradition claims (24 tīrthaṅkaras stretching back beyond history).

## Why Pārśva matters for historicity

The corpus treats the 24-tīrthaṅkara list as doctrine, not chronicle: the first 22 (through Ariṣṭanemi) leave no historical trace and belong to the cosmic narrative (utsarpini-avasarpini.md). Pārśva is the **boundary where doctrine meets history**: Buddhist texts reference the *cāujjāma* (four-fold restraint) of the Nirgrantha order Pārśva founded, giving an external, non-Jain attestation. So Pārśva is the earliest figure where "tīrthaṅkara" and "historical individual" can both be said with a straight face — and Mahāvīra (the next file) is the second and far better-attested one.
