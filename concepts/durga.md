## Gloss / Divergence map

**Durgā** (Sanskrit *durgā*, "the inaccessible / impassable one," from *dur-* "difficult" + *gam-* "to go through"; also "fort, stronghold") is the principal **warrior** form of the Hindu Goddess (**Devī / Śakti**) — goddess of protective power, strength, and motherhood, and in **Śāktism the supreme reality (Mahādevī = Brahman)**. She is iconographically a many-armed (8–18) goddess riding a **lion or tiger**, each hand bearing a weapon given by a god (trident, cakra, sword, bow).

**Mahiṣāsura-mardinī — the defining narrative.** The central episode of the *Devī Māhātmya* is Durgā's slaying of the buffalo-demon **Mahiṣāsura**, who held a boon of near-invulnerability to all males. The gods, unable to defeat him, **pooled their energies (*tejas*)** into a single blaze from which Durgā emerged — so she is not a deity helped by the gods but the **concentrated power of all the gods made one**. This is the theological point: the Goddess is cosmic power asserting order (*dharma*) against the forces of disorder, not merely one deity among many.

**Relation to Pārvatī and Kālī.** In the integrated Śaiva-Śākta system Durgā is the **fierce battle-form of the gentle Pārvatī** (the gentle goddess "becomes" Durgā to fight); and in the *Devī Māhātmya*'s Śumbha-Niśumbha episode, the still-fiercer **Kālī springs from Durgā's own brow (her wrath made incarnate)**. The three are aspects of one Devī, distinguished by mode (benign / warrior / terrible), not three separate goddesses.

**Status note.** Unlike consort-goddesses defined by their husbands, Durgā in the *Devī Māhātmya* is **independent and supreme** — she has no consort-subordination in the text; the lion-mounted warrior is self-sufficient. The "fierce aspect of Pārvatī" framing is the *Śaiva* integration; the *Śākta* reading makes Durgā/Devī the absolute and Śiva derivative.
