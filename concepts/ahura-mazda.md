## Gloss / Divergence map

**Ahura Mazdā** — "**The Creator of Existence, The one who establishes the Order of Existence (*aṣ̌a*)**" — is the supreme god of Zoroastrianism, and the corpus's **second Iranian node**, written because he is the other half of the correspondence Ch 33 §4.1 could only state from the Indian side.

### The name is the comparison

Both halves are transparently Indo-Iranian, and both matter:

| element | derivation | the Indian cognate |
|---|---|---|
| ***ahura*** | PIE *\*h₂ńsuros*, "lord" — "**cognate with Sanskrit *ásura***" | ***asura*** — which, at RV 5.63.3, is an **approving** title of [[varuna|Varuṇa]], the Ṛgveda's supreme moral god (see [[varuna]]) |
| ***mazdā*** | Proto-Iranian *\*mazdáH*, from PIE *\*mn̥sdʰh₁éh₂*, literally "**placing one's mind**" — hence intelligence, **wisdom** | Sanskrit *medhā* |

So the god's name means something very close to ***Wise Lord***, and the Vedic rendering of it would be *Asura Medhya*.

### *Aṣ̌a* and *ṛta* are the same inherited word

This is the part that turns a name-match into evidence:

> "Avestan *aṣ̌a* and its Vedic equivalent *ṛtá-* **both derive from Proto-Indo-Iranian *\*Hṛtá-* 'truth'**" — and further back from PIE *\*h₂r-to-*, "properly joined, right, true." (Old Persian *arta-*; Middle Iranian *ard-*.)

*Aṣ̌a* "cannot be rendered by a single English word," being "commonly summarized in accord with its contextual implications of '**truth**' and '**right**' (or 'righteousness'), '**order**' and '**right working**'" — which is very nearly what [[rta|ṛta]] does in the Ṛgveda. Its opposite is ***druj*** (deceit, falsehood), and the Gāthic formulation is striking: "**truth is existence (creation) inasmuch as falsehood is non-existence.**"

It is also **quantitatively central**: "in the 238 verses of these hymns, *Aṣ̌a* appears **157 times**." That is a hard textual datum of a kind this corpus rarely gets, and it settles that *aṣ̌a* is not a peripheral term.

> **The correspondence is therefore two-term and simultaneous: *ahura/asura* and *aṣ̌a/ṛta*, the title and the thing it upholds, matching across both languages.**

That is a far stronger comparative datum than any single name-match — and it is exactly the contrast Ch 33 §4 draws against Dumézil's **withdrawn** Varuṇa–Ouranos etymology. A one-term match can be coincidence or convergence; a **two-term match in which the second term is the first term's characteristic activity** is much harder to get by accident.

### The Varuṇa question

The sources go further than structural analogy, and the corpus records the claim with its hedge intact:

> "**Some scholars believe** that Ahura Mazdā originates from *\*vouruna-miθra*, or **Vedic Varuṇa (and Mitra)**."
> "Both Varuṇa (in the Vedic period) and Ahura Mazdā (in old Iranian religion) represented **the same Indo-Iranian concept of a supreme 'wise, all-knowing lord'**."

Note the two different strengths. The **second** claim — a shared *concept* of a wise all-knowing sovereign — is well supported by everything above, and the corpus asserts it as a **structural parallel**. The **first** — an etymological derivation from *\*vouruna-miθra* — is attributed to "some scholars," is not the article's own voice, and the corpus does **not** assert it. Ch 33 §4 has just recorded what happens when a famous etymological derivation for Varuṇa's name is accepted and then abandoned; the same caution applies here in the same session.

And a real disanalogy has to be kept attached: **Ahura Mazdā is the sole uncreated creator of a system organised around a cosmic moral conflict; Varuṇa is chief of the Ādityas in a polytheism, and then declines to a water-god and guardian of the west.** Same job description at one moment in prehistory; entirely different careers afterwards.

### The inversion

Two sourced facts, one from each side of the corpus:

- **Iran.** Zarathustra "proclaimed that some Iranian gods were ***daēva*s who deserved no worship**. These 'bad' deities were created by **Angra Mainyu**, the destructive spirit."
- **India.** *Asura* originally meant "possessor of *māyā*," with **no demonic connotation**, and became the standard word for demon by the Purāṇas ([[varuna]] §; Ch 33 §1).

Put together — and the corpus states this as **its own synthesis of two sourced facts, not as a claim it found in a source** — the two traditions **demote opposite halves of a shared inherited pantheon**. *Ahura* stays honorific in Iran and *daēva* becomes demonic; *deva* stays honorific in India and *asura* becomes demonic.

⚠️ **What the corpus does not do with this.** The elegant story — a schism, a religious quarrel, two communities anathematising each other's gods — is a **hypothesis about prehistory that none of the sources consulted here states**, and it is not asserted. What is established is the *pattern*, not its cause. Ch 33 §3.4 shows how much can hang on which reconstruction of Indo-Iranian prehistory one adopts, and how three competent scholars can read one dataset three ways.

### Dualist or monotheist? A dispute with a date

The standing theological problem is **Angra Mainyu**, "the destructive spirit," set against Ahura Mazdā. The corpus records the modern turn precisely because it is datable and interested:

| reading | what it says |
|---|---|
| **dualist** | Angra Mainyu is Ahura Mazdā's opposite number; two principles |
| **monotheist** — **Martin Haug, 1884** | "Angra Mainyu was now **not Ahura Mazda's binary opposite, but — like Spenta Mainyu — an emanation of Him**," which framed "Ahura Mazda as monotheistic," resolving the dualism |

Haug's is an **influential nineteenth-century reinterpretation**, and the sources present it as such rather than as a neutral finding. The corpus notes without adjudicating that a reading which converts a dualism into a monotheism, produced in 1884, arrives in a period when European scholarship treated monotheism as a mark of religious maturity — which is a reason to hold it as *one reading with a history*, exactly as Ch 29 holds Psellos and Ch 33 holds Dumézil. **The relevant contrast is structural: Spenta Mainyu and Angra Mainyu as two emanations gives you a very different system from Ahura Mazdā and Angra Mainyu as two principals.**

The ***Amesha Spentas*** — "the six primordial divine entities through which Ahura Mazdā created all existence," including ***Aṣ̌a Vahiṣta***, "**Best Truth**" — are what a monotheist reading needs: divine aspects rather than independent gods. **Aṣ̌a Vahiṣta is closely associated with fire, which "detects" truth and sinners** and is "the auxiliary of the truth." Compare [[agni]], where fire is *ṛtasya gopā*, "guardian of *ṛta*" — the **same functional pairing of fire with cosmic order**, in both languages, using the cognate word for the order. That is a third term in the correspondence, and the corpus records it as the strongest single item it has found.

[[verethragna]] supplies the corpus's other Indo-Iranian pair.
4. **Provenance weighting** (§4 signal 5): **both signals are Wikipedia articles**, so the independence is between *articles*, not between *authorities* — the same limitation recorded for [[verethragna]] and repeated here deliberately. **No Avestan text was read**, in translation or otherwise; the Gāthās are the obvious first fetch, and the 157/238 figure in particular is taken on an encyclopedia's word.

**CONTESTED** — on whether the system is fundamentally dualist or monotheist (a live dispute with a datable modern intervention), and on whether the Varuṇa relation is derivation or shared concept. **Converged** on the etymology of both name-elements, on *aṣ̌a*'s cognacy with *ṛta*, on the Amesha Spentas and on the *daēva* demotion. Confidence **medium**, capped by signal 4: two encyclopedia articles and no primary text.
