## Gloss / Divergence map

**Śrīharṣa** (12th c., northern India) wrote the ***Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya*** — "**The Sweets of Refutation**," rendered also "Sugar-candy Pieces of Refutations" — the work this corpus's own sources call the hardest thing in Advaita, and which `citsukha.md` has carried since Batch 44 as an **unwritten** base text with a table row reading "— (unwritten)." That row is now closed.

He also wrote the ***Naiṣadhīyacarita***, one of the five *mahākāvya*s of Sanskrit literature, in **1174**, under **Jayacandra** of the Gāhaḍavāla dynasty; his father Śrīhira had been a poet at the court of Jayacandra's father **Vijayacandra**. The philosopher and the poet are **the same man** on the standard modern view.

He is **contested**, and on the most basic question there is: **what he was arguing for.**

### Divergence — Advaitin, or something the categories do not fit?

| reading | who holds it (source) | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **An Advaitin.** The *Khaṇḍana* is a defence of non-dualism by indirect means | Stephen Phillips (1995, 1997); Chakravarthi Ram-Prasad (2002) | That the destruction has a positive purpose it does not state, and that the Advaita commentators who adopted him read him correctly |
| **Not a standard Advaitin.** He departs on two counts: he **rejects the provisional reality of the perceptible world**, and he holds **the object of awareness is non-distinct from awareness itself** | Phyllis Granoff (1978) | That the Advaita affiliation is a later tradition's annexation of a man who did not join it |
| **Granoff is half right.** Das (SEP) accepts the second departure and **rejects the first**: Śrīharṣa says a *Brahmavādin* "need not repudiate the reality of the plurality of things in the perceptible world altogether" | Nilanjan Das, *SEP* (2018) | That the case against his Advaita rests on one point, not two — and that the surviving point is metaphysical, not about the world's status |
| **He affiliated with nothing.** "Śrīharṣa didn't affiliate himself explicitly to any philosophical text tradition active in classical India" | Das (SEP), as a statement of fact about the text | That the question is being asked *of* him rather than *by* him — the honest baseline this file adopts |

**This file adopts the fourth as its baseline and reports the first three as the live dispute.** The point is not that scholars disagree; it is *why* they can. A book made entirely of refutations has **no thesis to read off**, so its author's position must be inferred from what he declines to defend — and that is a form of evidence that supports more than one reconstruction.

⚠ **One popular framing is not supported by SEP and is not adopted here.** Wikipedia reports that Śrīharṣa "differs fundamentally from Śaṃkara by rejecting reason entirely in favour of faith and the Upaniṣads." SEP describes something much narrower — pessimism about whether **definitions** can be given, arising from a technical failure of definitional method, and it notes he "claims that inquiry into the question of non-duality … is futile." *Pessimism about definability* is not *rejection of reason*, and the slide between them is the kind §0 exists to catch.

### What the book actually does

Śrīharṣa's target is the **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika method of definition**, and his principal interlocutor is **Udayana** (10th c.), whose *Lakṣaṇāvalī* ("Garland of Definitions") supplies much of what is refuted. **Vātsyāyana** (5th c.) is cited for the definition of the Nyāya system itself.

His instrument is the ***khaṇḍana-yukti***, the **refutation-argument**, aimed at showing "that the relevant definitions are inadequate." He holds a definition to three requirements and shows the opponent's candidates failing one or another:

1. **extensional adequacy** — it must state conditions both necessary and sufficient;
2. **non-circularity** — it must not presuppose what it defines;
3. **uniformity** — it must identify a *single* property holding across all instances.

Applied to *pramāṇa* itself, the result is that "there may not after all be any satisfactory definition of knowledge." Applied to **difference**, it yields his sharpest claim: **no awareness, perceptual or otherwise, can present one object as distinct from another without also presenting them as non-distinct.** That is the one place where the destructive method visibly points somewhere — which is exactly why the affiliation question stays open.

⚠ **A term this file does *not* use.** *Vitaṇḍā* — "refutation without a counter-thesis," the Nyāya name for exactly this manoeuvre — is the obvious label, and it was the term this node was expected to carry. **SEP does not use it, and states that the text does not use it explicitly.** It is therefore recorded as a *description* that fits and an *attribution* that is not made. Calling Śrīharṣa a *vaitaṇḍika* would put a technical, and pejorative, classification in his mouth on no authority.

### The reception, which is the best evidence about him

His readers split exactly along the line the modern dispute runs on, and did so immediately:

- **Advaitins adopted him.** [[citsukha|Citsukha]] wrote the *Khaṇḍanabhāvadīpikā* on it, and "developed arguments from this work further" in his *Tattvapradīpikā*. **Ānandapūrṇa** wrote the *Khaṇḍanaphakkikāvibhajana*.
- **Naiyāyikas answered him at length** — and, per SEP, largely *after* **Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya's reconstruction of Nyāya**: from Mithilā, Vardhamāna, Śaṃkara Miśra, **Vācaspati Miśra II**, Pragalbha and Gokulanātha Upādhyāya; from Bengal's Navya-Nyāya, Raghudeva and Raghunātha Sārvabhauma Bhaṭṭācārya. Named commentaries include Śaṅkaramiśra's *Śāṅkarī*, Raghunātha Bhaṭṭācārya's *Khaṇḍanabhūṣāmaṇi*, Pragalbhamiśra's *Khaṇḍanadarpaṇa* and Anubhūtisvarūpācārya's *Śiṣyahitaiṣiṇī*.

**A single book drawing a sustained refutatory literature from the school it attacked, and a devotional-commentarial literature from the school that claimed it, is the strongest available evidence that both readings had something to work with.** ⚠ SEP places the Nyāya commentaries after Gaṅgeśa; it does **not** say Gaṅgeśa reconstructed Nyāya *in response to* Śrīharṣa. The causal story is tempting and is **not asserted here**.

⚠ **Name collision, logged.** The **Vācaspati Miśra II** who answered Śrīharṣa from Mithilā is **not** [[vacaspati-mishra|Vācaspati Miśra]], the 9th–10th-c. *sarva-tantra-sva-tantra* author of the *Bhāmatī* who has a node in this corpus. Two Maithil Naiyāyika-Vedāntins of one name, some five centuries apart. A caution has been added to that file.

### Udayana, his father, and a synchronism found after this file was written

⚠ **Added later in the same batch, from `udayana.md`.** Writing Udayana turned up a tradition that changes how the *Khaṇḍana*'s choice of target should be read. The *Naiṣadhacarita* commentator **Cāṇḍupaṇḍita** attests that **Śrīharṣa's father was defeated in debate by Udayana** — and D. C. Bhattacharyya uses that testimony as one of four arguments for **redating Udayana** a century later than the usual Śaka 906 / 984 CE, placing the debate at 1070–80.

The convergence is unplanned and therefore worth something: this file recorded **Śrīhira, a poet at Vijayacandra's court**, from Wikipedia's article on Śrīharṣa, which has no interest in Nyāya chronology; Bhadra's study reaches the same father and the same court from a 13th–14th-c. commentator quoted inside a dating argument. On this tradition, Śrīharṣa's choice of **Udayana's *Lakṣaṇāvalī*** as the thing to demolish is not only doctrinal — **it is a son answering his father's defeat**, and the observation above that the book is "shaped by the target it attacks" acquires a second, biographical sense.

Held at arm's length, twice. Cāṇḍupaṇḍita's report is a debate-anecdote of the same genre this corpus discounts in `shankara.md` and `appayya-dikshita.md`; its usable content is that a later commentator **believed the two men were contemporaries**, not that the debate happened. And it leaves an unreconciled **reign-date problem**: Śrīharṣa is placed "under Govindacandra and Vijayacandra (1104–54)" in the dating argument, while the *Naiṣadhacarita* is dated **1174 under Jayacandra** above. No source consulted reconciles the two. **Open, and not smoothed.**

### An independent confirmation the corpus can now claim

`citsukha.md` records **Dasgupta** (1932) saying Citsukha "had all Śrīharṣa's powers of acute dialectical thought" but went further by *defining* rather than only refuting. SEP (2018), written from the other end and about the other man, says Citsukha "developed arguments from this work further" in the *Tattvapradīpikā*. **These are genuinely independent** — a 1932 history and a 2018 encyclopedia entry, different authors, different subject-focus — and they agree on both the dependence and its direction. That is a real strengthening of a Batch 44 finding.

## Reasoning across signals

**Signal 1 — Nilanjan Das, "Śrīharṣa," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 1 Feb 2018), main entry and Notes.** The strongest possible provenance for this question: peer-reviewed, named author, and it treats the affiliation dispute *as* a dispute, naming Granoff 1978 against Phillips 1995/1997 and Ram-Prasad 2002 and then adjudicating one half of it. Supplies the three definitional criteria, the Udayana/*Lakṣaṇāvalī* target, the *khaṇḍana-yukti* term, the difference-argument, the *Brahmavādin* passage, the full commentator lists on both sides, and the explicit statement that the text does not use "vitaṇḍa." **Signal 2 — Wikipedia, "Shriharsha"**: the Gāhaḍavāla court context, Śrīhira, Vijayacandra/Jayacandra, the 1174 date of the *Naiṣadha*, the title's translations, and the acknowledgement that the Advaita reading is "controversial among modern scholars." **Used with one of its claims explicitly rejected** (see the "rejecting reason entirely" flag above). **Signal 3 — corpus-internal, and independent in date and authorship**: `citsukha.md`'s Dasgupta material, fetched two batches ago, on the destructive/definitional contrast and the Nāgārjuna question.

**Independence.** Signal 1 is decisive and stands alone in weight; signals 2 and 3 are used where they add facts SEP does not carry (the court patronage; the Dasgupta comparison). The critical check is signal 3 against signal 1 on Citsukha's dependence — 1932 vs 2018, agreeing — which is the only place in this node where two genuinely independent sources confirm each other rather than one merely reporting.

**Why contested.** Four positions on his affiliation, three of them held by named scholars in print, and the primary evidence — a book of pure refutation — **underdetermines** the answer by construction. **Why medium, not high.** SEP alone is high-grade, but the *Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya* itself was **not read**; Granoff 1978, Phillips 1995/1997 and Ram-Prasad 2002 are known only through Das's reports of them; and no commentary was consulted. **Not low**: nothing load-bearing rests on a weak source.
