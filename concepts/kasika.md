## Gloss / Divergence map

The **Kāśikā** — more fully the ***Kāśikāvṛtti***, "the Benares gloss" — is the running commentary on the [[astadhyayi|Aṣṭādhyāyī]] written by the **joint authors Jayāditya and Vāmana**, conventionally in the **7th century CE**. Abhyankar's description is the useful one: it is "an excellent help to **beginners** to understand the sense of the pithy Sūtras of Pāṇini," and it has held "a very prominent position among students and scholars of Pāṇini's grammar **in spite of**" the later re-orderings — the *Bhāṣāvṛtti*, the *Prakriyā Kaumudī*, the *Siddhānta Kaumudī*.

⭐ **Its structural significance for this corpus is a number.** [[mahabhashya|The Mahābhāṣya]] discusses **1,228 of 3,981 sūtras — 31%**. The Kāśikā glosses **all of them, in Pāṇini's own order**. It is described as "**the only extant full-length commentary on the sūtras of the Aṣṭādhyāyī**." So the text through which the tradition's most authoritative commentary reaches a reader covers under a third of the root text, and **the text that covers all of it is a beginners' gloss six centuries younger.**

## Reasoning across signals

**⭐ The finding: Batch 47's source-weighting rule is now predictive, not just cautionary — and this batch tested it twice on adjacent entries of the same book.** The rule, as Batch 47 stated it: *"read at first hand" and "specialist" are strong marks for what a discipline knows internally — vocabulary, manuscript contents, which attributions the tradition itself doubts — and weak for anything depending on outside scholarship that has moved, chronology above all.* Applied to Abhyankar:

- **[[kaiyata|Kaiyaṭa's date]]** — a chronology question. Predicted posture: **over-confident**. Observed: a flat "lived in the 11th century" with no argument, where careful modern usage gives only a *pre-thirteenth-century* bound. ✅
- **The Kāśikā's authorship** — an attribution the tradition itself doubts. Predicted posture: **reliable, and appropriately hedged**. Observed: "**nothing definitely can be said**," with the popular 5/3 split explicitly downgraded to what "some scholars believe." ✅

**Two opposite epistemic postures, in one dictionary, both correctly predicted by the rule.** ⚠ And the practical consequence runs *against* the intuitive one: on this question the specialist is the **more** sceptical witness and the general sources are the confident ones. Abhyankar is not uniformly loose or uniformly tight — **his reliability is a function of the question type**, and the rule now names which type is which.

**⭐⭐ The witness problem — an inconsistency inside the corpus, surfaced by putting two nodes next to each other.** Both Abhyankar and Wikipedia give the Kāśikā a 7th-century date and **neither offers any evidence for it**. The evidence the Indological literature actually cites is the Chinese pilgrim **Yijing (I-tsing, 635–713)**, who reports that **Bhartṛhari was a contemporary of Jayāditya**, and that Jayāditya died some thirty years before his own visit (Yijing was in India 673–695), i.e. c. 643–650.

⚠ **That is the same witness, and on the reported accounts the same passage, that [[bhartrhari]] records the modern consensus as having *discredited*.** That node states it plainly: "the current 5th–6th c. consensus rests largely on **discrediting the only external witness, not on replacing him**" — researchers finding "some of the details given by Yi-Jing to be erroneous, **specifically the time period** that he was alive," and Yijing further mistaking Bhartṛhari for a Buddhist.

**So one sentence of Yijing is rejected where it dates Bhartṛhari and relied on where it dates Jayāditya.** The asymmetry may well be defensible — a 7th-century Jayāditya collides with nothing, whereas a 7th-century Bhartṛhari collides with a consensus built on other grounds — but **the witness's reliability does not vary by which half of his sentence one is reading.** If Yijing was wrong about the period, the Kāśikā's only cited anchor inherits the doubt; if he was right, [[bhartrhari]]'s dating problem reopens. ⚠ **Stated as a tension the corpus can see, not as a result.** The corpus has *not* read the primary scholarship on the Kāśikā's date and therefore **cannot assert that Yijing is its sole ground** — only that no source consulted here offered another. **Carried as a follow-up.**

**The Mahābhāṣya dependency, and what it cannot cover.** Abhyankar: the Kāśikā's "wording is **based almost on the Mahābhāṣya** which it has followed, **avoiding, of course, the scholarly disquisitions** occurring here and there." ⚠ This raises a question none of the sources consulted answers, and the corpus should not answer it by inference: the Mahābhāṣya is silent on **69% of the sūtras**, so for most of the Aṣṭādhyāyī there was no Mahābhāṣya wording to follow. **What the Kāśikā's gloss rests on across that majority is unestablished here.** Recorded as an open question rather than filled in.

**Buddhist contact, recorded and not overstated.** The Kāśikā is reported as "marked by its profound awareness of grammatical works that preceded it, like the Mahābhāṣya and **Cāndra-Vyākaraṇa**" — the Buddhist grammarian Candrācārya's system — and its principal commentary, the ***Nyāsa*** (*Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā*), is by **Jinendrabuddhi**, "a **Buddhist** grammarian of the eighth century." ⭐ **Batch 49 gave him a node — see [[jinendrabuddhi]] — and the result was larger than this file expected**: on the scholarly majority he is also the author of the *Viśālamalavatī*, the ṭīkā from which Dignāga's lost Sanskrit is reconstructed. The corpus was already carrying that half in `pramana-samuccaya.md`. ⚠ Batch 47 already flagged that the available accounts of Candrācārya **contradict one another** (`vakyapadiya` follow-up 7); nothing here resolves that, and no borrowing claim is made. The second great commentary is **Haradatta's *Padamañjarī*** — the same Haradatta whose Śaivite, non-hierarchical reading of the three sages [[kaiyata]] contrasts with Kaiyaṭa's.

**⚠ Name-collision, flagged by Abhyankar himself before the corpus could make it.** "Kāśikā" is *also* used as a title for unrelated commentaries "as possibly they were written at Kāśī" — e.g. **Hari Dīkṣita's Kāśikā on the *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇasāra***, and **Vaidyanātha Pāyaguṇḍe's Kāśikā on the *Paribhāṣenduśekhara***. This node is the **Kāśikāvṛtti on Pāṇini** and nothing else. That makes **seven** flagged name-collisions across Batches 47–48; the earlier six are listed in the Batch 47 run-log. ⭐ Note that a **place-name title generates collisions the way a personal name does** — the corpus's earlier six all turned on people.
