## Gloss

*Dukkha* is **suffering, unsatisfactoriness, or stress** — the First Noble Truth of Buddhism. The Buddha's first discourse (SN 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, the "Setting in Motion of the Wheel of Dhamma" at Sarnath) defines it:

> *jātipi dukkhā, jarāpi dukkhā, byādhipi dukkho, maraṇampi dukkhaṁ* — "birth is suffering, aging is suffering, sickness is suffering, death is suffering; association with the unpleasant is suffering, separation from the pleasant is suffering, not getting what one desires is suffering."

The summary formulation: *pañcupādānakkhandhā dukkhā* — "the five aggregates subject to clinging are suffering." This ties dukkha to the khandha-structure: suffering is not an external affliction but the condition of clinging-to-aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness).

## Three types (SN 45.165, Dukkhatā Sutta)

| type | Pali | description |
|---|---|---|
| Suffering of pain | dukkha-dukkha | obvious physical and mental pain: birth, aging, illness, death |
| Suffering of change | viparināma-dukkha | pleasant experience is unreliable — what changes cannot be a stable ground for happiness |
| Suffering of conditioned existence | saṅkhāra-dukkha | the deepest: conditioned existence itself is unsatisfactory — pervasive dissatisfaction because everything is impermanent and without permanent selfhood |

**Saṅkhāra-dukkha is the most philosophically significant**: it claims not merely that painful things hurt but that *conditioned existence as such* — regardless of whether it feels pleasant at any moment — is pervasively unsatisfactory. This is Buddhism's most radical claim and its direct consequence of anattā (no permanent self) and anicca (impermanence).

## The Three-Noble-Truth dependencies

Dukkha (the First Noble Truth) does not stand alone; it forms a logical chain:
1. **Dukkha** — the condition: conditioned existence is suffering
2. **Samudaya** — the cause: craving (*tṛṣṇā* / *taṇhā*) for permanence in impermanent things
3. **Nirodha** — the cessation: nirvāṇa (the unconditioned, where craving ceases)
4. **Magga** — the path: the Noble Eightfold Path

Dukkha is therefore not the full picture of Buddhist soteriology — it is the diagnosis, whose treatment is the rest of the Four Noble Truths.

## Cross-tradition comparison: suffering and liberation

| tradition | equivalent of dukkha | cause | liberation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buddhist | dukkha (conditioned existence) | craving (*taṇhā*); clinging to aggregates | nirvāṇa — cessation of craving; no-self vindicated |
| Jain | bandha (karmic bondage) + samsāra cycling | kaṣāya (passions) generating karma-influx (āsrava) | mokṣa — jīva freed at universe-summit; individual soul persists |
| Hindu/Sāṃkhya | tri-fold suffering (ādhyātmika/ādhibhautika/ādhidaivika) | puruṣa-prakṛti confusion | discrimination (*viveka*) — puruṣa recognises its separation from prakṛti |
| Advaita Vedānta | bondage in māyā (ignorance) | avidyā (cosmic ignorance, mistaking world for real) | mokṣa — ātman recognises identity with Brahman |

**Critical distinction from Jain bandha**: Buddhist dukkha is the condition of a *momentary consciousness-stream with no permanent self*; there is nothing that suffers in the sense of a substance. Jain bandha is the condition of a *real permanent jīva-substance* constrained by physical karma. The same phenomenology (suffering, cycling, the need for liberation) is grounded in opposite metaphysics: Buddhist no-self vs. Jain real-self.

**Critical distinction from Sāṃkhya**: Sāṃkhya's three miseries are classified by their *source* (internal/external/divine); Buddhist three types of dukkha are classified by their *depth and character* (obvious pain/change/conditioned existence). Different taxonomic axes.
