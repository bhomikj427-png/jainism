## Gloss / Divergence map

In Nyāya philosophy, anumāna is **inference** — the second of the four pramāṇas, following direct perception (pratyakṣa). It is the process of arriving at a conclusion (*nigamana*) about an unperceived fact through a perceived mark (*liṅga*) whose invariable concomitance (*vyāpti*) with the target property has been established by prior observation.

**The five-member syllogism (pañcāvayava) — public inference (*parārthānumāna*)**

The Nyāyasūtra prescribes five constituent steps:

| member | Sanskrit | function | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | pratijñā | the claim to be established | "There is fire on the hill" |
| Reason | hetu | the observed mark that grounds the claim | "because there is smoke" |
| Example | udāharaṇa / dṛṣṭānta | the vyāpti-carrying exemplar case | "wherever there is smoke, there is fire, as in a kitchen" |
| Application | upanaya | the mark is subsumptively applied to the subject | "the hill is indeed smoke-bearing" |
| Conclusion | nigamana | re-states the thesis as established | "therefore the hill is fire-bearing" |

The example (*udāharaṇa*) is what makes Nyāya's five-member form explicitly empirical: the vyāpti is grounded by ostensible positive (anvaya) and negative (vyatireka) instances, not by definition alone. This contrasts with Aristotle's three-term syllogism, which encodes the universal as a formal major premise rather than an empirical co-occurrence.

**Vyāpti (invariable concomitance) as the logical heart**

The whole inferential structure rests on vyāpti — the invariable correlation between the mark (*liṅga/hetu*) and the target (*sādhya*). Without verified vyāpti, the inference is invalid regardless of form. Nyāya grounds vyāpti in observed agreement/non-agreement across many cases — a semi-inductive base. The Naiyāyika holds vyāpti as holding between universals (the universal *smoke-ness* always accompanies the universal *fire-ness*), not merely between token instances.

**Private vs. public inference**

Nyāya distinguishes *svārthānumāna* (inference for oneself — the personal reasoning process) from *parārthānumāna* (inference for another — the formal five-member proof for debate). The five-member form is for the latter context: designed for persuasion in public disputation (vāda), ensuring each step is explicitly verifiable.

**The four-locus / hetu-ābhāsa framework**

Valid inferential marks satisfy three conditions (the *trairūpya*, though Nyāya states it differently from Dignāga):
1. The mark is present in the subject (*pakṣa-dharmatā*)
2. The mark is present in cases similar to the subject (*sapakṣa-sattva*)
3. The mark is absent in dissimilar cases (*vipakṣa-vyāvṛtti*)

*Hetvābhāsa* (fallacious reasons) — including the inconclusive (*anaikāntika*), contradictory (*viruddha*), and counter-balanced (*satpratipakṣa*) — are catalogued in the Nyāyasūtra.
