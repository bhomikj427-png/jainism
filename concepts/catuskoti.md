## Gloss / Divergence map

The catuṣkoṭi ("four corners" / tetralemma) is the **fourfold logical schema** that enumerates all exhaustive and mutually exclusive possibilities for any proposition:

| corner | Sanskrit | predication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | asti | It **is** (affirmation) |
| 2 | nāsti | It **is not** (negation) |
| 3 | asti ca nāsti ca | It **both is and is not** (conjunction) |
| 4 | na asti na nāsti | It **neither is nor is not** (negation of both) |

**Two distinct uses in Buddhist tradition:**

**Use 1 — The Buddha's silence (*avyākata*):** In the Pali Canon, the Buddha encounters 14 (or 10) "unanswerable questions" (*avyākata-vatthu*) — e.g., "Is the world eternal? Is it not? Is it both? Is it neither?" — and remains silent on all four corners of each. The silence signals that NONE of the four conceptual categories correctly applies. The question is malformed, not that the answer is merely unknown. The catuṣkoṭi here structures the space of possible answers in order to show the space itself is the problem.

**Use 2 — Nāgārjuna's prasaṅga:** In the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (~2nd c. CE), Nāgārjuna applies the catuṣkoṭi to any essentialist claim and, using *prasaṅga* (reductio ad absurdum), shows that ALL four corners lead to contradiction. The catuṣkoṭi is a tool of dialectical deconstruction: if no corner holds, the concept under examination is shown to be empty of inherent existence (*svabhāva* = śūnyatā). This extends Use 1 from metaphysical questions to ALL philosophical assertions.

**Modern formalisation — Graham Priest (2018):** Priest argues that the catuṣkoṭi is best understood through *paraconsistent logic* (a logic where contradictions can be true without explosion), giving it a formal semantics in which corner 3 (both asti and nāsti) is genuinely possible. Fabien Schang (2013) compares catuṣkoṭi and saptabhaṅgī formally, reconstructing both within a common framework without asserting equivalence.

## Catuṣkoṭi vs. Saptabhaṅgī — structural comparison

| dimension | Buddhist catuṣkoṭi | Jain saptabhaṅgī |
|---|---|---|
| Number of forms | 4 | 7 |
| Purpose | enumerate all logical possibilities; then negate all (Nāgārjuna) | make syāt-qualified assertions from different standpoints |
| Key operation | exhaustive enumeration → dialectical rejection | standpoint-parameterisation (*syāt*) → conditional assertion |
| Third corner | "both is and is not" (simultaneous; paraconsistent) | "avaktavya" (simultaneously asti + nāsti cannot be expressed in one breath) |
| Relation to truth | seeks to escape all four corners (ultimate: śūnyatā) | each of 7 assertions is conditionally true from its standpoint |
| Background logic | classical + paraconsistent (Priest) OR Madhyamaka two-truths framework | standpoint-relative predication; not a truth-value algebra |

Despite structural surface similarity, the catuṣkoṭi and saptabhaṅgī serve different philosophical purposes: catuṣkoṭi deconstructs all views to reveal śūnyatā; saptabhaṅgī organises partial truths to express anekāntavāda. They are analogous tools, not equivalent systems.
