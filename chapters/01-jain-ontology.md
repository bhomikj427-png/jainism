## 1. What does it mean to exist? — *sat* {#sat}

### 1.1 The claim {#sat-claim}

In ordinary English, "exists" is a yes/no predicate: a thing either exists or it doesn't. Jain philosophy rejects this. The Tattvārtha Sūtra (a systematic philosophical treatise composed by Umāsvāti, ca. 2nd–5th century CE) gives a precise definition in verse 5.30:

> **utpādavyayadhrauvyayuktaṃ sat**
> "Existence (*sat*) is with origination (*utpāda*), destruction (*vyaya*), and permanence (*dhrauvya*)."
> — TS 5.30, Vijay K. Jain (tr.), Motilal Banarsidass 2018

Five Sanskrit words. The whole Jain theory of existence is inside them. To exist is to instantiate **all three** simultaneously:

| component | Sanskrit | what it names |
|---|---|---|
| Origination | utpāda | a new state being attained — something coming to be |
| Destruction | vyaya | a former state being lost — something ceasing to be |
| Permanence | dhrauvya | the underlying nature persisting across all changes |

The 5th–6th-century commentator Pūjyapāda (*Sarvārthasiddhi*, via Vijay K. Jain 2018) fixes this with an analogy: **the clay pot.**

A lump of clay is shaped into a pitcher. The lump-form vanishes (*vyaya*). The pitcher-form appears (*utpāda*). The clay itself continues unchanged (*dhrauvya*). Now smash the pitcher. Again: the pitcher-form vanishes, shards appear, clay persists. This happens at every moment, everywhere, in everything that genuinely exists.

**Why three, not one?** Because Jain philosophers are responding to two rivals who each got one piece right:
- The Buddhists said: things change; there is no permanent substrate. (They got utpāda/vyaya; they denied dhrauvya.)
- The Advaita Vedāntins said: only permanent being (Brahman) is real; change is appearance. (They got dhrauvya; they denied real utpāda/vyaya.)

The Jain move is to insist that **both are real, operating at different levels of the same thing** — and that denying either is philosophically incomplete.

### 1.2 Three-way comparison {#sat-comparison}

| tradition | position | what they deny |
|---|---|---|
| **Jain** | sat = utpāda + vyaya + dhrauvya simultaneously; change and persistence both real | neither |
| **Buddhist** (*anicca*, impermanence) | all conditioned things arise and cease; no enduring substrate | **dhrauvya** — no permanent substance behind the process |
| **Advaita Vedānta** | Brahman is pure being (*sat*), eternal, changeless; apparent change is *māyā* (not ultimately real) | **utpāda/vyaya** — change is not ultimately real |

The deliberate philosophical middle: **Jainism is neither pure permanence nor pure impermanence.** Change is real (pace Advaita). A persisting substrate is real (pace Buddhism). The work of the rest of this chapter is to cash out what the substrate is (dravya) and what the change is (paryāya).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *sat* ≠ Advaita Brahman**
>
> Both traditions use the Sanskrit word *sat*. Advaita's *Brahman* is *sat-cit-ānanda* (being-consciousness-bliss) and is explicitly changeless — no origination, no destruction. Jain *sat* **requires** all three components. A Brahman that has only dhrauvya (no real utpāda/vyaya) fails the Jain definition — it would not count as genuinely existing in the Jain sense. The surface similarity (same word, positive ontological meaning) is precisely what drives the conflation. The difference is structural and deliberate: Jain *sat* is a critique of Advaita's account as half a theory.

## 2. Substance: the six things that exist — *dravya* {#dravya}

### 2.1 The definition {#dravya-def}

*Sat* tells us what existence requires. *Dravya* (substance) names the kind of thing that exists in this way. The Tattvārtha Sūtra gives two definitions that work together:

**Definition 1 — the process definition** (TS 5.30): The same verse that defines *sat* also defines dravya — a dravya is something that undergoes modal change (*paryāya* — its modes arise and perish) while its underlying nature persists. The triadic structure of sat *is* the triadic structure of a dravya.

**Definition 2 — the property/mode definition** (TS 5.38, directly fetched):

> **guṇaparyāyavat dravyam**
> "That which has qualities (*guṇa*) and modes (*paryāya*) is a substance (*dravya*)."
> — TS 5.38, Vijay K. Jain (tr.), Motilal Banarsidass 2018

- **Guṇa** (quality): permanent, inalienable attributes — what the substance *always is*
- **Paryāya** (mode): transient modifications — what the substance *is now, briefly*

These two definitions say the same thing from different angles. The process definition describes the temporal rhythm (always changing in modes, always persisting in nature). The property/mode definition describes the structural composition (always has guṇas, always has paryāyas).

### 2.2 The six dravyas {#six-dravyas}

The Jain universe contains exactly **six substances** and no others. This is a closed ontology — everything that exists is an instance of one of these six:

| # | dravya | English | nature | sentient? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | jīva | soul | characterised by consciousness (*cetanā*) | yes |
| 2 | pudgala | matter | has touch, taste, smell, colour; sole locus of atoms | no |
| 3 | dharma-dravya | medium of motion | enables but does not cause movement | no |
| 4 | adharma-dravya | medium of rest | enables but does not cause stopping | no |
| 5 | ākāśa | space | provides room for all other substances | no |
| 6 | kāla | time | enables change and sequence (*vartanā*) | no |

**Important name-trap:** #3 is called *dharma* but has nothing to do with the Hindu/Buddhist concept of dharma (duty, righteousness, teaching). It is a purely physical medium. The commentarial tradition flags this repeatedly. The fish-in-water analogy from Pūjyapāda (via Vijay K. Jain 2018): water enables fish to move but does not push them; dharma-dravya enables souls and matter to move but does not cause the movement. Adharma-dravya is the same logic for rest: earth enables a traveller to rest but does not force them to stop.

### 2.3 Astikāyas and kāla {#astikaya}

Five of the six dravyas are **astikāyas** — they have multiple space-points (*pradeśa*) and spatial extension. They literally take up space. Kāla (time) is a dravya (confirmed by TS 5.39: *kālaśca* — "time also is a substance") but is **not** an astikāya: kāla-atoms occupy single, non-combining space-points and cannot aggregate. Time exists at every space-point but never combines spatially.

This is a sectarian touchpoint: Digambara Jains (who use the Sarvārthasiddhi) accept kāla as the sixth dravya; some Śvetāmbara sources treat the list differently. The TS itself includes kāla (TS 5.39 directly fetched).

## 3. Mode: how substances change — *paryāya* {#paryaya}

### 3.1 Guṇa vs paryāya {#guna-paryaya}

Every dravya has both guṇas and paryāyas. The distinction is critical:

| | guṇa (quality) | paryāya (mode) |
|---|---|---|
| Pūjyapāda's term | *anvaya* — continuity, association | *vyatireka* — distinction, discontinuity |
| What it is | permanent, inalienable attribute | transient modification |
| Example (soul / jīva) | capacity for knowledge (always present) | knowing this pitcher right now (a momentary act) |
| Example (matter / pudgala) | having some colour (always present) | being red vs yellow (changes) |
| Maps onto sat's triad | dhrauvya side | utpāda/vyaya side |

The guṇa is what the dravya *is*; the paryāya is what the dravya *is doing* at this moment. The soul always has the capacity for knowledge (guṇa); but whether it is currently knowing a pitcher or knowing a cloth is a paryāya — it will change next moment.

Source: TS 5.38 and Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi, Vijay K. Jain 2018.

### 3.2 Why paryāya matters for epistemology {#paryaya-naya}

This distinction is not just metaphysics for its own sake. It directly grounds the Jain theory of knowledge (*anekāntavāda*, covered in Chapter 02). The seven standpoints (*nayas*) split cleanly along this line:

- **Substance-standpoints** (*dravyārthika nayas*): naigama, saṃgraha, vyavahāra — they foreground the guṇa/dravya dimension; they emphasise what is permanent and shared
- **Mode-standpoints** (*paryāyārthika nayas*): ṛjusūtra, śabda, samabhirūḍha, evaṃbhūta — they foreground the paryāya dimension; they emphasise what is specific and momentary

*Anekāntavāda*'s claim that "a substance is both permanent and impermanent" is grounded here: permanent with respect to guṇas and dravya; impermanent with respect to paryāyas. Each naya captures one real dimension; no single naya captures the whole.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain paryāya ≠ Buddhist pratītyasamutpāda (dependent origination)**
>
> Buddhist dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) is also a theory of change: things arise in dependence on conditions, and cease when conditions change. The surface similarity to paryāya (things change, moment by moment) is real.
>
> The difference is the substrate: Buddhist dependent origination explicitly denies an enduring dravya-substrate behind the changes — the "arising and ceasing" is the whole story; there is no guṇa-persisting-dravya underneath. Jain paryāya-change **requires** a dravya-guṇa substrate — you cannot have a mode-change without something that is changing. This is the anti-Buddhist realist move in Jain metaphysics: change without a substance is, for Jains, incoherent. The two theories are not compatible; they answer the same question ("what is change?") with mutually exclusive ontologies.

## 4. Matter: the one substance with sensory form — *pudgala* {#pudgala}

### 4.1 Definition and the four qualities {#pudgala-def}

Of the six dravyas, five are *amūrta* — formless, without sensory qualities. Pudgala (matter) is the one exception: it is *mūrta*, having sensory form. The Tattvārtha Sūtra defines it in five words:

> **rūpiṇaḥ pudgalāḥ**
> "Things which have form constitute matter (pudgala)."
> — TS 5.5, Vijay K. Jain (tr.), Motilal Banarsidass 2018

Pūjyapāda's commentary specifies what "form" (*rūpa*) means: the aggregated effect of four qualities, which are always found together in any piece of matter:

| quality | Sanskrit |
|---|---|
| touch | sparśa |
| taste | rasa |
| smell | gandha |
| colour / visual form | varṇa |

Every unit of matter — from the smallest atom to the largest aggregate — has all four. This is different from Vaiśeṣika atomism (Chapter 06), where only earth-atoms have all four qualities; water-atoms have three, fire-atoms two, air-atoms one.

**The etymology** (reported in secondary sources; not from a directly fetched critical commentary): *pud* = to combine/accumulate; *gala* = to dissolve. Pudgala is "that which accumulates and dissolves" — a dynamic substance, not inert matter. Treat this as a mnemonic, not a verified etymology.

### 4.2 Two and only two forms of matter {#pudgala-forms}

All matter is either:
1. **Paramāṇu** — the ultimate, partless atom (section 5 below)
2. **Skandha** — any aggregate of two or more paramāṇus (section 6 below)

There is nothing in between. A piece of wood is a skandha. Karma particles are a skandha. Your body is a skandha. The entire physical universe of Jain ontology is paramāṇus and their combinations.

## 5. The atom: matter's ultimate unit — *paramāṇu* {#paramanu}

### 5.1 The definition {#paramanu-def}

The paramāṇu is the irreducible, partless unit of pudgala. The argument for its existence is the infinite-regress argument, given in Pūjyapāda's commentary on TS 5.11: if you keep dividing matter, division cannot go on forever — there must be a terminal unit. That unit is the paramāṇu.

The Tattvārtha Sūtra (TS 5.11, Vijay K. Jain 2018) states that the paramāṇu has **no space-points** (*pradeśa*) — it is partless in the strictest sense: no spatial extension, nothing to divide. Everything with spatial extension is already an aggregate.

**What every paramāṇu has:**
- One touch: either *snigdha* (oily/adhesive/viscous) **or** *rūkṣa* (dry/repellent)
- One taste
- One smell
- One colour

No paramāṇu is quality-free. The qualities are not added later — they are constitutive. (A note on verse attribution: WisdomLib aggregations cite "TS 5.5" for paramāṇu qualities, while Vijay K. Jain 2018 has *rūpiṇaḥ pudgalāḥ* at TS 5.5 and the partless-atom claim at TS 5.11. The discrepancy likely reflects Digambara/Śvetāmbara recension differences in verse numbering; treat TS 5.11 from Vijay K. Jain 2018 as the verified reference here.)

**The paramāṇu is posited, not observed.** Ordinary sense faculties cannot perceive a single paramāṇu in isolation. It is an ontological requirement — the atomic floor that matter's combinatorics demands — not an empirical discovery.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain paramāṇu ≠ modern atom**
>
> The surface similarity: both are "the smallest unit of matter," arrived at by asking "what happens if you keep dividing?" The modern atom was named after the Greek *atomos* (indivisible) for the same reason.
>
> **Three precise differences:**
>
> 1. **Structure**: The modern atom has internal structure — protons, neutrons, electrons — and can be split (nuclear fission). The Jain paramāṇu is truly partless: no internal structure, no further division possible even in principle.
>
> 2. **Method**: The modern atom is an empirical discovery, confirmed by experiment (cathode rays, Millikan's oil-drop, etc.). The Jain paramāṇu is a philosophical posit from the infinite-regress argument — it is known by inference, not by observation.
>
> 3. **Qualities**: The modern atom has no intrinsic smell, taste, or colour — these are emergent properties of aggregates interacting with sensory systems. The Jain paramāṇu has one of each as intrinsic properties, present in isolation.
>
> The `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edge between these two concepts is the primary anti-inflation guardrail in this corpus. "The Jains discovered atomic theory" is the exact claim the project's prime directive exists to prevent.

## 6. Aggregates: how atoms build the world — *skandha* {#skandha}

### 6.1 The bonding rule {#skandha-bonding}

Paramāṇus do not combine arbitrarily. Each paramāṇu has an intensity of its snigdha/rūkṣa quality measured in degrees. The binding rule (from the commentarial tradition via Wikipedia citing Jain sources; the specific TS verse is not confirmed in this run):

**Two paramāṇus bind into a skandha if and only if their intensity values differ by at least two degrees.**

Examples:
- A 2-degree-snigdha paramāṇu + a 4-degree-rūkṣa paramāṇu → they bond (difference = 2)
- A 3-degree-snigdha + a 3-degree-snigdha → they do not bond (same polarity, difference = 0)

This gives matter its structural rules. It is not magic or divine will; it is a determinate physical law built into the qualities paramāṇus already have.

Two processes operate:
- **Saṃghāta** (fusion): paramāṇus or smaller aggregates combine into a larger skandha
- **Bheda** (fission): an aggregate splits into smaller skandhas or paramāṇus

The entire material world is a continuous churn of saṃghāta and bheda.

### 6.2 Six types of aggregate {#skandha-types}

The Niyamsāra (a Jain philosophical text; Digambara tradition) classifies skandhas into six types by subtlety — from the most tangible to the imperceptible (source: Wikipedia citing Niyamsāra; Niyamsāra critical edition not fetched):

| type | Sanskrit | example | can you perceive it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross-gross | sthūla-sthūla | stone, wood | yes; tangible; breaks but does not reunite |
| Gross | sthūla | water, milk | yes; tangible; splits and reunites naturally |
| Gross-fine | sthūla-sūkṣma | light, shadow | visible but not tangible |
| Fine-gross | sūkṣma-sthūla | smell, taste, sound | perceptible by non-visual senses |
| Fine | sūkṣma | karma particles | not by physical senses |
| Fine-fine | sūkṣma-sūkṣma | subtler than karma | not even by supra-normal senses |

### 6.3 Karma as a fine skandha {#skandha-karma}

The "fine" skandha (sūkṣma) is the category karma-matter occupies. In Jain physics, **karma is a physical substance** — a real material aggregate fine enough to be imperceptible to the senses but capable of adhering to the soul (jīva). This is not a metaphor. The entire Jain soteriological project — the liberation of the soul — is the physical removal of karma-skandhas that have attached to it.

This makes the Jain account unique among Indian traditions:
- Hindu/Vedic karma: a moral-causal law (intention + action → consequence), not a material substance
- Buddhist karma: rooted in *cetanā* (intention); not a material particle
- Jain karma: fine physical matter that literally sticks to the soul via passion (*kaṣāya*) and must be shed (*nirjarā*) to achieve liberation

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain skandha ≠ Buddhist khandha (five aggregates)**
>
> The Sanskrit word is the same. The concepts are not.
>
> **Jain skandha**: strictly material — an aggregate of paramāṇus. Mental and conscious phenomena do not aggregate this way; they belong to jīva (soul), not pudgala (matter). A Jain skandha is always and only matter.
>
> **Buddhist khandha** (Pali; Sanskrit *skandha*): the five aggregates that constitute personal experience — *rūpa* (form/body), *vedanā* (feeling), *saññā* (perception), *saṅkhārā* (mental formations), *viññāṇa* (consciousness). Four of the five are explicitly mental/experiential. The Buddhist khandhas are used to argue that there is no permanent self behind experience — each khandha arises and passes, and there is nothing else.
>
> Same word: (1) matter-only aggregates in a realist ontology vs (2) psycho-physical aggregates in a no-self analysis. They answer different questions.
