# Chapter 19 — The Texts: Śruti, Smṛti, and the Authors Who May Not Have Existed

**Concepts covered:** upaniṣad · ṛta · yajña · brahma-sūtra · bādarāyaṇa · mahābhārata · rāmāyaṇa · gītā · samatva · sthitaprajña · jīvanmukti · viṣṇu-sahasranāma · vyāsa · vālmīki · dāna · saṃsāra
**Confidence:** medium throughout, with an unusually **uneven** evidential base that this chapter foregrounds rather than averages away. Three files here rest partly on **directly-quoted verses** (BG 2.48 *samatvaṁ yoga ucyate*; BG 2.54–55 *sthitaprajñasya kā bhāṣā*; TS 7.38 *anugrahārthaṃ svasyātisargo dānam*, the one item fetched from an edition with commentary). Two rest on **named scholarly monographs** ([upanishad.md](../../../concepts/upanishad.md): Olivelle 1998 and Radhakrishnan 1953; [badarayana.md](../../../concepts/badarayana.md): a Triveni Journal essay plus a dedicated monograph). The rest rest on encyclopedia summaries, and **no critical edition was fetched** — not BORI's Mahābhārata, not Baroda's Rāmāyaṇa, not a Brahmasūtra edition, not the Ṛgveda.
**Two files are `contested`:** [gita.md](../../../concepts/gita.md) (what is the text's real aim?) and [badarayana.md](../../../concepts/badarayana.md) (is its author Vyāsa or not?). Both disagreements are the finding.

## How to use this chapter

Chapter 11 read these texts as **philosophy** — what Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, and Dvaita each make of the *prasthānatrayī*, and how four *mahāvākyas* support three incompatible metaphysics. Chapter 17 read the Veda as the object of **exegesis** — Mīmāṃsā's claim that it is eternal, authorless, and self-validating. Chapter 18 read the **deities** who inhabit these texts.

This chapter reads the texts **as texts**: how they were made, layered, attributed, transmitted, and classified; what their own tradition says they are; and what the ideas at their centre — *ṛta*, *yajña*, *saṃsāra*, *dāna* — actually claim.

**Remit note.** The Vedānta metaphysics is Ch. 11's and is not re-derived; the four mahāvākyas and their three readings live there. The Mīmāṃsā theory of scriptural authority (*apauruṣeyatva*, *śabda-nityatva*, *svataḥ-prāmāṇya*) is Ch. 17's and is summarised here only where a text-historical point needs it. The deities are Ch. 18's.

**The organising question, and it is a genuinely awkward one.** Almost every major text in this chapter is attributed to a figure whose historical existence the corpus's own sources decline to affirm — and in two cases the *tradition itself* says the name is a job description. Meanwhile the texts that carry the most authority are precisely the ones the tradition insists have **no author at all**. So the question this chapter is organised around is: **what work is authorship doing in Indian textual culture, if it is so often admittedly fictional?** §7 answers it. The short version is that an attribution is a claim about *authority and lineage*, and reading it as a claim about *who wrote this* is a category error — an error the tradition rarely makes and modern readers routinely do.

## 1. The genre grid — and why "Hindu scripture" is a misleading phrase {#genre}

Nothing else in this chapter parses correctly until this grid is in place. The tradition's own categories cut differently from the Western "scripture / not scripture" line.

| category | literally | authority | what belongs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **śruti** | "heard" | revealed, **authorless** (*apauruṣeya*), eternal | the four Vedas — Saṃhitās, Brāhmaṇas, Āraṇyakas, **Upaniṣads** |
| **smṛti** | "remembered" | human-authored, derivative, revisable | the epics, Purāṇas, Dharmaśāstra, the **Bhagavad Gītā** |
| **itihāsa** | "thus it was" | a sub-genre of smṛti: history-as-story | **Mahābhārata**, **Rāmāyaṇa** |
| **kāvya** | "poetry" | literary art, judged as art | the Rāmāyaṇa is the *ādikāvya*, "the first poem" |
| **sūtra** | "thread" | aphorism-compendium requiring commentary | **Brahmasūtra**, Mīmāṃsāsūtra (Ch. 17), Nyāya/Vaiśeṣika-sūtra (Ch. 16), Yogasūtra (Ch. 15) |

Three consequences, each of which trips up readers who skip the grid:

**First — the Upaniṣads are śruti, and the Gītā is not.** This is the single most consequential fact about the Vedānta canon. The *prasthānatrayī* (Ch. 11 §1) is deliberately **heterogeneous**: one śruti strand (the mukhya Upaniṣads), one sūtra strand (the Brahmasūtra), and one *smṛti* strand (the Gītā). Every Vedānta school elevated a remembered text to sit beside a revealed one. That is a choice, and an interesting one: it means the tradition wanted a **narrative, dialogic, personal** authority alongside the impersonal revealed and the terse systematic — three genres, not three volumes.

**Second — "the fifth Veda" is an honorific, not a reclassification.** Tradition calls the Mahābhārata the fifth Veda, and the concept file is emphatic about what that does and does not mean: it is a claim about **authority and scope**, *not* a claim that the epic is *śruti*. It remains *itihāsa* + *kāvya*, human-authored. The distinction survives the compliment.

**Third — "Hindu scripture" as a category is partly a colonial import.** The IEP's Gītā entry cautions explicitly against the phrase as anachronistic, and the caution is worth taking seriously here rather than filing away. The single English word flattens the śruti/smṛti line that the tradition treats as the most important line there is, and it imports an expectation — one closed book, one canon, one revealed authority — that fits no Indian tradition in this corpus and misdescribes several. The corpus therefore says *śruti*, *smṛti*, *itihāsa*, and *sūtra* and lets the difference show.

## 2. The Upaniṣads — the end of the Veda {#upanishad}

*→ concept file: [upanishad.md](../../../concepts/upanishad.md)*

**Upaniṣad** (*upa-ni-ṣad*, "sitting down near [a teacher]") names the philosophical-contemplative texts forming the **final stratum** of Vedic literature — hence *Vedānta*, "the end of the Veda," a phrase that is simultaneously a position in the corpus and a claim about culmination.

**The canon has two sizes, and the gap is the point.** The Muktikā canon lists **108** Upaniṣads. But the **12–13 *mukhya*** ("principal") ones — defined, circularly and revealingly, as *those Śaṅkara commented on* — constitute the scriptural foundation of every Vedānta school. The remaining ~95 span the last centuries BCE through the **15th c. CE**. So the "Upaniṣads" are not one corpus but a genre that stayed open for two thousand years, with a small early core that a single 8th-century commentator's attention effectively canonised.

| Veda | mukhya Upaniṣads |
|---|---|
| Ṛgveda | Aitareya, Kauṣītakī |
| Sāmaveda | Chāndogya, Kena |
| Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda | Taittirīya, Kaṭha, Śvetāśvatara, Maitrī |
| Śukla Yajurveda | Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Īśā |
| Atharvaveda | Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Praśna |

**Dating: contested in detail, converged at order of magnitude** — and this file is one of the corpus's better-sourced, because it has two genuinely independent scholarly treatments 45 years apart with different selection criteria and different chronological methods:

| source | dating |
|---|---|
| **Olivelle 1998**, *The Early Upaniṣads* (critical text + translation, 32 Upaniṣads) | Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya — the two oldest — **7th–6th c. BCE**, i.e. **pre-Buddhist**; Taittirīya/Aitareya/Kauṣītakī 6th–5th c. BCE |
| **Radhakrishnan 1953**, *The Principal Upaniṣads* (18 Upaniṣads) | wider: **900–600 BCE**; notes mutual śramaṇa–Upaniṣadic exchange |
| Western scholarship generally | 700–500 BCE for the earliest |

That Olivelle and Radhakrishnan converge on the brahman–ātman–mahāvākya core while differing on selection and date-method is what licenses the corpus's "converged" verdict here. Note also Radhakrishnan's observation about **mutual śramaṇa–Upaniṣadic exchange**: the traffic between the Upaniṣadic and the Jain/Buddhist worlds ran both ways, which is the honest framing for a period the Ch. 03–04 material approaches from the other side.

**The doctrinal ambiguity is a scholarly consensus, not a defect.** The four mahāvākyas support Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita, and Bhedābheda readings (the full table is Ch. 11 §1) — and, as the concept file puts it, the Brahmasūtra's bhāṣyas **do not resolve the ambiguity; they produce the disagreement.** Olivelle, Radhakrishnan, and all major Indologists agree the texts are genuinely ambiguous at exactly this point. This is worth stating plainly because the alternative framing — that three of the four schools are simply misreading — is what every school says about the other three, and no neutral party endorses it.

**The European transmission is a separate line worth knowing**, because it explains why 19th-century Europe met these texts in a particular shape. **Dārā Shukūh's Persian translation (*Sirr-i Akbar*, 1656) → Anquetil-Duperron's Latin (*Oupnek'hat*, 1801–02) → Schopenhauer.** That path runs **entirely independent of the Śaṅkara bhāṣya tradition** — a Mughal prince's Persian, rendered into Latin, read by a German pessimist. The Upaniṣads that entered European philosophy did not arrive through the Advaita commentarial apparatus that shaped them in India.

## 3. Ṛta — the order the Veda is about {#rta}

*→ concept file: [rta.md](../../../concepts/rta.md)*

**Ṛta** ("that which is properly joined; fixed order; truth") is the Vedic concept of **cosmic, moral, and ritual order** — and it has one structural feature that makes it unusual in the history of religion:

> **Ṛta is not a deity. The gods themselves obey ṛta and are judged by it; they do not command it.** It is a normative order **prior to and above** the gods.

Varuṇa and Mitra, the Ādityas, are its **guardians, not its authors** — Varuṇa is the "friend" and "charioteer" of ṛta, its celestial enforcer who punishes oath-breakers. He does not create it. With ~390 occurrences it is the **highest-frequency abstract principle in the Ṛgveda**.

**Etymology as a hard constraint** (this is one of the corpus's few cases where PIE philology genuinely constrains rather than merely suggests): PIE *\*h₂r-tós*, "properly joined, right, true" — cognate with Latin *artus*, Greek *arariskō*, and English **"art."** Sanskrit *ṛ-* ("to go, move") + *-ta* (past passive participle) = **"what has moved in its proper course."** Both senses live in the one root: physical regularity (the sun moves as it should) and normative rightness (an act is *ṛta* when properly done).

| domain | what ṛta governs | example |
|---|---|---|
| cosmic / physical | sunrise and sunset, seasons, rain, the celestial bodies | Varuṇa's "fixed paths" of the night sky |
| moral / ethical | honesty and righteousness in conduct; cosmic truth | oath-keeping; *anṛta* (falsehood) is the violation |
| ritual | the proper performance and correct order of sacred acts | correctly ordered *yajña* generates ṛta; misperformance is against it |

**Ṛta and *satya*** appear together as a cognate pair throughout the Ṛgveda: **ṛta is the active cosmic process; satya is the static truth-quality**. How things *move correctly* and how things *are*. The concept file names the closest Greek analogue — *logos* (rational structure) paired with *alētheia* (truth) — as an analogy, and it is a good one precisely because it is offered as an analogy.

**The ṛta → dharma transition** is the large historical fact. By the late Vedic and Upaniṣadic period ṛta recedes and **dharma** takes its place: dharma is ṛta's "particularised manifestation" in duty, social obligation, and religious law — more prescriptive and context-sensitive, less cosmic in sweep. The move from Vedic ritualism (right cosmic order) to the classical Dharmaśāstra tradition (right social conduct) *is* this substitution. And it is worth registering what is lost in it: ṛta is an order the gods obey; dharma, in most of its classical uses, is an order that people obey.

**The cross-cultural table — offered as comparison, labelled as comparison:**

| tradition | ordering principle | personal? | moral force? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vedic | **ṛta** | impersonal — gods obey, cannot override | yes — *anṛta* violates it |
| Iranian / Avestan | ***aṣ̌a*** (a true cognate of ṛta) | impersonal; Ahura Mazdā embodies it | yes — *aṣ̌a* vs *druj* (the lie) |
| Greek | *moira* (allotment) / *logos* (rational structure) | impersonal | partially |
| Chinese | *Dao* (the Way) | impersonal | yes |
| Buddhist | *dhamma* in its natural-law sense (Ch. 12) | impersonal cosmic law | partially |

The concept file's own caveat is the model for how this corpus handles such tables: *"these are structural comparisons, not identities."* Ṛta and Dao share impersonality and cross-domain scope; ṛta and *aṣ̌a* share a **PIE etymology and a dual cosmic/ethical function** — a real inheritance, not a resemblance. But the absence of a cosmic redemption narrative (Zoroastrianism has one) or a metaphysical substrate (Advaita's Brahman is one) keeps each distinct.

> ⚠️ **Ṛta ≠ Brahman.** The temptation is strong — both are the deep order of things, and ṛta is genuinely the Ṛgvedic precursor from which Brahman-as-absolute develops. But ṛta is a **functional ordering principle** (how things move rightly), while Advaita's Brahman is a **metaphysical substance** (what everything ultimately *is*): pure consciousness and being, not cosmic law. The corpus types this as a non-equivalence *and* stores the historical influence, which is the right pair for a genuine ancestor that is not an identity.

## 4. Yajña — the ritual, and its three successive reinterpretations {#yajna}

*→ concept file: [yajna.md](../../../concepts/yajna.md)*

**Yajña** (root *yaj*, "to worship, honour, revere") is Vedic sacrifice performed in or before the sacred fire, offering oblations — ghee, grain, soma — with mantras. Its history is the clearest case in this corpus of **one practice surviving three complete reinterpretations without ever being abandoned**, and the three layers *co-exist* rather than replacing one another:

| layer | source | yajña becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Brāhmaṇa / Śrauta** | Brāhmaṇa texts, Yajurveda | external fire-ritual; priest-performed; the cosmic mechanism maintaining order |
| **Upaniṣadic internalisation** | Chāndogya Upaniṣad (~700 BCE) | inner discipline — *"what is called yajña is really the chaste life of the student"* |
| **Gītā expansion** | BG 3.11, 4.24 | **any** selfless action done without attachment, offered up |

**The cosmic-exchange theology** is stated outright at **BG 3.11**, one of the chapter's directly-quoted verses:

> *devān bhāvayatānena te devā bhāvayantu vaḥ / parasparaṃ bhāvayantaḥ śreyaḥ param avāpsyatha*
> "By this [yajña], nourish the gods; may the gods nourish you; mutually nourishing one another, you will attain the supreme good."

Agni carries the offering heavenward (Ch. 18 §7.1); the devas return rain, crops, prosperity. This is a **structured reciprocal exchange**, not petition and not praise — which is why the Vedic religion looks so unlike what "religion" usually connotes, and why Ch. 17's finding that Mīmāṃsā is functionally godless is less shocking than it first sounds. A system of exchange does not need a God; it needs correct performance.

**The Pañca Mahā-Yajña** (five great daily sacrifices required of householders) ritualise the exchange at daily scale: *Deva-yajña* (ghee to the gods), *Pitṛ-yajña* (libations to ancestors), *Brahma-yajña* (Vedic recitation), *Bhūta-yajña* (food to living beings), *Manuṣya-yajña* (alms to humans). Note the reach: gods, ancestors, texts, animals, strangers — the whole obligated world in five acts.

**The Mīmāṃsā formalisation** (Ch. 17 §1) takes yajña as its paradigm case for deriving binding Vedic injunctions (*vidhi*). The sacrifice is *apauruṣeya* (unauthored) and *nitya* (eternal), and its authority derives not from any divine command but from the Veda's own self-validating injunctive force. The consequence, stated as flatly as the concept file states it: **the sacrifice does not worship a god who mandated it; the sacrifice *is itself* the dharmic obligation.** That single sentence is why the most orthodox school in India needs no deity.

**Yajña and ṛta.** In the Ṛgvedic worldview, yajña *maintains* ṛta. The **Puruṣa Sūkta** goes further: the cosmos itself arises from a primordial sacrifice, the dismemberment of the cosmic Puruṣa. So ritual sacrifice **re-enacts the originary cosmic event** — every yajña is a repetition of the act that made the world, keeping it in order. That is the strongest possible answer to "why does the ritual matter?", and it is why misperformance is not merely ineffective but *anṛta*.

> ⚠️ **The ahiṃsā problem — flagged, not resolved.** Vedic animal sacrifice (*paśu-yajña*) collides head-on with Jain absolute ahiṃsā (Ch. 08) and Buddhist compassion ethics (Ch. 12). The Brāhmaṇical response is that Vedic ritual killing is **dharmic, not *hiṃsā***, because it fulfils an eternal injunction — the act is reclassified by its warrant. **Jain and Buddhist traditions reject the distinction entirely** and condemn Vedic sacrifice as violence. The corpus stores this as `yajna often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent ahimsa` and does not adjudicate: this is a genuine normative disagreement between traditions, not a confusion one side can clear up for the other.

## 5. The Brahmasūtra — terseness as a design property {#brahma-sutra}

*→ concept files: [brahma-sutra.md](../../../concepts/brahma-sutra.md) · [badarayana.md](../../../concepts/badarayana.md)*

The **Brahmasūtra** (also *Vedāntasūtra*, *Uttara-Mīmāṃsā-sūtra*, *Śārīraka-sūtra* — three aliases for one text, which the concept file is careful to mark as aliases rather than as a conflation) is the founding text of the Vedānta darśana. Core material ~200 BCE–200 CE; surviving form ~400–450 CE. Structure: **~555 sūtras in 4 adhyāyas / 16 pādas / 223 adhikaraṇas.**

**Its opening pair sets the project:**

> **BS 1.1.1** — *athāto brahma-jijñāsā*, "now, therefore, the inquiry into Brahman."
> **BS 1.1.2** — *janmādy asya yataḥ*, "[Brahman is] that from which the origination, sustenance, and dissolution of this world proceed."

Two things follow immediately. First, BS 1.1.1 is a deliberate echo of Mīmāṃsāsūtra 1.1.1, *athāto dharma-jijñāsā* — the two texts announce themselves in the same words with one noun changed, and the pairing is the whole architecture of Vedic exegesis. Second, note what BS 1.1.2 already commits to: **Brahman is a conscious source**, which is a thesis, and specifically a thesis aimed at Sāṃkhya.

**Terseness is not a defect of the text; it is what the text is.** The sūtras are "often two or three words," and the concept file's phrase is exact: **"intelligible only through a commentary (*bhāṣya*)."** A sūtra-text is a mnemonic index to an oral teaching, not a self-standing exposition. Which produces the consequence this chapter cares about:

**One text, three incompatible systems, built on the same words.** Śaṅkara (8th c.) reads strict non-dualism; Rāmānuja (11th–12th c.) reads qualified non-dualism; Madhva (13th c.) reads dualism; Nimbārka and Vallabha read further variants. The corpus calls this **"the clearest case of a single authoritative text generating divergent, mutually exclusive interpretations."** And it is worth seeing *why* the Brahmasūtra is that case rather than the Upaniṣads: the Upaniṣads are ambiguous because they are many voices over centuries; the Brahmasūtra is ambiguous because it was **compressed on purpose** and the key was never written down. Two different mechanisms producing the same result.

> ⚠️ **Brahmasūtra ∥ Mīmāṃsāsūtra — and NOT equivalent.** The parallel is real and structural: *Uttara* (later) Mīmāṃsā to Jaimini's *Pūrva* (prior) Mīmāṃsā, identical openings, both treating the Veda as supreme authority. The non-equivalence is that the two inquiries **point opposite ways**: Pūrva Mīmāṃsā is the *karma-kāṇḍa* (ritual action, dharma), Uttara Mīmāṃsā the *jñāna-kāṇḍa* (knowledge of Brahman, liberation). One "Mīmāṃsā," two halves of the Veda, two goals.
>
> ⚠️ **Brahmasūtra ∥ Sāṃkhyakārikā — and NOT equivalent.** Both are āstika root-texts accounting for the world's ultimate cause. But **the Brahmasūtra explicitly refutes Sāṃkhya**: the cause is the *conscious* Brahman, not an *unconscious* prakṛti evolving on its own (Ch. 15). A conscious cause against an unconscious one — and BS 1.1.2 states the Vedānta side in four words.

### 5.1 Bādarāyaṇa — an author who is contested, not unknown {#badarayana}

The Brahmasūtra's authorship is **converged**: Bādarāyaṇa wrote it. What is **contested — and is the finding** — is *who Bādarāyaṇa was*:

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| Bādarāyaṇa is **distinct** from Veda-Vyāsa | early Vedāntins (Upavarṣa; Śaṅkara, per the Triveni analysis); modern historical scholarship | a separate historical sūtra-author; the Vyāsa equation is a later overlay |
| Bādarāyaṇa **=** Veda-Vyāsa, the Mahābhārata compiler | later Advaitins, 8th–9th c. onward | one legendary super-author — flagged by critics as *"patently an anachronism"* |
| the text reached its form c. 400–450 CE over a core of 200 BCE–200 CE | the encyclopedia and reference sources | the figure anchors a **long-redacted text**, not a datable individual |

Three genuinely independent signals — an encyclopedia, a scholarly journal essay arguing the distinctness directly from Upavarṣa and Śaṅkara, and a dedicated monograph on the identity dispute — **converge on the authorship and split on the identity.** That split is why the file is `contested`, and it is a good illustration of a distinction worth internalising: *contested* here does not mean "poorly researched." It means the research produced two defensible answers, and the corpus stores both.

The third row is the one that matters most, and it generalises to nearly every figure in §7: **the figure anchors a long-redacted text, not a datable individual.**

## 6. The two epics {#epics}

*→ concept files: [mahabharata.md](../../../concepts/mahabharata.md) · [ramayana.md](../../../concepts/ramayana.md)*

Two *itihāsa* ("thus it was") epics — sibling *smṛti* narratives that transmit dharma **through story rather than through revelation**. Both are *smṛti*, not *śruti*; both are also *kāvya*. Neither claims to be revealed, and both are, by any measure of actual influence, more formative of lived Hindu practice than the śruti they defer to.

### 6.1 Mahābhārata — a text that documents its own growth {#mahabharata}

The **Mahābhārata** narrates the dynastic struggle between the five **Pāṇḍavas** and the hundred **Kauravas**, two branches of the Kuru family, culminating in the eighteen-day war at Kurukṣetra. Traditionally ascribed to **Vyāsa**, who is himself a character in it. Actually a layered redaction compiled over roughly **400 BCE – 400 CE**, reaching its received form by the early Gupta period.

**The tradition preserves its own three growth stages**, which is a remarkable piece of textual self-awareness:

| stage | verses | reciter |
|---|---|---|
| *Jaya* ("victory") | 8,800 | Vyāsa |
| *Bhārata* | 24,000 | Vaiśaṃpāyana |
| *Mahābhārata* | ~100,000 | Ugraśravas Sauti |

An eleven-fold expansion, with a different reciter named at each stage. The text tells you it grew, and roughly by how much, and through whose mouths. Compare the Rāmāyaṇa (§6.2), where the layering has to be **reconstructed by scholars** against the text's own claim of unity — two epics, two completely different relationships to their own composition history.

**Scale:** ~100,000 ślokas in **18 parvas** plus the *khila* (the *Harivaṃśa* appendix) — about four times the Rāmāyaṇa, and roughly ten times the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* combined.

**Why it matters structurally for this corpus: the Mahābhārata is a *container*.** Two separately-written concept nodes sit inside it:

- the **Bhagavad Gītā** — *Bhīṣma Parva* (Book 6), chapters 25–42 (§6.3);
- the **Viṣṇu-sahasranāma** — *Anuśāsana Parva* (Book 13), ch. 149 (§6.4).

And the longest book, the ***Śānti Parva*** (Book 12), is Bhīṣma's dying instruction to Yudhiṣṭhira on *dharma*, kingship, and the four *puruṣārthas* — which makes the epic **as much a dharmaśāstra-style teaching vehicle as a narrative**. An epic that swallowed a theological dialogue, a thousand-name hymn, and a treatise on statecraft, and kept going.

*Sourcing note:* the BORI critical edition (Sukthankar et al., 1919–1966) — a landmark of textual scholarship — **was not fetched**; verse counts are traditional and rounded; the *khila* and the regional recensions vary. Britannica returned HTTP 403 and could not serve as a third signal.

### 6.2 Rāmāyaṇa — where the layering changes the theology {#ramayana}

The **Rāmāyaṇa** ("Rāma's journey") is the shorter epic, ascribed to **Vālmīki** and celebrated as the ***ādikāvya***, "the first poem" — the foundational work of Sanskrit *kāvya*, and thus a text whose status is **literary as well as religious**. ~**24,000 ślokas** in **7 kāṇḍas**: Bāla, Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundara, Yuddha, Uttara.

The narrative: Rāma's fourteen-year exile (engineered by his stepmother Kaikeyī), Sītā's abduction by **Rāvaṇa** of Laṅkā, the war to recover her with Hanumān and the monkey army (Ch. 18 §6.3), and the return and coronation — *Rāma-rājya*, the ideal reign.

**The layering, and why it is theologically load-bearing.** The core is **Books 2–6**, roughly **5th–4th c. BCE** with growth to ~3rd c. CE. The **Bāla Kāṇḍa** (first) and **Uttara Kāṇḍa** (last) are widely held to be **later interpolations** — absent from the oldest manuscripts, differing in language and rhetoric. And here is the consequence:

> **Rāma's explicit identification as the seventh *avatāra* of Viṣṇu is concentrated in exactly those two framing books.** The older core presents him chiefly as an **ideal human hero**. The fully divine Rāma is, in significant part, a **later devotional overlay on an older heroic narrative** — and the full avatāra theology develops further still in the medieval retellings (the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa's Advaita rereading; Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas*).

This is the cleanest case in the corpus of **textual stratigraphy producing a theological result**. It does not show that Rāma's divinity is false — it shows *when* it entered the text, which is a different and answerable question. And it is why Ch. 18 §2.4 states the caution rather than simply calling Rāma the seventh avatāra.

The epic's theme is dharma **taught through character-ideals**: Rāma as ideal son and king, Sītā as ideal wife, Bharata as ideal brother — with the *dharma-saṅkaṭa* episodes (Vālin's killing; Sītā's banishment) as the hard cases the tradition argues over rather than recites.

*Sourcing note:* the Baroda critical edition (Oriental Institute, 1960–1975) was **not fetched**; verse counts are traditional; the Northern and Southern recensions differ; the avatāra-stratification rests on standard scholarly consensus rather than on a fetched apparatus.

### 6.3 The Bhagavad Gītā — contested about its own aim {#gita}

*→ concept file: [gita.md](../../../concepts/gita.md) — status: **contested***

A 700-verse dialogue between **Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna**, embedded in the *Bhīṣma Parva*. Technically ***smṛti***, yet elevated by all three major Vedānta founders into the *prasthānatrayī*.

**The contested question is not metaphysical but exegetical: which of the three paths is the text's real aim?**

| reading | commentary | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **jñāna-primacy** — the Gītā ultimately teaches Brahman-realisation through knowledge; karma and bhakti are preparatory rungs | Śaṅkara's *Gītābhāṣya* (~8th c.) | ātman = Brahman; the personal Kṛṣṇa is saguṇa Brahman, a provisional object; liberation is knowledge dissolving individuality |
| **bhakti-culmination** — it teaches surrender (*prapatti*) and loving devotion to Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa, with karma and jñāna subordinated | Rāmānuja's *Gītābhāṣya* (~11th c.); Yāmunācārya's three-hexad division | souls and matter are modes of Brahman's body; liberation is dwelling in Brahman's presence, individuality retained |
| **bhakti within eternal dualism** — total devotion to a Viṣṇu utterly distinct from jīvas; knowing the five eternal differences is liberating | Madhva's *Gītā-tātparya* (~13th c.) | *pañcabheda* is real; liberation is eternal devotional service, individuality fully preserved |

**All three cite the same verses.** The clearest bhakti anchor is the ***charama-śloka***, BG 18.66 — *sarva-dharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja*, "abandon all dharmas and take refuge in me alone." **And Śaṅkara reads even this as *upāya*, a means, not the final teaching.** That single move is the whole shape of the dispute: there is no verse so plainly devotional that a jñāna-primacy reading cannot reclassify it as preparatory, and none so plainly non-dual that a bhakti reading cannot subordinate it to surrender. The text is 700 verses long and every school finds its own thesis in the same ones.

The **three-hexad division** attributed to Yāmunācārya — chapters 1–6, 7–12, 13–18 mapped onto karma, bhakti, and jñāna — is itself an interpretive instrument: divide the text into thirds and the middle third, bhakti, becomes structurally central. Commentators do not merely read the Gītā; they **partition** it, and the partition does much of the arguing.

Three verses recur across this corpus: **BG 4.7–8** (the avatāra declaration — Ch. 11 §7.2, Ch. 18 §2.2), **BG 2.20** (the eternal ātman, *na jāyate mriyate vā kadācit*), and **BG 18.66** (the charama-śloka). None was fetched from a critical edition; all are cited through corpus prior-work and reference sources.

### 6.3.1 Two teachings the Gītā gives its own names {#samatva-sthitaprajna}

*→ concept files: [samatva.md](../../../concepts/samatva.md) · [sthitaprajna.md](../../../concepts/sthitaprajna.md) · [jivanmukti.md](../../../concepts/jivanmukti.md)*

Beneath the three-school quarrel, the Gītā's second chapter contains a self-contained teaching that the sectarian dispute barely touches, and it is anchored by two directly-quoted verses — the best-sourced material in this chapter.

***Samatva*** — **the Gītā's own definition of yoga**, BG 2.48:

> *yogasthaḥ kuru karmāṇi saṅgaṃ tyaktvā dhanañjaya / siddhy-asiddhyoḥ samo bhūtvā samatvaṃ yoga ucyate*
> "Established in yoga, perform actions, abandoning attachment; being even-minded toward success and failure — **this equanimity is called yoga**."

Two things are worth pressing on. First, this is *not* indifference or withdrawal: Kṛṣṇa explicitly commands continued action (*kuru karmāṇi*). Samatva is the inner steadiness that makes **desireless action** possible — the affective core of karma-yoga. Second, and this is a genuine chronological point the concept file makes: **the Gītā defines yoga as equanimity centuries before Patañjali defines it as *citta-vṛtti-nirodha***, the stilling of the mind (Ch. 15).

> ⚠️ **The Gītā's yoga ≠ the Yoga-darśana's yoga.** Same word, two definitions, and they pull opposite ways: the Gītā's is a disposition held **within action**; Patañjali's is the **cessation** of mental modification via aṣṭāṅga toward kaivalya. Engaged equanimity against meditative cessation. The corpus types this as an explicit non-equivalence — one of the most consequential single-word collisions in Indian philosophy, since "yoga" is now the most exported Sanskrit noun there is.

***Sthitaprajña*** — the **exemplar** in whom samatva is a settled character. The passage begins with Arjuna's question at BG 2.54, *sthitaprajñasya kā bhāṣā* — "what is the mark of the person of steady wisdom; how does he speak, sit, move?" — and Kṛṣṇa's answer runs to the end of the chapter (2.55–72). The defining verse, BG 2.55:

> *prajahāti yadā kāmān sarvān pārtha mano-gatān / ātmany evātmanā tuṣṭaḥ sthitaprajñas tadocyate*
> "When one casts off all the desires of the mind and is satisfied in the Self by the Self alone, then one is called *sthitaprajña*."

The portrait that follows: untroubled in sorrow, free of craving in pleasure, without attachment, fear, or anger (2.56); withdrawing the senses **as a tortoise draws in its limbs** (2.58); abandoning all desire and the sense of "I" and "mine" (2.71); established in *brāhmī sthiti*, unshaken even at the hour of death (2.72). **Wisdom expressed not as withdrawal from action but as unshakable inner balance amid it.**

***Jīvanmukti*** — the doctrine the sthitaprajña portrait becomes. **Liberation while still embodied**, against *videhamukti*, liberation at death. Its central puzzle is sharp: if liberating knowledge has dawned, **why does the body persist?** Advaita's answer classifies karma into three kinds — knowledge burns up ***sañcita*** (stored) and ***āgāmin*** (future) karma, but ***prārabdha*** karma, the portion already "begun," which produced *this* body and is now bearing fruit, must simply **play itself out**. The image the tradition uses: a potter's wheel still spinning after the potter's hand is withdrawn. The jīvanmukta lives out the prārabdha **generating no new karma**, until the body falls.

And here too the Vedāntas split: Advaita makes jīvanmukti the supreme goal (liberation is *knowledge*, so it can be complete while alive), while **Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita resist or reframe it** — if liberation is union with or nearness to a personal God attained on shedding the body, then full mukti *is* videhamukti and a living person is at most a devotee-in-progress. **The same individuality question that divides the three Vedāntas over mokṣa divides them over whether anyone can be liberated right now.**

> ⚠️ **Jīvanmukta ∥ Jain arihant — a striking structural match, and a metaphysical break.** Both are liberated **while still embodied**, with a residue of karma (Advaita *prārabdha* / Jain *aghātiyā*) sustaining the body until it falls. The parallel is real and the corpus draws it. It breaks on what is liberated: the jīvanmukta has realised **non-dual identity** with Brahman (individuality ultimately unreal); the arihant is an **eternally distinct omniscient jīva** (Ch. 07, Ch. 10). Monism against jīva-pluralism, with the same mechanism bolted on. The corpus also notes the mirror in Buddhism: *saupādisesa* / *anupādisesa* nibbāna, with-remainder and without — same shape again, and again a different metaphysics of what persists (Ch. 12).

### 6.4 The Viṣṇu-sahasranāma — one text, four schools, no fight {#vsn}

*→ concept file: [vishnu-sahasranama.md](../../../concepts/vishnu-sahasranama.md)*

The **Viṣṇusahasranāma** ("Thousand Names of Viṣṇu") is a *stotra* of **108 verses** containing 1,000 names, sitting in the *Anuśāsana Parva* (Mbh. 13.149, vv. 14–120). Its narrative frame is one of the great scenes of the epic: the dying **Bhīṣma**, on his bed of arrows at Kurukṣetra, recites the names in answer to Yudhiṣṭhira's question about the highest refuge.

Each name is a compound functioning as an epithet — one attribute, function, or cosmic role. Those bearing philosophical weight include *Dharmādhyakṣa* (overseer of dharma), *Bhāvanaḥ* (cause of all existence), *Vidhātā* (dispenser of karma-fruits), *Apramattaḥ* (the vigilant). A *phalaśruti* (fruit-of-recitation) section closes it.

**The commentarial pattern is the same as the Brahmasūtra's — with one difference worth noticing.**

| commentator | school | reading |
|---|---|---|
| Śaṅkarācārya (~8th c.) | Advaita | Viṣṇu = Brahman itself (nirguṇa); names referencing Śiva are **non-different** qualities — a Smārta non-sectarian synthesis |
| Parāśara Bhaṭṭar (~12th c.) | Viśiṣṭādvaita | each name describes a quality of the Supreme Person who has souls and matter as his body; explicitly sectarian |
| Vidyādhirāja Tīrtha | Dvaita | the names affirm Viṣṇu's utter distinctness from jīvas and matter |
| Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa | Acintya-bhedābheda | the names establish simultaneous difference and non-difference |

The difference from the Brahmasūtra case: this text's divergent readings produced **cross-sectarian reach rather than schism**. Śaṅkara's commentary — reading the Śiva-names as non-different qualities — made the hymn acceptable to **Smārtas who revere Śiva equally**; Vaiṣṇavas hold it their supreme stotra; Sikh communities include a version in the *Sundar Gutka*. A text that four incompatible metaphysics can each claim ends up being recited by more people than any of them.

That is worth setting against Ch. 18 §8. A *thousand names* is a form that can absorb theological disagreement, because a name is an epithet and epithets are cheap to reinterpret. A *sūtra* cannot: it makes a claim, and the claim has to mean one thing. **Genre determines how much disagreement a text can hold.**

## 7. The authors who may not have existed {#authors}

*→ concept files: [vyasa.md](../../../concepts/vyasa.md) · [valmiki.md](../../../concepts/valmiki.md) · [badarayana.md](../../../concepts/badarayana.md)*

Now the chapter's organising question. Three of the four great attributions in this chapter dissolve under examination in three *different* ways — and the tradition is, in each case, considerably less naive about it than a modern reader expects.

### 7.1 Vyāsa — a title for a function {#vyasa}

**Vyāsa** means **"the arranger, the divider."** Full name Kṛṣṇa-Dvaipāyana Vyāsa, "the dark one, island-born." And the concept file's first substantive point is the one that reframes everything: **the name is a title for a function, not merely a personal name.** He is called *Vyāsa* because he **divided the one primordial Veda into four** — hence *Veda-Vyāsa*.

| work | role |
|---|---|
| Mahābhārata | composer, dictating to Gaṇeśa as scribe |
| the division of the Veda into four | **the act that names him** |
| the eighteen Purāṇas | arranger / abridger |
| Brahmasūtra | *identified with* Bādarāyaṇa, its author |

**He is a character in his own epic**, and not a peripheral one: through *niyoga* he is the biological grandfather of **both warring lineages** — father of Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Pāṇḍu, and Vidura — and intervenes as a spiritual counsellor. The author writes himself into the story as its progenitor.

**And the tradition itself makes the role explicit.** The Viṣṇu Purāṇa: *"in every Dvāpara age Viṣṇu, in the person of Vyāsa, divides the Veda."* This is not a claim about one man. It is a claim that the Veda-dividing function **recurs every cosmic age and is discharged by whoever occupies the office**, with Viṣṇu as its *aṃśāvatāra* (partial descent) each time. He is a *chiranjīvī*, one of the seven immortals, honoured on **Guru Pūrṇimā** — the archetypal guru, which is precisely a role rather than a person.

Modern scholarship's verdict — that "Vyāsa" is very likely a **role-title for the Veda-compiler discharged across ages and editors** — therefore does not *debunk* the tradition. **It restates it in secular vocabulary.** The traditional attributions are claims of *authority and lineage*, not verifiable biography, and the tradition says so in its own idiom.

> ⚠️ **Two Vyāsas.** The "Vyāsa" credited with the *Yoga-Bhāṣya* (Ch. 15) is a **later honorific overlay, NOT** this legendary Mahābhārata Vyāsa — possibly Patañjali himself. Same name, distinct authorial figure. The corpus draws it as an explicit non-equivalence, because the honorific-name habit that makes Vyāsa a role-title also makes him a magnet for unrelated attributions.

### 7.2 Vālmīki — the poet whose origin story is about poetry {#valmiki}

**Vālmīki** is the ***ādikavi***, "the first poet," author of the *ādikāvya*. His name derives from *valmīka*, "anthill" — after years of motionless meditation, anthills are said to have grown over him.

**The origin-of-poetry legend is the most elegant etiology in this chapter.** Witnessing a hunter shoot one of a mating pair of *krauñca* birds, the grieving sage's spontaneous curse **fell into perfect metre** — and *śoka* (grief) became *śloka* (verse). At Brahmā's command he then composed the whole Rāmāyaṇa in the new measure. So the tradition's account of how Sanskrit's foundational metre was invented is: **the first poem was an involuntary cry of grief that happened to scan**, and poetry is what sorrow does when it finds a shape. Whatever its historicity, that is a serious claim about what verse is for, and it is embedded in a pun.

**The redemption legend** — later, Purāṇic — makes Vālmīki first a forest robber (abandoned as a child, raised by hunters, turned dacoit; popularly named **Ratnākara**, though the fetched sources give *Lohajaṅgha* or leave him unnamed, and the concept file flags the discrepancy rather than smoothing it) who is redirected by sages, given a mantra, and meditates until transformed into the ādikavi. A paradigmatic **bhakti** motif: *even a murderer is redeemed by repeating the divine name.*

**He too is a character in his own poem.** In the Uttara Kāṇḍa the exiled **Sītā** takes refuge in his hermitage, where her twins **Lava and Kuśa** are born, become his disciples, and are taught the Rāmāyaṇa — which they then recite. The poem contains the scene of its own transmission. (Note where that scene sits: the **Uttara Kāṇḍa**, one of the two later books — §6.2. The frame that authorises the poem is part of the later layer.)

**Historicity:** unfixable. Textual stages span roughly the 8th–4th c. BCE with growth to ~3rd c. CE; the anthill and redemption legends are Purāṇic and not part of the oldest stratum. **"Vālmīki" names a tradition's founding-poet figure more than a securely datable individual.**

### 7.3 The pattern {#author-pattern}

Set the three side by side:

| figure | what the corpus can say | how the attribution dissolves |
|---|---|---|
| **Vyāsa** | a **role-title** for the Veda-compiler, recurring each Dvāpara age, discharged by many editors | the tradition itself says the office recurs; scholarship agrees in different words |
| **Vālmīki** | a **founding-poet figure**; datable text-stages, undatable person; the biography is later Purāṇic accretion | legends postdate the text; his in-poem frame sits in an interpolated book |
| **Bādarāyaṇa** | authorship **converged**, identity **contested**; anchors a text redacted across ~650 years | the Vyāsa equation was called "patently an anachronism" by critics *within* the tradition |

Three different dissolutions, one shared structure: **the name marks a lineage and a claim to authority, and the text behind it is a redaction across centuries.**

And now the fact that makes the pattern coherent rather than embarrassing. **The texts with the most authority in this system claim no author at all.** The Veda is *apauruṣeya*, unauthored — and Mīmāṃsā's argument (Ch. 17 §1) is that this is exactly *why* it cannot be impeached: an author, human or divine, would be a fallible point of failure. In a textual culture where authorlessness is the highest status, an attributed author is not a historical assertion competing with authorlessness. It is a **different kind of marker** — of school, of transmission line, of who vouches for the text.

Which resolves the chapter's organising question. Asking "did Vyāsa write the Mahābhārata?" imports a notion of authorship — one individual, one act of composition, one copyright-bearing origin — that this textual culture did not use for its central texts and explicitly denied for its highest. The tradition's own answer is *"Vyāsa divides the Veda in every age,"* and the honest translation of that is not "yes" or "no" but **"that is not what the name is claiming."**

## 8. What the texts are for — saṃsāra and dāna {#purpose}

*→ concept files: [samsara.md](../../../concepts/samsara.md) · [dana.md](../../../concepts/dana.md)*

Two cross-tradition concepts close the chapter, because they mark the frame every text in it presupposes and the practice every tradition in this corpus shares.

### 8.1 Saṃsāra — the shared frame, and the three-way disagreement inside it {#samsara}

**Saṃsāra** ("wandering, flowing together") is the beginningless cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. **All three major Indian traditions agree on the structure** — beings cycle through realms driven by karma; the goal is escape. They disagree, fundamentally, on **what cycles** and **what karma is**:

| tradition | what cycles | what karma is | liberation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buddhist** | **no permanent self** — a stream of consciousness carried by karmic momentum | intentional mental action generating volitional formations — *not a substance* | *nirvāṇa*: craving ceases, the grasping stops |
| **Jain** | the ***jīva*** — a real, permanent, individual soul-substance | **physical ultra-fine matter** (*karma-vargaṇā*) adhering to the jīva through passion | *mokṣa*: the jīva sheds all karma and ascends to the siddha-śilā, **individual identity intact** |
| **Hindu / Vedic** | the ***ātman*** — a real, permanent, unchanging self | a **moral/cosmic force**, not matter; ripens as life-circumstance | *mokṣa*: recognition of identity with Brahman (Advaita), or blissful relation to a personal God |

**The Buddhist paradox** — if there is no permanent self, what wanders? — gets its canonical answer at **SN 15.3** (Assu Sutta): *"From an inconstruable beginning comes transmigration. A beginning point is not evident, though beings hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving are transmigrating and wandering on."* What wanders is a stream of conditioned processes, not a substance — the standard image being a flame passed from candle to candle with no "fire-essence" transferring.

**The realms** differ too: six Buddhist realms (deva, asura, human, animal, *preta*, *naraka*, functioning both cosmologically and psychologically); four Jain *gatis* (human, heavenly, infernal, animal-and-plant). And the Jain and Buddhist traditions converge on a striking restriction: **liberation is possible only from human birth.** The concept file's framing is the right one — *this is not chauvinism but soteriological logic*: gods live too long in comfort, hell-beings suffer too intensely, animals lack the reflective capacity. Liberation needs a specific combination of reflective capacity and freedom from extremes, and only one realm supplies it.

All three agree saṃsāra is *anādi*, beginningless. They differ on ending: Buddhist and Jain traditions hold it can be **definitively ended** for an individual; the Hindu account liberates the individual ātman while cosmic saṃsāra may repeat in vast cycles — Brahmā's kalpas (Ch. 18 §1.1) turning regardless.

> ⚠️ **Saṃsāra's shared vocabulary is the corpus's most dangerous.** `saṃsāra`, `karma`, `mokṣa` appear in all three traditions and mean **structurally different things in each**. Reading Buddhist rebirth as requiring a jīva-equivalent violates *anattā* outright; reading Jain karma as a "moral force" erases the physical-matter theory that the entire Jain soteriology (Ch. 03) is built on. The corpus stores `samsara often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent jiva` for exactly this.

### 8.2 Dāna — the same act, three mechanisms {#dana}

**Dāna** (giving) is present in all three traditions, always as **spiritual practice rather than philanthropy**, and it carries this chapter's one primary-source-with-commentary citation — Jain, as it happens, from **Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.38**, fetched directly:

> *anugrahārthaṃ svasyātisargo dānam* — "Charity is the giving of one's own objects to another for **mutual benefit** (*anugraha*)."

Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi* glosses *anugraha* as beneficial to **both** parties: the giver accumulates *puṇya*, the recipient gains support for spiritual advancement. That mutuality is doing real work — it makes dāna a transaction in a karmic economy rather than a one-way transfer, which is why the Jain analysis then decomposes it into four elements (giver, recipient, substance, method/intention) and four categories: *āhāra* (food), *auṣadha* (medicine), *śāstra* (knowledge), and ***abhaya-dāna*** (fearlessness) — the last being **the highest, because it directly enacts *ahiṃsā***.

The traditions agree that intention matters and that giving without expectation of return is higher. **They disagree on the mechanism that makes giving meritorious**, and the disagreement is the same one that runs through saṃsāra:

| | Jain | Buddhist | Hindu |
|---|---|---|---|
| mechanism of merit | **karma-particles dislodged** by the generous act | *cetanā* (intention) generates positive kamma; selfless giving reduces ego-binding | dharma-karma: giving as **cosmic reciprocity** |
| highest form | *abhaya-dāna* — fearlessness / protection | *dhamma-dāna* — sharing the teaching | *sāttvika-dāna* — pure, unconditional, at the proper time and place |
| primary recipient | Jain monastics | the Saṅgha; the spiritually advanced | a worthy recipient, assessed for character |
| Hindu textual anchor | — | — | **BG 17.20–22**: three types by *guṇa* (sāttvika / rājasika / tāmasika) |

Notice the shape. **Jain merit is mechanical** (matter dislodged), **Buddhist merit is psychological** (intention purifies), **Hindu merit is economic** (the cosmic exchange of §4 again, now in the register of the gift). One act, three physics.

And notice where the Hindu answer lands: *sāttvika* dāna is analysed by *guṇa* — which is Sāṃkhya's apparatus (Ch. 15) deployed inside a Gītā ethics. The texts of this chapter borrow their machinery from the darśanas of the last four.

## 9. Cross-tradition: how canons are made {#cross-tradition}

Setting this chapter's textual history beside the other traditions in the corpus:

| | Vedic/Hindu | Jain (Ch. 10) | Buddhist (Ch. 12) | Greek (Ch. 14) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| highest authority | ***śruti*** — revealed, **authorless**, eternal | the Jina's omniscient teaching, transmitted through the ācāryas | the Buddha's word (*buddhavacana*), as remembered and recited | none — argument answers to argument |
| the authorship of the central texts | denied for śruti; **role-titles** for smṛti | attributed to named ācāryas within a lineage | attributed to the Buddha, compiled by councils | **named individuals**, disputed but real |
| what a commentary does | **produces** the schools — one sūtra, three systems | transmits and elaborates; sect-specific | elaborates; school-forming | interprets a text taken to have one author's meaning |
| how disagreement is handled | rival *bhāṣyas* on a shared root text | a **canon split** — the Digambara/Śvetāmbara divide over which Āgamas survive | *nikāya* divisions; separate recensions | schools succeed and refute one another |
| genre of the most-read text | *itihāsa* / *kāvya* — **story**, not revelation | doctrinal sūtra + devotional *stotra* | *sutta* — the Buddha's remembered discourse | dialogue and treatise |

Two observations the table earns.

**First — the Vedic system is the only one that makes authorlessness the highest credential**, and the only one whose central texts are therefore *unfalsifiable by authorship criticism*. You cannot impeach the Veda by showing who wrote it, because it claims nobody did. Mīmāṃsā (Ch. 17) built an entire epistemology on that structure. Every other tradition here rests its authority on a *person* — the Jina, the Buddha, or in the Greek case nobody at all — and is correspondingly vulnerable to (and sometimes ruined by) historical criticism of that person.

**Second — the commentary does different work in each column, and this is the most portable finding in the chapter.** In Greek philosophy a commentary explicates a text whose author had a meaning. In Vedānta the commentary **constitutes the school**: Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, and Madhva are not three interpreters of Vedānta, they are three Vedāntas, and the Brahmasūtra's ~555 sūtras are the shared surface on which the disagreement is conducted. Ch. 17 §2 found the same pattern in Mīmāṃsā (a single agreed bhāṣya, Śabara's, forking into Bhāṭṭa and Prābhākara sub-commentaries) and Ch. 16 §8 found the extreme case in Vaiśeṣika, where **Praśastapāda's commentary displaced the sūtra as the anchor**. **Where a school's identity lives — in the root text, the commentary, or the sub-commentary — is a variable of Indian intellectual history, not a constant.**

7. "The sacrifice does not worship a god who mandated it; the sacrifice *is itself* the dharmic obligation." Unpack that sentence and connect it to Ch. 17's finding about Mīmāṃsā's atheism.
8. Why is the Brahmasūtra's terseness a **design property** rather than a flaw? Compare the mechanism that makes it ambiguous with the mechanism that makes the Upaniṣads ambiguous — they are not the same mechanism.
9. What exactly is contested about Bādarāyaṇa, and what is *not*? Why is `contested` the right status for an entry whose central fact is agreed?
10. The Mahābhārata records its own three growth stages; the Rāmāyaṇa's layering must be reconstructed against its claim of unity. What does that difference change about how each text can be read?
11. State the theological consequence of the Bāla and Uttara Kāṇḍas being later. Why does that *not* amount to saying Rāma's divinity is false?
12. Śaṅkara reads even BG 18.66 as *upāya*. What does that move show about whether the Gītā's aim can be settled by citing more verses?
13. Distinguish the Gītā's *samatva* from Patañjali's *citta-vṛtti-nirodha*. Both are called "yoga." What is at stake in the collision?
14. Explain the *prārabdha* mechanism in jīvanmukti, and why Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita resist the whole doctrine.
15. The Viṣṇu-sahasranāma has four incompatible commentaries and gained *cross-sectarian* reach; the Brahmasūtra has four incompatible commentaries and produced *schools*. What explains the difference?
16. Take the three attributions — Vyāsa, Vālmīki, Bādarāyaṇa — and say how each dissolves differently. Then explain why the pattern is not embarrassing to the tradition, given what it says about the Veda's own authorship.
17. In one paragraph: three traditions share the words *saṃsāra*, *karma*, and *mokṣa*. Pick one and show precisely how the shared word conceals three different theories.
