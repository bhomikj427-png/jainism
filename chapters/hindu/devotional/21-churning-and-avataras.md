# Chapter 21 — The Churning of the Ocean: the Samudra-manthana Cycle and the Ten Descents

**Concepts covered:** samudra-manthana · amṛta · hālāhala · kūrma · vāsuki · dhanvantari · mohinī · rāhu · ketu · svarbhānu · airāvata · uccaiḥśravas · kaustubha · vāruṇī · pārijāta · kalpavṛkṣa · śeṣa · kadrū · vinatā · jaṭāyu · sampāti · matsya · varāha · narasiṃha · vāmana · bali · paraśurāma · kalki
**Confidence:** medium throughout — but the **sourcing shape here is different from every other chapter in this corpus, and better in one specific way.** Almost every node in the churning half is anchored on **one public-domain critical translation, read directly**: Kisari Mohan Ganguli's *Mahābhārata*, Book 1 (Ādi Parva), Section XVIII, via Wikisource. Several files add a second **primary-text** witness read directly — Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa Bāla Kāṇḍa 1.45.36–37 and Araṇya-Kāṇḍa 14; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.8.6 and 8.8.30 in Sanskrit; Ṛgveda 5.40.5–9 in Griffith's translation; and Sāyaṇa's commentary on RV 5.40.5. That is far more direct primary-text contact than Ch. 18 or Ch. 19 had.
**The countervailing weakness is equally specific and is stated in the concept files themselves:** the Purāṇic retellings of this episode are **weakly independent** — one narrative tradition told many times — so agreement between the Viṣṇu, Bhāgavata, and Matsya Purāṇas is *not* triangulation (charter §4: "copies are not confirmation"). Confidence stays at medium for that reason, not for thin sourcing.
**Five files are `contested`:** [kurma.md](../../../concepts/kurma.md) · [bali.md](../../../concepts/bali.md) · [varuni.md](../../../concepts/varuni.md) · [svarbhanu.md](../../../concepts/svarbhanu.md) · and (from Ch. 18) [krishna.md](../../../concepts/krishna.md). In every case the divergence is the finding.

## How to use this chapter

Three earlier chapters approached this material from outside it. **Ch. 11 §7.2** gave the *avatāra doctrine* — descent, BG 4.7–8, the tīrthaṅkara inversion. **Ch. 18** gave the *deities* — Viṣṇu, Śiva, the devotional families, and the vāhanas. **Ch. 19** gave the *texts* — the Mahābhārata as a container, the Rāmāyaṇa's layering, and what *smṛti* means.

This chapter is the **narrative cycle itself**: one episode, its apparatus, its products, its resolution, and the ten descents that grow out of and around it. It is the largest single cluster of written concept nodes in the corpus, and until now it had no chapter home.

**Remit note.** The avatāra *doctrine* (descent vs ascent; divine initiative; the Buddha-inclusion problem) is Ch. 11 and Ch. 18 §2.2 and is not re-derived — this chapter takes the **individual descents**. Viṣṇu, Śiva, Lakṣmī, Indra, Kāmadhenu, Garuḍa, and Aruṇa are primary-covered in Ch. 11 or Ch. 18 and appear here only where the cycle needs them.

**The organising question.** A reader who has absorbed Ch. 18 expects the daśāvatāra to be a single theological structure: ten descents of one God, listed in order. The concept files show something considerably messier and more interesting. **Three of the first four "descents of Viṣṇu" were originally somebody else's** — Vedic cosmic animals belonging to Prajāpati/Brahmā, later transferred to Viṣṇu as his cult rose. The fifth grew organically out of Viṣṇu's *own* Ṛgvedic act. The tenth is a prophecy whose biography is a chain of textual elaboration rather than an attested tradition. And the list itself is not closed. §7 works this through; §8 says what it means.

## 1. The apparatus {#apparatus}

*→ concept file: [samudra-manthana.md](../../../concepts/samudra-manthana.md)*

The **Samudra-manthana** ("churning of the ocean") is the cosmogonic episode in which the **devas and asuras agree a temporary truce** and churn the ocean of milk (*kṣīra-sāgara*) together to extract the **amṛta**, the nectar of immortality. The truce is the premise the whole story runs on, and it is worth registering before the treasures start surfacing: **the two sides that define cosmic order by their opposition have to cooperate to produce the thing they will then fight over.**

The machine is assembled from three parts, each of which is a written node in this corpus:

| part | what it is | node |
|---|---|---|
| **churning-rod** | Mount **Mandara** | — (no node) |
| **cord** | **Vāsuki**, king of the nāgas, wound round the mountain | [vasuki.md](../../../concepts/vasuki.md) |
| **pivot** | **Kūrma**, the tortoise, bearing the mountain on his back | [kurma.md](../../../concepts/kurma.md) |

And the two teams pull the serpent back and forth. Ganguli's translation is precise about the arrangement, and it is one of the details this chapter can quote from a critical translation read directly: **"the gods and the Asuras made Mandara their churning staff"**; **"Vasuki the cord"**; and **"The Asuras held Vasuki by the hood and the gods by the tail."**

**Vāsuki** (son of the sage Kaśyapa and **Kadrū** the serpent-mother, brother of **Śeṣa** the eldest and of Takṣaka and Manasā) does double duty across this corpus's Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava material: churning-cord here, and the serpent coiled about **Śiva's neck** in iconography (Ch. 18 §3). In the Mahābhārata's snake-sacrifice cycle he is central again — cursed with the nāgas by Kadrū, he arranges the marriage that produces Āstīka, who halts King Janamejaya's *sarpa-satra*. One serpent, three narrative jobs.

**Śeṣa** — also **Ananta** — is the cycle's other great serpent and the one node in this chapter carrying **confidence: high**. He is the eldest of Kadrū's thousand nāga sons, supports the earth on his hoods, and is the thousand-headed couch on which Viṣṇu reclines in the cosmic ocean between world-cycles. His name is a doctrine: ***śeṣa* = "the remainder,"** from √*śiṣ*, "to remain" — because when the universe is annihilated at *pralaya*, **Śeṣa alone remains**, the residual indestructible substrate persisting across dissolution and re-creation. **Ananta** ("endless") names the same being from the complementary angle. The concept file's caution is one this project should keep visible: *"Recorded precisely and not modernized — this is a doctrine of the cosmic remnant, not a physics claim."* A serpent who survives the destruction of everything is not a conservation law.

## 2. What comes up, and in what order the tradition cannot agree {#products}

The churning throws up a succession of ***ratnas*** — treasures. And the first thing to record about the list is that **there isn't one**:

> **The set and order of the treasures varies by text** — the Mahābhārata, Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Bhāgavata Purāṇa, and Rāmāyaṇa each differ. The corpus records this explicitly rather than presenting a canonical "fourteen ratnas."

Here is what this corpus has nodes for, in the roughly standard sequence:

| product | what it is | where it goes | node status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **hālāhala** | the deadly poison, first up | drunk by **Śiva** | converged |
| **Kāmadhenu** | the wish-cow | to the Saptarṣi → Vasiṣṭha (Ch. 18 §7.4) | converged |
| **Kalpavṛkṣa** | the wish-tree | Indra's heaven | converged |
| **Pārijāta** | the night-flowering tree | Indra's heaven (later uprooted by Kṛṣṇa) | converged |
| **Kaustubha** | the crest-jewel | **Viṣṇu's chest** — permanently | converged |
| **Vāruṇī** | wine, and the goddess personifying it | **contested** — devas or asuras? | **contested** |
| **Airāvata** | the white elephant | **Indra's** mount | converged |
| **Uccaiḥśravas** | the seven-headed white flying horse | Indra (or, in some texts, **Bali**) | converged |
| **Lakṣmī** | the goddess Śrī — the pre-eminent ratna | Viṣṇu (Ch. 11) | — (primary in Ch. 11) |
| **Dhanvantari** | the physician-god, rising **last** | bearing the **amṛta** | converged |

### 2.1 Hālāhala — the poison before the nectar {#halahala}

*→ concept file: [halahala.md](../../../concepts/halahala.md)*

**Hālāhala** (also **Kālakūṭa**, "poison of death") is what the ocean gives up **first**. Before the nectar, the churning throws up a toxin whose fumes threaten to destroy devas and asuras alike — both teams, indiscriminately, which is the narrative point. They appeal to **Śiva**, who **drinks the poison** to save creation; his consort grips his throat so it cannot pass into his body, and the toxin stains his throat blue.

This is the standard etiology of **Nīlakaṇṭha**, "blue-throated" — one of Śiva's best-known names, and a rare case where a Śaiva epithet is grounded in a Vaiṣṇava-framed episode. Note the structural asymmetry: the churning is a Viṣṇu story (his tortoise is the pivot, his female form resolves it), and the one moment of pure self-sacrifice in it belongs to **Śiva**.

Two minor variants are recorded and not reconciled: whether Śiva drinks the whole of it or **Vāyu first lessens its potency**; and whether it rises from the churned ocean or is **emitted by Vāsuki** the churning-serpent and drunk directly from him.

### 2.2 The two wish-granting trees, and a class-vs-member trap {#trees}

*→ concept files: [kalpavriksha.md](../../../concepts/kalpavriksha.md) · [parijata.md](../../../concepts/parijata.md)*

The **Kalpavṛkṣa** (also *kalpataru*, *kalpadruma*) is the divine **wish-fulfilling tree**, described with jewelled parts — golden roots, coral leaves, gemstone fruit — which Indra takes to his heaven. It pairs with **Kāmadhenu** the wish-cow (Ch. 18 §7.4): the corpus stores `kalpavriksha structurally-parallel-to kamadhenu`, *"twin churning-born emblems of inexhaustible divine abundance."* Tree and cow, the same idea in two bodies.

**Pārijāta** is the divine **night-flowering tree** (popularly identified with *Nyctanthes arbor-tristis*, coral jasmine), also planted in Indra's Amarāvatī. Its famous narrative belongs to the Kṛṣṇa cycle: to please his consort **Satyabhāmā**, Kṛṣṇa wars with Indra and **uproots the Pārijāta** to Dvārakā — and then, in the tale's moral twist, its blossoms fall into the rival queen **Rukmiṇī's** yard, rewarding her greater devotion. A story about getting exactly what you demanded and finding it prefers someone else.

> ⚠️ **The class-vs-member trap, and it is a genuinely instructive one.** Both of these statements are true, and flattening them loses a taxonomy:
>
> - **Pārijāta *is* a kalpavṛkṣa** — it is a wish-fulfilling tree, and the generic class-name applies to it. Popular usage says "Pārijāta the Kalpavṛkṣa."
> - **Pārijāta is *not* the Kalpavṛkṣa** — because in the **five celestial trees** (*pañca-vṛkṣa*) of Indra's garden — **Mandāra, Pārijāta, Santāna, Kalpavṛkṣa, Haricandana** — the two are **distinct named members**.
>
> Both hold at once *because* "Kalpavṛkṣa" is simultaneously **the class** (any wish-tree) and **one specific sibling** in the five-list. The corpus draws `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` between them for exactly this reason: collapsing the two erases the five-trees enumeration. This is the same shape as a word being both a genus and one of its own species — rarer in myth than in taxonomy, and worth recognising when it appears.

### 2.3 Kaustubha — the one uncontested treasure {#kaustubha}

*→ concept file: [kaustubha.md](../../../concepts/kaustubha.md)*

The **Kaustubha** is the divine gem that comes to rest **permanently on Viṣṇu's chest**, giving him the epithet **Kaustubhadhāri**, "bearer of the Kaustubha." The concept file makes a point of its exceptional status in this cluster: **unlike the horse (owner disputed) or the poison (source disputed) or the wine (recipient disputed), Kaustubha's role is uncontested** — every source checked puts it on Viṣṇu's chest with no rival claimant.

And it has the chapter's cleanest two-witness support. Ganguli's Ādi Parva has the gem emerge from the churned ocean **already** described as gracing "the breast of Nārāyaṇa" — no separate claiming scene, the association immediate in the earliest telling. **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.8.6**, read in Sanskrit, makes it an act of will instead: *kaustubhākhyam abhūd ratnaṃ … tasmin maṇau spṛhāṃ cakre vakṣo-'laṅkaraṇe hariḥ* — "the gem named Kaustubha … Hari **desired** that jewel to decorate his chest." Different text, different era, same result, with the second supplying a motive the first left as description.

A small continuity worth noting: the gem carries forward into **Kṛṣṇa's** iconography (Mahābhārata Sabhā Parva ch. 5) — **one continuous attribute across Viṣṇu and his avatāra**, not a separately gifted duplicate. Given §7's finding about how loosely assembled the avatāra set is, a shared object that travels intact between Viṣṇu-proper and a descent is a real piece of theological glue.

### 2.4 Vāruṇī — a contested recipient, and a folk etymology flagged {#varuni}

*→ concept file: [varuni.md](../../../concepts/varuni.md) — status: **contested***

**Vāruṇī** ("belonging to Varuṇa") names **both** the wine produced by the churning **and** the goddess who personifies it — a genuine dual usage attested in the sources themselves, not an artefact of this project's filing. She is consistently a daughter of, or arising alongside, **Varuṇa**.

**Who takes her is a flat contradiction between two primary texts**, both read directly:

| reading | text |
|---|---|
| **devas accept, asuras reject** — "the sons of Diti have not espoused … but the sons of Aditi have espoused that impeccable Vāruṇī" | **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 1.45.36–37** |
| **asuras take her** — the demons, headed by **Bali**, "take possession" of Vāruṇī with Hari's permission | **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.8.30** |

A harmonising gloss exists — that the second version reflects "a growing ambivalence toward intoxicants," making her "a consolation for the defeated Asuras" — and the concept file records it as **interpretation, not as a primary source resolving the split**. This corpus does not promote a plausible reconciliation to a finding. Meanwhile the Mahābhārata's own Ādi Parva treats "Vāruṇī" mainly as **the name of the wine**, without personifying her as an actor at all — which is its own data point about how the personification developed.

> 🚩 **The *sura*/*asura* etymology — a folk etymology inside a primary text, and the corpus refuses to let it pass as philology.** The Rāmāyaṇa passage above plays directly on a narrative etymology: *those who accept **surā** (wine) are **sura** (gods); those who reject it are **a-sura**.* It is a real, attested, in-text derivation and worth recording as such.
>
> It is **not** the historical derivation. Modern historical linguistics derives ***asura*** from Proto-Indo-Iranian ***\*Hásuras***, "lord, powerful one" — a term applied in the early Ṛgveda **to gods, including Varuṇa himself**, and shifting toward "demon" only later. It is **unconnected to *surā* "wine."**
>
> This is exactly the discipline the charter's §4 signal 3 asks for: **etymology is a clue, not a verdict**, and a *tradition's own* etymology is evidence about the tradition, not about the word. Note also that the folk etymology is doing theological work — it makes accepting or rejecting an intoxicant the thing that *constitutes* the god/demon distinction, which is a far more interesting claim than the true derivation and is precisely why it was invented.

### 2.5 The animals, and the horse that starts another story {#animals}

*→ concept files: [airavata.md](../../../concepts/airavata.md) · [ucchaihshravas.md](../../../concepts/ucchaihshravas.md)*

**Airāvata** is the **white elephant**, king of elephants, who becomes **Indra's** mount — multi-headed (three or ten, depending on the source), tusked, and linked to **clouds and rain**, which is a natural fit for a storm-god's vehicle: the mythic elephants draw up water and release it as precipitation. He recurs in **Jain** cosmology and became a royal emblem across Southeast Asia (Thailand, Laos).

**Uccaiḥśravas** ("[the horse] of loud neighing") is the **seven-headed white flying horse**, "the best of horses," taken by **Indra** — or, in some texts, by the asura king **Bali**, an ownership variance the corpus records rather than settles.

The two are paired in the corpus as *"the churning's two great animal-treasures — the white elephant and the white horse."* But the horse does something the elephant does not: **it starts a second story that runs all the way into the Rāmāyaṇa.** That is §3.

## 3. The wager on the horse's tail — how the churning generates the Rāmāyaṇa's birds {#wager}

*→ concept files: [kadru.md](../../../concepts/kadru.md) · [vinata.md](../../../concepts/vinata.md) · [jatayu.md](../../../concepts/jatayu.md) · [sampati.md](../../../concepts/sampati.md)*

This is the chapter's most satisfying piece of narrative engineering, and it is worth tracing link by link because each step is a separate written node.

**Kadrū** and **Vinatā** are daughters of Prajāpati Dakṣa and **co-wives of the sage Kaśyapa**. Kadrū becomes **mother of the nāgas** — a thousand serpent sons, the eldest being Śeṣa, plus Vāsuki and Takṣaka. Vinatā asks instead for **two sons mightier than Kadrū's thousand** — and gets **Aruṇa** and **Garuḍa** (Ch. 18 §§6.4, 7.2).

**The wager.** The two sisters bet on the colour of **Uccaiḥśravas's** tail — the churning-horse. Kadrū wins **by deceit**, instructing her serpent sons to coil around the tail so that it shows black. Vinatā loses and is **enslaved**.

Now watch what that single act sets off:

1. **Vinatā's bondage** is the engine of **Garuḍa's** epic. To free his mother he must fetch the **amṛta** from the gods — so he **storms heaven, defeats the gods including Indra, and seizes the nectar-vessel** (Ch. 18 §6.4). The nectar the churning produced is stolen a second time, by a bird, for an entirely different reason.
2. It seeds the **perpetual enmity between nāgas and birds** — Kadrū's line against Vinatā's — which is why Garuḍa is "the enemy of all serpents" and why the corpus stores `shesha shares-vocabulary-with garuda` as *"the serpent and bird poles … sons of Kaśyapa by the rival co-wives."*
3. **Aruṇa**, Vinatā's elder son, was hatched prematurely and half-formed (she broke the egg impatiently) and **cursed her to 500 years of servitude** — the very bondage the wager then delivers. The curse precedes the wager and the wager fulfils it, which is a tighter piece of plotting than the tradition usually bothers with.
4. And **Aruṇa's own sons**, with Śyenī, are **Sampāti and Jaṭāyu** — the vultures of the Rāmāyaṇa.

**Jaṭāyu** is the *gṛdhrarāja*, "king of vultures," an old friend of King Daśaratha. In the Araṇya-Kāṇḍa, when Rāvaṇa abducts Sītā and carries her south through the sky, **the aged Jaṭāyu intercepts him and fights to free her.** Rāvaṇa severs his wings; the dying bird tells Rāma that Sītā was carried **southward**, and expires. Rāma performs his funeral rites. He is the epic's model of **self-sacrificing dharma — a creature who dies in another's service**.

**Sampāti** is his elder brother, and the brothers' origin story is its own small myth: racing toward the sun in youth, **Jaṭāyu soared too high and began to scorch, and Sampāti spread his wings above him as a shield** — burning them off and falling to the Vindhyas, where the sage Niśākara tended him and foretold their regrowth. His decisive moment comes in the Kiṣkindhā-Kāṇḍa: the despairing vānara search-party reaches the southern shore, and Sampāti — **on learning of his brother's death** — uses his long-range sight to declare that Sītā is held **across the sea, in Rāvaṇa's Aśoka-grove on Laṅkā**. That intelligence redirects the search and **enables Hanumān's leap**. As foretold, his wings then regenerate.

**So the causal chain runs: the churning produces a horse → two co-wives bet on its tail → one cheats → the loser is enslaved → her son raids heaven for the nectar → her other son's children are the two vultures who, between them, delay Sītā's abduction and then locate her.** The Rāmāyaṇa's search for Sītā turns on a bet placed at the churning of the ocean.

*A genealogical variant the corpus preserves rather than smooths:* encyclopedias give **Śyenī as the brothers' mother**; the Vālmīki text itself (Araṇya-Kāṇḍa 14, in Jaṭāyu's own mouth) makes Aruṇa the father and **Śyenī a more distant ancestress** in the Kaśyapa → Tāmrā → Vinatā avian line. Both are recorded. Sampāti's bird-form also varies between vulture and eagle.

## 4. The resolution — Mohinī, and a beheading with astronomical consequences {#resolution}

### 4.1 Mohinī {#mohini}

*→ concept file: [mohini.md](../../../concepts/mohini.md)*

**Mohinī** ("the enchantress," from *moha*, "delusion") is **Viṣṇu's female form — his only one.** At the climax of the churning, when the asuras seize the **amṛta**, Viṣṇu becomes the irresistibly beautiful Mohinī, **beguiles them into letting her distribute** the nectar, and serves it **to the devas alone**.

Note what the resolution actually is. The truce of §1 does not end in a battle won; it ends in a **trick**. The gods do not out-fight the asuras for the nectar they jointly produced — they are handed it by a disguise. Ganguli's text is direct about the mechanism: Viṣṇu, "assuming the form of an enchanting female," deludes them and recovers the nectar for the gods.

> ⚠️ **A classification the corpus declines to force.** Sources "present her ambiguously — sometimes as a temporary avatar, sometimes as Viṣṇu's *māyā*":
>
> | reading | commitment |
> |---|---|
> | Mohinī is an **avatāra** of Viṣṇu — his only female one | a genuine descent-form, counted among his manifestations |
> | Mohinī is Viṣṇu's ***māyā*** — a temporary guise | a deluding appearance, not a full avatāra in the daśāvatāra sense |
>
> **Her *function* is stable across both — deception in service of cosmic order. Only the label varies.** Which makes her a useful test case for Ch. 11's avatāra doctrine: is a form God takes briefly, to deceive, a *descent*? The tradition does not answer, and the corpus records the non-answer.

In later South Indian myth, Mohinī's union with **Śiva** produces **Ayyappa / Śāsta** (Hariharaputra) — another rare Vaiṣṇava-Śaiva bridge, alongside Hanumān's Rudra-aspect birth (Ch. 18 §6.3).

### 4.2 Rāhu and Ketu {#rahu-ketu}

*→ concept files: [rahu.md](../../../concepts/rahu.md) · [ketu.md](../../../concepts/ketu.md)*

During Mohinī's distribution, the asura **Svarbhānu** disguises himself among the gods to steal a sip. **Sūrya and Candra — the sun and moon — expose him**, and Viṣṇu beheads him with the discus. But the nectar has already reached his throat, so **the head is immortal while the body dies.**

- **Rāhu** = the severed **head**.
- **Ketu** = the severed **body/tail**.

In *jyotiṣa* both are reckoned **grahas** among the *navagraha*, and identified with the **lunar nodes**: Rāhu the **ascending (north)** node, Ketu the **descending (south)**, **180° opposite**, sharing the ~18-year nodal cycle. Rāhu is held responsible for solar eclipses, Ketu for lunar. Astrologically Ketu is read as **mokṣa-ward** — non-attachment, karmic residue, liberation — the inward pole to Rāhu's outward craving. *Ketu* also means "comet, banner, sign," carrying older associations with comets and meteors that were later absorbed into the node-identification.

And the sun and moon are the ones who informed on him. **The two luminaries he periodically swallows are the two who got him beheaded** — the myth's own explanation of why the enmity is permanent and why it recurs on a schedule.

> 🚩 **The eclipse conflation — §0 material, and one of the corpus's most important flags.**
>
> | layer | what it asserts |
> |---|---|
> | **myth** | Rāhu, the headless immortal, periodically **swallows** the sun and moon, who slip out of his open neck — the narrative cause of eclipses |
> | **astronomy (*siddhānta*)** | eclipses occur at the **lunar nodes**, where the moon's path crosses the ecliptic; classical Indian astronomers such as **Āryabhaṭa** gave a shadow-and-geometry account |
>
> **The two coexisted.** The node-points took the *names* Rāhu and Ketu, and the eclipse-myth was **correlated** with the nodal geometry. But the swallowing-demon story is **not itself an astronomical theory of eclipses**, and reading it as one — or as "the ancients secretly knew about the nodes" — **collapses a mythic etiology into a mathematical model they held separately, and demonstrably knew to be separate.**
>
> The relation is **naming and correlation, not identity.** This deserves emphasis because it is the single most common form the failure mode takes with Indian material: a genuine astronomical tradition existed *alongside* a myth, the myth's vocabulary was borrowed to label the astronomy, and later readers infer that the myth *was* the astronomy in code. It was not. Indian astronomers computed eclipses; Indian mythographers told a story about a beheaded asura; the same culture did both, and used one set of words for both. That is more interesting than the conflation, not less.

### 4.3 Svarbhānu — two layers, one name {#svarbhanu}

*→ concept file: [svarbhanu.md](../../../concepts/svarbhanu.md) — status: **contested***

**Svarbhānu** ("sun-splendour," *svar* + *bhānu*) is the asura behind both Rāhu and Ketu. And the sources reveal **two distinct textual layers, not one continuous story** — structurally the same stratification the corpus logged for **Indra** (Ch. 18 §7.3):

| layer | content | source |
|---|---|---|
| **Ṛgvedic (older)** | Svarbhānu pierces **Sūrya** "through and through with darkness" by *māyā*, plunging creatures into confusion; **Indra** shatters his magic; the sage **Atri**, through tapas and a sacred formula, locates and restores the hidden sun. **No churning, no amṛta theft, no beheading, no Rāhu/Ketu split appear in this hymn at all.** | **Ṛgveda 5.40.5–9** — Maṇḍala V, a Family Book, among the oldest strata |
| **epic/Purāṇic (later)** | the disguise at the churning, the stolen nectar, the beheading, head→Rāhu and body→Ketu | Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva §XVIII |

**The two layers are linked by name-continuity, not plot-continuity.** A self-contained Vedic eclipse-etiology — in which a demon darkens the sun by magic and *Indra and Atri* fix it — and a wholly separate Purāṇic amṛta-theft-and-beheading myth share only a name. Note what the Vedic version does *not* have: it has no Viṣṇu, no discus, no nectar, and no severed head. It is a different story about the same phenomenon.

**And the identity question is genuinely open:**

| reading | commitment | source |
|---|---|---|
| **outright identity** — Svarbhānu *is* Rāhu under an older name | the Vedic demon and the Purāṇic Rāhu are one being across two eras | **Sāyaṇa's commentary** on RV 5.40.5 — a real commentarial position, not a modern gloss |
| **whole-then-split** — Svarbhānu is the **pre-beheading whole**; Rāhu and Ketu are its two **parts** | Svarbhānu is identical to neither: "Svarbhānu became both entities" | Wikipedia's careful framing |

Because casual sources flatten the two while careful ones distinguish whole-being from head-only-part, the corpus draws the **sanctioned two-type edge pair** toward Rāhu: `shares-vocabulary-with` **plus** `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent`. Real identity-claim, not asserted as flat equivalence.

**The genealogy is unsettled too, and the file says so rather than picking.** Sāyaṇa offers two parentages *in the same breath* — son of **Kaśyapa and Dānu**, or of **Vipracitti and Siṃhikā** (the commoner Purāṇic default) — and a Matsya/Saura Purāṇa line makes him a son of **Bāṇāsura, son of Bali**, an unrelated third option. The one stable thread is downstream: Svarbhānu is father of **Prabhā/Suprabhā**, who marries Āyus and becomes mother of Nahuṣa, **tying the asura line into the lunar dynasty**.

*One methodological note worth repeating from the concept file:* a **Grokipedia entry surfaced in search was deliberately not used** — an AI-generated tertiary compilation is not an independent scholarly or primary source, and admitting one would silently break the charter's independence requirement. Recording the *refusal* is part of the record.

## 5. Dhanvantari and the amṛta {#amrita}

*→ concept files: [dhanvantari.md](../../../concepts/dhanvantari.md) · [amrita.md](../../../concepts/amrita.md)*

**Dhanvantari** rises **last** from the churning, bearing the pot of nectar — Ganguli: **"Dhanwantari himself with the white vessel of nectar in his hand."** He is the **physician of the gods** and the presiding deity of **Āyurveda**, reckoned an avatāra of Viṣṇu; four-armed, holding conch, discus, the amṛta-pot, and medicinal herbs (or a leech).

A parallel tradition gives him an **earthly incarnation as a king of Kāśī**, ancestor of **Divodāsa**, named in the **Suśruta Saṃhitā** tradition as a founder of surgery — which links the churning-born god to the **historical transmission of Āyurveda**. The concept file records the open variant: whether the Kāśī king *is* the descended deity or a separate Dhanvantari in his lineage. (The Āyurveda cluster proper — `ayurveda`, `charaka`, `sushruta`, `tridosha` — is still without a chapter; see the roadmap.)

***Amṛta*** is "non-death" — the privative *a-* plus the root *mṛ*, "to die." In the Ṛgveda the word is already an **epithet or synonym of soma**, the drink of the gods, so the churning's prize has a Vedic prehistory as a ritual substance before it becomes a narrative object.

> **A real linguistic inheritance, and a structural comparison — kept apart.**
>
> **The hard constraint:** *amṛta* is a **true cognate** of Greek **ambrosia** (*a-* "not" + *brotós* "mortal"), both reflexes of **PIE *\*n̥-mr̥-tós*, "un-dying."** That is comparative philology, not resemblance — the same word, inherited in two branches.
>
> **The softer claim:** Dumézil read the churning as a reflex of a proto-Indo-European **"ambrosia cycle,"** and Oosten noted **Norse parallels** (a sacred drink, a sea, a serpent, a conflict among gods). The corpus records these as **structural comparison, not identity** — and the distinction between the two paragraphs above is exactly the distinction this project exists to maintain. **A shared word is evidence of common inheritance. A shared story-shape is a hypothesis about common inheritance.** The first is nearly certain; the second is a scholarly proposal.
>
> **Buddhist reuse, typed honestly:** the term is later reused in Buddhism (Pali *amata*), where it names **not a literal nectar but "the deathless"** — an epithet of *nibbāna*, and in Vajrayāna "the sweet rain of Dharma." Shared vocabulary, different referent. The corpus stores `amrita shares-vocabulary-with nibbana-theravada` with precisely that note.

> 🚩 **The Kumbha Melā anachronism — flagged explicitly by the concept file.** The widely-repeated story that drops of amṛta **spilled at four sites**, which is why the Kumbha Melā rotates among them, is **absent from the ancient Purāṇas.** It is a **later theological overlay**, not part of the source episode. This matters because the festival is one of the largest human gatherings on earth and the etiology is universally recited as ancient: the *practice* may well be old, but **this myth is not the reason recorded in the old texts**, and the corpus does not let a living tradition's self-explanation stand as textual history.

## 6. The ten descents — a list that is not one thing {#dasavatara}

The **Daśāvatāra** is given in Ch. 11 §7.2 and Ch. 18 §2.2 as a list. This section takes the individual descents — and the first thing to establish is that **the list is not a fixed ancient canon.**

**Position six (Paraśurāma) and position ten (Kalki) are stable across every enumeration the corpus checked.** Positions **eight and nine are not** — some traditions, notably **Jayadeva's *Gīta Govinda***, swap or double up **Balarāma and Buddha**. The closed set of ten is a **Purāṇic-era standardisation**, not an original datum. (And the Buddha's presence in it is the assimilation move Ch. 18 §2.2 flags as unreciprocated.)

### 6.1 The reattribution pattern — three descents that were somebody else's {#reattribution}

*→ concept files: [matsya.md](../../../concepts/matsya.md) · [kurma.md](../../../concepts/kurma.md) · [varaha.md](../../../concepts/varaha.md)*

This is the chapter's central historical finding, and it holds across the first four positions with one exception.

**Matsya (1), the fish.** The narrative: a small fish begs **Manu** for protection, grows until no vessel holds it, warns him of a world-deluge; Manu builds a boat, loads the **seven sages** and the seeds of all species, ties it to the fish's horn, and is towed to the **northern mountain** to re-found creation. The Bhāgavata adds a second motive: Matsya slays the demon **Hayagrīva** and recovers the **stolen Vedas**.

But **who the fish is changes by textual layer**:

| layer | the fish is | source |
|---|---|---|
| Vedic | **a fish, associated with no deity at all** | Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa I.8.1 |
| epic | **Brahmā / Prajāpati**, who grants Manu the power of creation | Mahābhārata, Vana Parva |
| Purāṇic | explicitly **Viṣṇu** | Matsya / Bhāgavata Purāṇas |

**Kūrma (2), the tortoise** — the churning-pivot of §1, and the corpus marks the file **contested** on exactly this point. The **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** has a cosmic tortoise identified with **Prajāpati/Brahmā**; as Viṣṇu-worship rose, that role was **transferred**. Treating the Vedic Prajāpati-tortoise and the Vaiṣṇava Kūrma-avatāra as one being *"flattens a real diachronic shift … continuous in image but not in theology."*

**Varāha (3), the boar** — where the shift is most explicitly documented. The Purāṇic myth: the demon **Hiraṇyākṣa** drags the earth-goddess **Bhūdevī** into the cosmic ocean; Viṣṇu takes boar-form, slays him, and **lifts the earth out on his tusks**. The layering:

| layer | the boar is | source |
|---|---|---|
| Vedic | **Emūṣa**, a cosmic boar identified with **Prajāpati/Brahmā**, raising the hand-sized earth from the waters | Taittirīya Saṃhitā / Brāhmaṇa; Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa |
| **transitional** | ŚB 14.1.2 **harmonises the two**, conflating Emūṣa into Prajāpati | Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa |
| Purāṇic | **Viṣṇu**, the Hiraṇyākṣa-slayer | Mahābhārata; Viṣṇu / Bhāgavata Purāṇas |

The concept file quotes the point directly: *"In the Taittirīya Saṃhitā … and the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa it is **Prajāpati** who saves the earth in his boar incarnation and **not Viṣṇu**"* — the boar later "completes the full transition from Prajāpati-Brahmā to the avatar of Nārāyaṇa-Viṣṇu."

**So three of the first four descents are Vedic cosmic animals of Prajāpati/Brahmā, reattributed to Viṣṇu as his cult rose.** Set that beside Ch. 18 §1.1's finding that **Brahmā has two temples and two curse-myths explaining why** — and the picture sharpens considerably. Brahmā did not merely fade. **His mythology was harvested.** The creator-god's cosmic animals became the preserver-god's descents, and a later reader encounters them as having always been Viṣṇu's.

### 6.2 Vāmana — the exception that proves the pattern is a pattern {#vamana}

*→ concept files: [vamana.md](../../../concepts/vamana.md) · [bali.md](../../../concepts/bali.md)*

**Vāmana (5), the dwarf** — the first descent in human form, and the one that works **the opposite way**.

The Purāṇic story: the generous asura king **Bali** has conquered the three worlds; Viṣṇu appears at Bali's sacrifice as a **dwarf brahmin** and begs only **three paces of land**. Bali grants it, against his guru **Śukra's** warning. Vāmana then swells to cosmic size — **Trivikrama**, "of the three strides" — covering the **earth** with one step and the **heavens** with the second. With nowhere left, **Bali offers his own head** for the third, and is pressed down to rule the netherworld; the three worlds are restored to **Indra** (Ch. 18 §7.3 — the sky-god as beneficiary rather than agent).

**And here the Vedic connection is a genuine descent, not a takeover:**

| layer | content | source |
|---|---|---|
| Ṛgvedic | **Viṣṇu measures the three worlds in three strides** (*tri-vikrama*) — **no dwarf, no Bali** | Ṛgveda 1.154 |
| Purāṇic | the three strides become the **dwarf-and-Bali** narrative | Purāṇas |

This is **not** the Prajāpati→Viṣṇu reattribution. Here the avatāra **grows out of Viṣṇu's own Ṛgvedic act**: the three strides are Viṣṇu's defining Vedic deed, and the Purāṇic story is a narrative frame built around a hymn that was always his. As the sources put it, the Ṛgvedic three-strides passages are *"the nucleus out of which the legend of the Dwarf was created"* — while "neither Bali nor Vāmana appears in" the Vedic Trivikrama. **The continuity is real; the dwarf-frame is the accretion.**

So positions 1–5 contain **two different kinds of historical process**: three appropriations and one organic elaboration. That is the finding §8 builds on.

**Bali himself** is `contested`, on two separate axes, and he is one of the more remarkable figures in this corpus because **he is the tradition's paradigmatic *righteous* asura.** Grandson of **Prahlāda** (Narasiṃha's devotee), son of Virocana, of the Hiraṇyakaśipu lineage — and sources uniformly describe his reign, *even after dispossessing Indra*, as an age of peace and prosperity.

**The moral crux (converged):** once Bali recognises the dwarf as Viṣṇu **mid-gift**, Śukrācārya warns him to renege. **He completes the gift anyway.** Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.21 has him say he would rather lose everything than be regarded as a cheat. **Vow-keeping and truthfulness outrank self-preservation** — and that, not the cosmological mechanics of the expansion, is the philosophical content of the Bali material.

**Contested axis 1 — grace or punishment?**

| reading | commitment |
|---|---|
| **grace, not punishment** | Bali is sent to rule **Sutala**, a suffering-free realm built by Viśvakarman and **personally guarded by Viṣṇu**; promised future Indra-hood; counted among the seven ***cirañjīvī***. Dispossession *is* the liberation — stripping attachment from an already-virtuous soul |
| **grace, independently attested** | Mahābhārata/Harivaṃśa: Bali "was shorn of his sins and became immortal," granting the boon willingly and "well-pleased" — **textually independent of the Bhāgavata**, corroborating the grace-frame from outside the Purāṇic chain |
| **the older, harsher strand** | some tellings, surviving alongside the grace-frame, have Bali **literally dragged to Pātāla by Garuḍa** — a downfall, not an elevation |

**Contested axis 2 — what Onam means.** Bali is believed in Kerala to return annually on **Thiruvōṇam** to visit his former subjects. What the festival *means* is contested **scholarship**, not folklore trivia:

| reading | commitment |
|---|---|
| **devotional / allegorical** | Viṣṇu's yearly grant of homecoming to a beloved virtuous king; read by some devotional writers as "the self who gained the Divine" |
| **Dravidian / Dalit-historiographical** | Deva/Asura read as an Āryan/Draviḍa proxy pair; Onam folk-songs read as **resistance literature** against a Brahmanical caste narrative; one scholarly strand reads the myth as an allegory for the **suppression of Buddhism in Kerala** by Vaiṣṇava Brahmanism |

The concept file adds a fact that should be part of how the second reading is weighed: **Bali entered mainstream academic attention specifically *through* Dalit historiography**, not through royal or Brahmanical patronage. Who studied a figure, and why, is part of that figure's evidential record.

*And an etymological restraint worth noting:* बलि (*bali*) is independently Ṛgvedic-attested meaning "offering, gift, oblation," later extending to tax and tribute. **Whether the king's name derives from the common noun, or the reverse, is not confirmed by any source consulted** — recorded as plausible but unverified, not asserted.

### 6.3 Narasiṃha — the theology of the loophole {#narasimha}

*→ concept file: [narasimha.md](../../../concepts/narasimha.md)*

**Narasiṃha (4), the man-lion** is the tightest-converged narrative in this chapter, and its content is a single structural idea executed five times.

The asura king **Hiraṇyakaśipu** (brother of Varāha's Hiraṇyākṣa — the two form a paired **Hiraṇya-cycle**) wins from Brahmā a boon that he cannot be killed *by man or beast, by deva, indoors or outdoors, by day or by night, on the ground or in the sky, by any weapon.* To protect the boy-devotee **Prahlāda** — his own son, who insists Viṣṇu is everywhere, **even in a palace pillar** — Viṣṇu bursts from the pillar as Narasiṃha and **satisfies every clause by contradiction**:

| boon-clause | how it is voided |
|---|---|
| not by man or beast | he is **neither** — a man-lion |
| not by day or night | at **twilight** (*saṃdhyā*) |
| not indoors or outdoors | on the **threshold** |
| not on ground or in sky | on his **lap** |
| not by any weapon | with his **claws** |

**This is the theology of the liminal: God acts precisely in the categorial gaps the boon left open.** The demon's error was not asking for too little protection — he asked for a great deal — but assuming his categories were **exhaustive**. Every clause is a binary, and Narasiṃha is the excluded middle of each one, five times over.

The narrative's stability is itself notable: the loophole structure is reproduced **identically across independent sources**, including devotional retellings that share no textual lineage. When a story's *logical armature* travels unchanged while its details vary, the armature is what the tradition thinks the story is.

### 6.4 Paraśurāma — the descent that meets the next descent {#parashurama}

*→ concept file: [parashurama.md](../../../concepts/parashurama.md)*

**Paraśurāma (6), "Rāma-of-the-axe"** is the **first fully human incarnation** and, uniquely among the ten, **overlaps in time with the seventh** — he and **Rāma** meet directly.

Born to the sage **Jamadagni** and **Reṇukā** in the Bhṛgu lineage, given an axe by **Śiva** after ascetic practice. The pivotal cycle: the kṣatriya king **Kārtavīryārjuna** steals the family's wish-granting cow and kills Jamadagni; Paraśurāma kills him in retaliation and then, in a **wider purge, exterminates the kṣatriya class twenty-one times over**, filling five lakes with blood at **Samanta-pañcaka** near Kurukṣetra. He is a ***cirañjīvī*** who retires to Mount Mahendra rather than dying, and in the Mahābhārata he is **martial guru to Bhīṣma, Droṇa, and Karṇa** — three of the epic's central warriors trained by an avatāra.

**The encounter with Rāma** (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa sargas 74–76): after Rāma breaks Śiva's bow at Sītā's *svayaṃvara*, Paraśurāma intercepts him and challenges him to string **Viṣṇu's own bow**; on Rāma's success he **yields his residual Viṣṇu-*tejas***. The tradition reads this as the moment the **avatāric charge passes from the sixth descent to the seventh** — a handover scene, staged inside the text, between two forms of the same God.

That is worth pausing on given §6.1. A list assembled partly from other gods' cosmic animals nonetheless generated, at positions six and seven, a narrative in which the descents **acknowledge each other and transfer their power in sequence.** The theology caught up with the list and made it continuous from inside.

*A sourcing caution the file states plainly:* the pass relied substantially on **tertiary encyclopedic summaries and popular retellings** for details like the Śiva-axe penance and the earth-gifted-to-Kaśyapa motif, rather than on a critical edition or an academic translation (van Buitenen, Debroy) of the Mahābhārata passages — **noted as a gap for a future upgrade pass rather than smoothed over.**

### 6.5 Kalki — a prophecy in two confidence tiers {#kalki}

*→ concept file: [kalki.md](../../../concepts/kalki.md)*

**Kalki (10)** is the **future** avatāra — the not-yet-arrived descent who will appear at the close of the current **Kali Yuga** to end it and inaugurate a new **Satya Yuga**. His ordinal position is **stable across every list found**, even where positions 8–9 vary.

**And the concept file does something this chapter wants to highlight as method: it splits the entry into two confidence tiers rather than averaging them.**

| tier | content | attestation |
|---|---|---|
| **high** | tenth/future avatāra; ends the Kali Yuga; **white horse, sword**; destroys the wicked; restores dharma | **Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 3.188–189** onward — the converged core |
| **medium** | born to **Viṣṇuyaśas** and **Sumatī** in **Śambhala** village; horse named **Devadatta**; training under Paraśurāma; gifts from Śiva; marriages; wars against Koka and Vikoka | Viṣṇu Purāṇa → Bhāgavata Purāṇa → **Kalki Purāṇa** — **a genealogical chain of textual elaboration, not three independent witnesses** |

That second row is the charter's §4 trap caught in the act. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa supplies the parentage and village; the Bhāgavata **adds** the mother's name Sumatī, absent from the Viṣṇu Purāṇa passage; the **Kalki Purāṇa** — a late dedicated text, the tradition dated **1500–1700 CE** with a Dhaka manuscript fixing a floruit of **1726 CE** — massively expands the biography. Three texts agreeing here is **one text, elaborated twice**. The file also flags that this research pass **could not independently confirm** the village and parentage are present in the Mahābhārata itself rather than read back into it from later paraphrase.

**Contested points, mapped:**

| point | divergence |
|---|---|
| etymology | *kalka* ("filth, sin") + destroyer, **vs.** a root tied to "white horse" — disputed within Sanskrit lexicography, unresolved |
| who ends the Kali Yuga | most Purāṇas: Kalki. **Vāyu Purāṇa 2.36–37 assigns it to a different figure, *Pramīti*** |
| systematisation | some texts (e.g. the **Yuga Purāṇa**) **omit Kalki entirely**; no single scheme unifies the sources |

> ⚠️ **Cross-tradition, typed honestly and not linked.** The Buddhist **Kālacakra Tantra** names a line of righteous Śambhala-based kings "Kalki" (culminating in Rudra Cakrin), and **Donald Lopez** states the Buddhist usage **"likely borrowed [the term] from Hindu Vedic texts"** — a vocabulary borrowing, not an independent coinage. Separately, **Maitreya**, the future Buddha, is a *structurally* comparable eschatological-saviour figure in comparative-religion scholarship — but **no Maitreya node exists in this repository**, so **no edge is drawn**; it is recorded as a candidate for a future `structurally-parallel-to` once that node is written. Neither comparison licenses treating Kalki and any Buddhist figure as the same figure.

## 7. What the cycle is doing {#structure}

Four observations the material earns, in increasing order of consequence.

**First: the resolution is a trick, not a victory.** Devas and asuras cooperate to produce the nectar; the gods get it by **disguise** (§4.1). Cosmic order is restored by deception in a story the tradition tells about itself without embarrassment. Set beside Ch. 18's **Govardhana** (Kṛṣṇa argues Indra out of his own cult) and Vāmana's three paces (§6.2), and a pattern appears: **the Vaiṣṇava tradition's characteristic mode of divine action is stratagem** — the dwarf who asks for three steps, the enchantress who offers to serve, the man-lion who is the excluded middle of five binaries. Compare the Śaiva mode in §2.1: Śiva simply drinks the poison. Guile against endurance.

**Second: the cycle is a narrative *generator*, not an episode.** §3 traces the horse's tail to the Rāmāyaṇa's vultures through five separate written nodes. Rāhu and Ketu leave the story and enter **jyotiṣa** as two of the nine grahas (§4.2). Kāmadhenu leaves it and becomes the theological archetype behind the sanctity of every living cow (Ch. 18 §7.4). Lakṣmī leaves it and becomes half a devotional family. The churning is where a great deal of later Hinduism is issued from — which is why a corpus that had nodes for twenty-eight of its pieces needed a chapter to hold them.

**Third — and this is the chapter's main finding — the daśāvatāra is not one kind of thing.** Positions 1–5 alone contain:

- **three reattributions** — Matsya, Kūrma, Varāha: Vedic cosmic animals of **Prajāpati/Brahmā**, transferred to Viṣṇu as his cult rose, with the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa **caught mid-harmonisation** at 14.1.2;
- **one organic elaboration** — Vāmana: a narrative frame grown around **Viṣṇu's own** Ṛgvedic three strides;
- **one apparently Purāṇic composition** — Narasiṃha, whose Vedic traces are allusive and whose developed narrative is a self-contained logical construction.

Plus, further down, a descent that **meets and empowers the next** (§6.4), a **future** descent whose biography is a chain of elaboration (§6.5), a **contested member** at position nine, and a female form nobody can classify (§4.1).

**The list is a Purāṇic-era synthesis over heterogeneous material, and it does not become less interesting for being one.** It becomes more interesting: you can watch a theology assemble itself out of inherited pieces, and then — at positions six and seven — write a scene in which the pieces hand power to one another and thereby claim to have always been continuous.

**Fourth: the honest comparative claims here are narrower than the exciting ones, and both are worth having.** This chapter contains one **true cognate** (*amṛta* / *ambrosia* < PIE *\*n̥-mr̥-tós*), one **false but theologically productive folk etymology** (*surā* → *sura*/*asura*, against the real *\*Hásuras*, §2.4), one **structural hypothesis** (Dumézil's ambrosia cycle, Oosten's Norse parallels), one **borrowing** (the Kālacakra's "Kalki"), one **coexistence mistaken for identity** (the eclipse myth beside *siddhānta* astronomy, §4.2), and one **modern overlay presented as ancient** (the Kumbha Melā etiology, §5). Six different relations between two things that look alike — and the work of this chapter, and of this project, is mostly telling them apart.

| **Matsya / Varāha: the Vedic animal ≠ the avatāra** | same narrative, same animal | the fish is **no deity** in the ŚB, **Brahmā** in the epic, **Viṣṇu** in the Purāṇas — a documented three-stage shift |

| **surā → sura/asura ≠ the real etymology** | stated as a derivation **inside** the Rāmāyaṇa | historical linguistics gives **PIE/PIIr *\*Hásuras*, "lord"** — applied to gods, incl. Varuṇa, in the early Ṛgveda; **unconnected to wine** |
| **amṛta / ambrosia — a TRUE cognate** *(not a flag)* | — | both < **PIE *\*n̥-mr̥-tós***, "un-dying"; a real shared inheritance, unlike the story-shape comparisons beside it |

| **Buddhist *amata* ≠ the nectar** | same word | Buddhist *amata* = **"the deathless,"** an epithet of nibbāna — a state, not a substance |
| **Kālacakra's "Kalki" ≠ the Hindu Kalki** | identical term, Śambhala in both | a **borrowing** from Hindu texts (Lopez), not an independent parallel figure |
| **Śeṣa's "remainder" ≠ a conservation law** | "alone remains at the annihilation of everything" | a **doctrine of the cosmic remnant**, recorded precisely and **not modernised** |
| **Mohinī: avatāra or *māyā*?** *(unresolved)* | she does what avatāras do | the sources genuinely divide; **function stable, label contested** |
| **Bali: grace or punishment?** *(contested)* | Sutala is described as a reward | a third, **older harsher strand** has Garuḍa drag him down; both survive |
