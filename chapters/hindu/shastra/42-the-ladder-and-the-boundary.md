# Chapter 42 — The Ladder and the Boundary: Pāṇini's Commentators and the Size of a Text

## How to use this chapter

Chapter 40 built the top of the Pāṇinian tradition — Pāṇini, Kātyāyana, Patañjali — and immediately discovered it was standing on something it had not written. Its §6.4 sketched a stack five tiers deep and named the upper tiers only in passing. Its §2 leaned on a maxim of Kaiyaṭa's that the batch **could not verify**. Its §7 gave Kautsa's attack on the meaningfulness of the Veda and admitted the corpus did not have Yāska's answer.

This chapter is the tier below. It has one governing question, and it is not the question the batch queued:

> **Who decides how big a text is?**
>
> Not how good, not how old — how *big*. How many rules the Aṣṭādhyāyī has. How many Vārttikas Kātyāyana wrote. How many men were called Vararuci. In every case the answer turns out to be **a decision made by a commentator**, not a fact recovered from a manuscript — and in every case the decision is invisible in the number that results.

Four things to carry out:

1. **The ladder has a ceiling its own builder installed.** Kaiyaṭa's maxim of ascending authority stops at Patañjali — one tier *below* Kaiyaṭa. Chapter 40 said it warranted the climb. It does not. (§2)
2. **Two commentaries a millennium apart are jointly cited as fixing the size of Pāṇini's text** — and the same authority that fixes it also shrinks it, by reclassifying eleven lines. (§4)
3. **The same operation runs at both ends of the tradition.** Patañjali reclassifies sūtras out of the Aṣṭādhyāyī; Nāgeśa, seventeen centuries later, reclassifies Vārttikas out of Kātyāyana. Neither discovers anything. Both change a number everyone repeats. (§5)
4. **Yāska's answer to Kautsa, at last** — and its strategy is the opposite of what a defence of scripture is expected to be. (§7)

⚠ **One methodological warning up front, because this chapter's sources punish carelessness.** Batch 48 caught **four separate chronology failures** in general reference sources and **one in a specialist one** — and then caught **one in this corpus's own follow-up list**. §8 collects them, because the pattern is the chapter's real subject.

## §1 What a commentarial tier is

Chapter 40 §6.4 set the stack out:

> Pāṇini's **sūtras** → Kātyāyana's **Vārttikas** → Patañjali's **Mahābhāṣya** → Kaiyaṭa's ***Pradīpa*** → Nāgeśa's ***Uddyota***

Each tier is a commentary on the one below. Nāgeśa, writing around 1700, is producing a commentary on a commentary on a commentary on a commentary on a rule-book. And the ladder does not stop at five: Nāgeśa's own pupil **Vaidyanātha Pāyaguṇḍe** wrote the *Bhāvaprakāśikā*, the *Cidasthimālā*, and a *Kāśikā* on three separate works of his teacher's — **a sixth tier, built in one generation.**

It is easy to read this as decline. Chapter 40 §2.2 argued it was not, on the strength of Kaiyaṭa's rule. That argument now has to be withdrawn, and the reason is more interesting than the argument was.

### §1.1 The Kāśikā, and what a "full" commentary means

Before the ladder, one lateral fact that reframes it.

Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* — the tradition's most authoritative commentary, the work whose author Kaiyaṭa's maxim ranks **above Pāṇini himself** — discusses **1,228 of some 3,981 sūtras**. Roughly **31%**. More than two-thirds of the Aṣṭādhyāyī is not in it.

The text that *does* cover all of Pāṇini is the ***Kāśikāvṛtti*** of **Jayāditya and Vāmana**, conventionally 7th century, described as "the only extant full-length commentary on the sūtras of the Aṣṭādhyāyī." It glosses every rule, in Pāṇini's own order.

And Abhyankar's description of it is deflating: it is "an excellent help to **beginners** to understand the sense of the pithy Sūtras of Pāṇini."

> ⭐ **So the only complete commentary on the greatest grammar of antiquity is a beginners' gloss, written six centuries after the authoritative one, which covers less than a third.** Neither of those facts is a criticism of either text. Together they are a fact about how a technical literature actually reaches its readers: **through the accessible thing, not the authoritative one.**

## §2 ⚠ The maxim, verified — and the ceiling Chapter 40 missed

Chapter 40 §2.1 recorded a maxim attributed to Kaiyaṭa: ***yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam*** — among the three sages, the **later** has the greater authority, so Patañjali outranks Kātyāyana, who outranks Pāṇini. It flagged the attribution as resting on **secondary report only**; the primary scholarship returned HTTP 403, and the formula is **not in Abhyankar** under *yathottara* — checked directly, and checked again in Batch 48 with the same result.

### §2.1 It is real, and now it has an address

Madhav Deshpande's 26th J. Gonda Lecture (2018), read at first hand, settles it:

> Kaiyaṭa's principle, *yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam* (**Pradīpa on Mahābhāṣya on P. 1.1.29; Vol. I: 217**), provides an ordered hierarchy between the three grammarian sages.

Wording correct, attribution correct, and the corpus can now cite the passage instead of the rumour.

### §2.2 ⚠ And the very next clause is a correction

Deshpande continues: the authority of the sages "increases successively, and **it effectively peaks in Patañjali**."

> ⭐ **The maxim ranks three men and stops.** It is not a general principle that later commentators outrank earlier ones. **Kaiyaṭa (tier 4) and Nāgeśa (tier 5) fall outside the scope of the rule Kaiyaṭa himself wrote.**

Chapter 40 §2.2 — "Why it is not decadence" — used the maxim to supply "the doctrinal warrant that makes the climb respectable rather than decadent," and `mahabhashya.md` said the same. **Both were wrong, and both are corrected.** The climb above Patañjali has **no doctrinal warrant at all**. Kaiyaṭa formulated a principle of ascending authority that stops one tier below himself.

This makes the parallel Chapter 40 drew with Navya-Nyāya *closer*, not weaker. There the corpus found that by the 17th century the live object of study is a commentary on a commentary, and called it a drift. Here the climb goes further — and the one explicit justification anybody offered turns out not to cover it. **In both literatures the climb is unlicensed. In one of them, someone wrote down a rule that looks like a licence and is not.**

### §2.3 The gloss that proves too much

Nāgeśa, commenting on the maxim at the same page of the same edition, rationalises it: ***uttarottarasya bahulakṣyadarśitvāt*** — the later authority ranks higher because he has observed **a wider range of actual usage**.

Deshpande immediately names the cost: "If this progressively wider vision is purely through chronological succession, **it does not explain why this process must end with Patañjali.**"

> ⭐ **By Nāgeśa's own reasoning, Nāgeśa should outrank Patañjali.** He has sixteen more centuries of Sanskrit to look at. No one in the tradition draws that conclusion, and Nāgeśa least of all: he refers to Pāṇini by name, to Kātyāyana as *vārttikakṛt*, and to Patañjali as ***bhagavān***.

**He supplies an empirical justification for a hierarchy and then defers to it on theological terms.**

### §2.4 Why the rule stops where it stops

Deshpande's explanation is the best thing in the lecture, and it turns on which story each commentator had heard.

- **Haradatta** — south Indian, Chola country, a Śaivite whose name means "given by Śiva" and whose *Padamañjarī* opens saluting Śiva — gives the **first attestation in the Pāṇinian tradition** of Pāṇini receiving the Śiva-sūtras from Maheśvara. He shows no knowledge of Patañjali as an incarnation of Śeṣa. **Result: three sages, complementary, no ranking.**
- **Kaiyaṭa** — Kashmiri, a Vaiṣṇava whose *Pradīpa* opens saluting Nārāyaṇa — shows **no** awareness of the Śiva revelation, but calls Patañjali ***Nāganātha***, which is first-hand evidence that he knew the Ādiśeṣa-incarnation motif. **Result: a hierarchy, with Patañjali at the apex.**

> ⭐⭐ **The tradition's central principle of textual authority may be a consequence of which incarnation myth reached which province.** And it answers Deshpande's own objection: **the ranking stops at Patañjali because the myth stops at Patañjali.** Nāgeśa's empirical gloss over-generates precisely because it has replaced a theological reason with a chronological one.

⚠ Deshpande hedges this causal claim with "perhaps," twice. The corpus records it with his hedge. But note what it does to Chapter 40's picture: that chapter, and `patanjali.md`, identified a **theological motor** behind the three-way Patañjali conflation — an avatar of Ādi Śeṣa, for whom mastering several sciences follows from the premise rather than confusing anyone. **The same motor turns out to drive the authority structure.**

## §3 Kaiyaṭa, and a date that behaves exactly as predicted

Batch 47 ended with a standing caution, and Batch 48 was its first real test:

> "Read at first hand" and "specialist" are strong marks for what a discipline knows **internally** — vocabulary, manuscript contents, which attributions the tradition itself doubts — and **weak** for anything depending on outside scholarship that has moved, **chronology above all.**

On Kaiyaṭa's date, three sources:

| source | what it says |
|---|---|
| **Abhyankar** (specialist, first hand) | "lived in the **11th century**" — flat, unargued, no range |
| Careful modern usage | "a **pre-thirteenth century** Kashmirian scholar" — a *terminus ante quem*, deliberately weaker |
| **Monier-Williams**, Apte | "son of Jaiyaṭa" — **no date at all** |

The specialist is not contradicted. He is **more confident than his evidence**. The caution predicted this before the source was opened.

⚠ **And a trap worth naming.** Searching "Kaiyaṭa" returns page after page agreeing that he was a resident of Kashmir whose father was Jaiyaṭa and whose *Pradīpa* is the light without which the Mahābhāṣya "would have remained unintelligible." That looks like overwhelming corroboration. **It is one source.** wisdomlib reproduces Abhyankar's entry verbatim under an explicit attribution, and the search summaries quote wisdomlib. **The apparent abundance is a single witness with an echo.**

## §4 Who decides how many rules Pāṇini wrote

Chapter 40 §4.1 gave five counts of the Aṣṭādhyāyī and concluded: *the text is stable and its boundary is not.* This chapter can now name who draws the boundary.

Abhyankar, read at first hand:

> The total number of Sūtras **as commented upon by the writers of the Kāśikā and the Siddhāntakaumudī is 3983**.

> ⭐ **A 7th-century beginners' gloss and a 17th-century re-ordering, cited together as one canon-fixing authority.** They are separated by a thousand years and belong to different genres. What makes them the arbiters is simply that they are the two commentaries that treat *every* rule — so what they gloss is what counts as a rule.

And the same sentence continues into the shrinkage:

> As **nine sūtras out of these are described as Vārttikas and two as Gaṇasūtras by Patañjali**…

Eleven lines that the tradition recites as Pāṇini's, the greatest commentator assigns elsewhere. Nothing was found; nothing was lost. **A genre label moved, and the text got smaller.**

⚠ **An arithmetic caveat, carried from `astadhyayi.md` and not smoothed over here:** 3,983 − 9 − 2 = 3,972, but the scan prints Patañjali's figure as 3,979. The two do not reconcile and it may be an OCR digit error in a two-column page. **The exact figure is not asserted — only the reason it is lower.**

## §5 The same move, seventeen centuries later

Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita (c. 1550–1630) settled in Varanasi before 1600, studied under **Śeṣa Kṛṣṇa**, and was supported by the Keladi rulers Veṅkaṭappa Nāyaka I and Vīrabhadra. His ***Siddhāntakaumudī*** re-arranges Pāṇini's sūtras "under appropriate heads."

**Why re-arrange at all?** Because Pāṇini groups rules by **operation**, so deriving a single word requires rules scattered across the whole book. The Siddhāntakaumudī collects them so that "all sūtras necessary for the achievement of a particular [derivation] are available at one place."

> ⭐ **The order in which Sanskrit grammar has been taught for four centuries is not Pāṇini's order.**

⚠ **And here the chapter declines an inference it would like to make.** Pāṇini's sequence is not merely an arrangement — rules interact *by* their sequence. Breaking it for pedagogy ought to make explicit metarules more necessary, and the tradition's monument to metarules is Nāgeśa's *Paribhāṣenduśekhara*, a century downstream. **That is a tidy story and no source consulted makes it.** It is recorded as a question. Tidy stories about ancient texts are what this project exists to resist.

### §5.1 Nāgeśa, and how much of Kātyāyana survives

Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa (fl. c. 1700) is where the tradition stops. Abhyankar's phrasing gives it away — "Patañjali and other reputed writers on Pāṇini's grammar **right on upto Nāgeśa of the 18th century**." Deshpande calls him "the last luminary."

Chapter 40 §6.1 already recorded what he did, and this chapter can now set it beside §4:

| who | what he reclassified | the effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Patañjali** (2nd c. BCE) | 9 sūtras → Vārttikas; 2 → Gaṇasūtras | the **Aṣṭādhyāyī** gets smaller |
| **Nāgeśa** (c. 1700) | Vārttikas into **two classes**, so that many lines the Mahābhāṣya presents as Vārttikas "will not strictly be termed as Vārttikas" | **Kātyāyana's corpus** falls from ~5,000 to ~1,400 |

> ⭐⭐ **The tradition's first great commentator and its last both resize a canon by reclassifying genre.** Neither discovers a manuscript. Both change a number that later handbooks repeat as a fact. And Abhyankar adds the detail that makes it a *textual* event rather than a scholastic one: "There are **some manuscript copies which give this reduced number**." **The definition propagated into the manuscripts.**

### §5.2 The family that ends the tradition

Assembling Batch 48's biographies gives one household and one pupil:

> **Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita** (c. 1550–1630) — brother **Raṅgojī** → son **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa** (early 17th c.); and Bhaṭṭoji's grandson **Haridīkṣita** (c. 1650) → pupil **Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa** (fl. c. 1700).

The Stanford Encyclopedia names **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa and Nāgeśabhaṭṭa** as the pair who **rejected Bhartṛhari's unitary sentence-meaning model**. They are an uncle's son and a grandson's pupil in one Varanasi family. ⚠ That explains how a position *travelled*; it says nothing about whether the position is right, and a shared root text — Bhaṭṭoji's own verse work — is a sufficient explanation without invoking kinship at all. **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa's own contribution is [Ch 43](../darsana/43-meaning-read-in.md)'s.**

### §5.3 Three abridgements

- Bhaṭṭoji's ***Prauḍhamanoramā*** is a commentary he wrote **on his own book** — and Abhyankar records that "on account of the difficult nature of it, it is usual to read [it] **upto the end of the Kāraka-prakaraṇa**."
- His disciple **Varadarāja** abridged the Siddhāntakaumudī into the *Laghusiddhāntakaumudī*.
- Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa's *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇasāra* is his own abridgement of his own *Bhūṣaṇa* — and it is the version everyone reads.

> ⭐ **The tradition records its own attrition point.** Students stop the self-commentary at the *kāraka* section. ⚠ Whether three abridgements in one family's output says something about this school or about Sanskrit pedagogy generally is **not established here.**

## §6 Vararuci: four men, and a lesson about entries

Chapter 40 §8.3 charged Abhyankar's dictionary with not checking itself across entries, using the Kātyāyana/Vararuci case: the *Vararuci* entry opens by calling the identification with Kātyāyana "very likely" and then supplies the evidence refuting it, while the *Kātyāyana* entry reports the identification without the refutation.

**Reading the whole entry, rather than its opening, changes the verdict.** Abhyankar's *Vararuci* entry is a **four-way disambiguation**, numbered by him:

| # | who | what he wrote |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | the figure identified with Kātyāyana | the Vārttikas — **on an identification Abhyankar then rejects** |
| (2) | a real, separate grammarian, "**Kātyāyana by Gotra name**," later than Patañjali | a **Prākṛt grammar**, and — with **Sarvavarman** — the **fourth adhyāya of the Kātantra** |
| (3) | "a comparatively modern grammarian" | a treatise on **genders**, ~125 stanzas, with a *Liṅgavṛtti* |
| (4) | "another modern grammarian" | a work **on syntax** |

His argument in sense (1) is from the earliest witness: "There is **no reference at all in the Mahābhāṣya to Vararuci as a writer of the Vārttikas**; there is only one reference which shows that there was a scholar by name Vararuci known to Patañjali, **but he was a poet**."

> ⭐ **The entry is not incoherent. It is an argument.** It states the received view, tests it against the primary text, rejects it, and then separates three further men. **Chapter 40 diagnosed the book from two-thirds of one entry.**

**What survives of the charge, and it is worth keeping:** the defect is the **missing cross-reference**. Enter the dictionary at *Kātyāyana* and you get the identification; enter at *Vararuci* and you get its refutation. Batch 46's generalisation — that reference works are unreliable **as a set of witnesses** — holds exactly as stated. The stronger claim, that the entry itself was self-contradictory, does not.

### §6.1 The mechanism, which the corpus did not have

Abhyankar's sense (2): "This Vararuci, who **also was [Kātyāyana] by Gotra name**, was a grammarian later than Patañjali."

> ⭐ **Two different men bore both names.** The merger needs no carelessness and no piety to explain it. Compare `patanjali.md`, where a **three-way** conflation has an identified theological motor. Here a **four-way** one has no motive at all — just a personal name and a gotra name colliding twice. **"Same name, therefore same man" does damage unaided.**

⚠ And Wikipedia's support for the identification is the *Kathāsaritsāgara* — the 11th-century tale-collection in which Kātyāyana is a reincarnation of Śiva's gaṇa Puṣpadanta. That is the very text **Abhyankar elsewhere calls "only legendary and hence, it has very little historical value."**

### §6.2 A gap this opens

Vararuci (2) wrote a **Prākṛt** grammar and a quarter of the **Kātantra** — a *non-Pāṇinian* system. And [Ch 43](../darsana/43-meaning-read-in.md) will meet the Buddhist **Cāndra-Vyākaraṇa** in the Kāśikā's background.

> ⚠ **Chapters 40–42 have treated "Sanskrit grammar" as effectively coextensive with the Pāṇinian tradition.** There were rival systems with their own sūtras and their own commentators, and this batch has now brushed against two. **The corpus has no node for any of them.** Named as a gap; not filled, under scope-lock.

## §7 Yāska's answer to Kautsa

Chapter 40 §7 gave Kautsa's attack — *the Vedic mantras have no meaning* — and had to leave it standing, because the relevant paper was paywalled. It still is. But **Lakshman Sarup's critical edition and English translation (Oxford, 1920–21) is public domain**, and the passage was read directly.

### §7.1 Six objections, six replies

| # | Kautsa (*Nirukta* 1.15) | Yāska (1.16) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Propositions **have their words fixed, their order immutably fixed** — formula, not speech | "it is **the same with regard to the everyday speech of the world**, as 'Indra and Agni', 'father and son'" |
| 2 | The **Brāhmaṇa**, not the mantra, enjoins the act | "a **mere reiteration** of what has been said already" |
| 3 | Their meaning is **impossible** — "Save him, O plant!"; while striking, "Do not injure him, O Axe!" | "no injury is to be inflicted, so it must be understood by the authority of the Vedic passage" |
| 4 | Their meaning is **contradictory** — "one Rudra and no second" vs. "Rudras… thousands without number" | "the same objection is applicable to the everyday speech of the world, as 'this Brahmana has no rival'" |
| 5 | They **enjoin someone already acquainted** | "in salutation a person announces his name to one who is already acquainted with it" |
| 6 | Their meaning is **obscure** — *āmyak*, *yādṛśmin*, *jārayāyi*, *kāṇukā* | "it is **not the fault of the post if the blind man does not see it**" |

### §7.2 What the shape of the defence shows

**Four of the six replies work by exhibiting the same feature in ordinary language.** And the whole answer opens on that principle: "Vedic stanzas are significant, **because their words are identical with those of the spoken language**."

> ⭐⭐ **Yāska defends the Veda's meaningfulness by assimilating it to ordinary speech, not by exalting it above ordinary speech.** The revealed text is saved by being shown to behave like everyone's language. That is a **linguistic** defence of scripture, and it is why this passage belongs to the history of linguistics and not only to the history of theology.

### §7.3 ⚠ One qualification the corpus adds to its own source

Sarup judges Yāska "altogether **free from fanaticism, bigotry, and intolerance**… but gives **rational answers** to the various points of objection." That is right for replies 1, 3, 4 and 5.

**Reply 6 is not an argument.** And it answers the objection hardest to answer: Sarup's *own footnotes* record that *āmyak*, *yādṛśmin*, *jārayāyi* and *kāṇukā* each **"occurs once only in the RV."**

> ⭐ **Kautsa's sixth argument is precisely about hapax legomena — words with no comparative evidence at all — and there Yāska changes the subject to the reader's eyesight.**

⚠ Recorded as this corpus's reading of the passage Sarup translated, **not** as a correction of his overall verdict, which concerns Yāska's conduct across the whole exchange and is otherwise well supported.

### §7.4 Two things Sarup notices that are better than his verdict

- **On why we have the objection at all:** "It is **inconceivable** that the learned theologians would reproduce, in their orthodox books, a controversy which challenges the most fundamental beliefs of their religion" — **and yet they transmitted it.** He draws it both ways: it shows such discussion was possible "with tolerance at that period," and it implies Kautsa was "an eminent scholar… whose thought could not be ignored." **The survival of the attack is itself the evidence.**
- **On whether Kautsa existed:** some hold Yāska invented him as a device. Sarup's counter is philological — Yāska uses *eke*, *ekam aparam* when attributing views **generically**, and "could have very well employed the same terms… had it not been associated with a particular individual."

⚠ And Sarup's apparatus records that Kautsa's six arguments track the **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā Sūtra i.2.31–38** at five points. **Two disciplines preserved the same sceptical argument and answered it in different idioms.** The corpus has read only one side; this is a lead, not a result.

## §8 ⚠ Six sources caught being wrong, and one of them is us

This chapter's method section, and the reason it is at the end rather than the beginning.

| # | source | failure |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Abhyankar** | Kaiyaṭa's date: a confident century where the evidence supports a bound (§3) |
| 2 | Wikipedia, *Siddhāntakaumudī* | "the author lived during **1700–1800**" — **~150 years off** — and an 1870 **print** date given as the work's date |
| 3 | Wikipedia, *Kāśikāvṛttī* | dates the text "c. the 7th century" and offers **no evidence at all** |
| 4 | **Hindupedia** | "The famous Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita was his grandfather," of Nāgeśa (§8.1) |
| 5 | search summaries | echoing Abhyankar and presenting it as corroboration (§3) |
| 6 | ⚠ **this corpus** | Batch 47's follow-up #2 (§8.2) |

**Five of the six are chronology or genealogy.** Batch 47's caution named chronology as the weak point of otherwise reliable sources. Five independent instances in one batch is no longer anecdote.

### §8.1 A relative clause that invents a bloodline

Three sources say **Nāgeśa was the pupil of Haridīkṣita, who was Bhaṭṭoji's grandson.** Hindupedia says Bhaṭṭoji was Nāgeśa's grandfather. The second is derivable from the first by one slip:

> *"Nāgeśabhaṭṭa was a pupil of Haridīkṣita, **the grandson of Bhaṭṭojidīkṣita**."*
> Drop the teacher, and the trailing appositive re-attaches to Nāgeśa.

> ⭐ **A relative clause changing which noun it modifies turns a teaching lineage into a blood one.**

⚠ **And the check reversed the blame.** A search summary produced the false genealogy first, and the obvious inference was that the *summariser* had misparsed. Fetching Hindupedia directly showed **the page itself asserts it.** The error is in a published source, not in the retrieval layer. **The corpus would have libelled the right party for the wrong reason.**

Hindupedia's own entry also names Nāgeśa's father as **Śiva Bhaṭṭa** — a man who appears in no account as Bhaṭṭoji's son. **The entry carries the material that sits badly with its own claim**, which is the Vararuci pattern of §6 exactly, now in a crowd-sourced encyclopedia rather than a scholarly dictionary. **The generalisation holds across both source types.**

### §8.2 ⚠ And the corpus does it too

Batch 47's follow-up #2 recorded, as a discovery, that Maṇḍana Miśra's *Sphoṭasiddhi* was "**absent from `mandana-mishra.md`**."

**It was not absent.** It is in that file's `source_text` front-matter, and its gloss says "He defended the *sphoṭa* theory of language (Sphoṭasiddhi)."

> ⭐ **A one-line grep would have closed the follow-up before it opened. Instead it was carried into a batch queue as a named test.** That is a failure of the charter's fourth signal — internal consistency, *"sweep with grep and check."*

**The charge this corpus has been levelling at reference works — that they do not check themselves across their own entries — applies to the corpus.** It is recorded here rather than quietly dropped, because a batch that reports only the tests it passes is not running tests. The *Sphoṭasiddhi* is treated properly in [Ch 43](../darsana/43-meaning-read-in.md) §3, on better grounds than the queue's.

## §9 ⚠ *Pratyāhāra*, and the prime-directive section

Chapter 40 §4.3 introduced the abbreviation device: the fourteen **Śivasūtras** list Sanskrit's sounds in a contrived order, each group closed by a mute marker, so that any contiguous run can be named by its first sound plus a closing marker. *aC* = all vowels; *haL* = all consonants. And Abhyankar's finding: **Pāṇini uses it constantly, names it never, defines it never.**

Batch 48 read the modern result on it at first hand — deliberately, rather than through summaries, because this is the single most inflatable claim in the grammar literature.

**Wiebke Petersen, *J. Logic, Language and Information* 13 (2004), Proposition 4.2: "Pāṇini's Śivasūtras form an optimal S-alphabet."** It is a real theorem with a real graph-theoretic proof, and its concrete core is this: among the pratyāhāras actually used in the Aṣṭādhyāyī there are **249 K5-triples**, every one containing **h** and no other segment common to all. So avoiding the duplication of *h* would require duplicating **more than one** segment. **The long-noticed oddity that *h* appears twice in the Śivasūtras is forced** — "necessary and, in fact, the best possible modification."

**Four limits, in the author's own words:**

1. **"Optimal" is a defined predicate**, not praise: minimal duplications and as few markers as possible (her Definition 2.5).
2. ⚠ **It does not mean shortest.** "Optimal S-alphabets **do not necessarily minimize the overall length**" — she gives a worked counter-example. **"Provably the shortest possible alphabet" is false, and it is the form the claim usually takes.**
3. **It is doubly relative** — to Pāṇini's own notation ("using his representation method") and to the classes his pratyāhāras pick out. It does not say his phonological classification is the right one.
4. **It is deliberately not phonology** — "a strictly set-theoretical point of view… does not explicitly take into account the phonological features of the segments."

> ⭐ **What this is: a modern proof of a property of an ancient list. Not evidence that Pāṇini had the mathematics.** He neither stated the proposition nor could have. **No formal-systems edge is drawn from this node**, on the precedent `avacchedaka.md` set and `karaka` and `sabdabrahman` followed in Batch 47.

**And what *is* earned, stated once.** The Śivasūtra ordering is **not arbitrary and not merely traditional**. It is demonstrably fitted to the classes the grammar needs, so tightly that a specific redundancy in it is forced. That is a substantial claim about a 4th-century-BCE text and it is fully supported — the more so because **Kiparsky (1991) reached the same conclusion by an entirely different route**, from the principle of economy internal to Pāṇini's own method. Two arguments sharing no premises, agreeing. ⚠ Kiparsky was not read; his position here is Petersen's report of it.

> **The distance between "his list has this property" and "he knew this property" is the whole of §0.**

## §10 What this chapter found

1. **The ladder's own rule installs a ceiling below the ladder's top.** Kaiyaṭa wrote a principle of ascending authority that peaks at Patañjali — one tier under himself. Chapter 40 said it licensed the climb; it does not, and the correction makes the parallel with Navya-Nyāya's drift closer rather than weaker. (§2)
2. **Size is decided, not discovered.** The Aṣṭādhyāyī's canonical count is what two commentaries a millennium apart happen to gloss; eleven of those lines Patañjali assigns away; and seventeen centuries later Nāgeśa reclassifies Kātyāyana from ~5,000 down to ~1,400 — into the manuscripts. **Nobody found anything.** (§4–§5)
3. **The order Sanskrit grammar is taught in is not Pāṇini's** — and the chapter refuses the tidy story about why that mattered. (§5)
4. **Four Vararucis, and no motive needed.** A personal name and a gotra name colliding twice explains a conflation that the corpus's other great name-collision needed a theology to explain. And Chapter 40's charge against Abhyankar is narrowed to the true one: a missing cross-reference. (§6)
5. **Yāska defends scripture by making it ordinary** — and his one non-argument answers the one objection about words with no evidence behind them. (§7)
6. **Six sources caught being wrong, five of them on chronology or genealogy, and the sixth is us.** (§8)
7. **A theorem about Pāṇini's alphabet is real, and says less than it is usually made to say.** (§9)
