## How to use this chapter

Ch 23 introduced Āyurveda and ended by naming its own gap:

> "*`vagbhata` and `dhatu`/`mala` are **unwritten concept nodes**… If they are written, this chapter should be revisited — the third classical authority is currently a hole in it.*"

This is that revisiting. Ch 23 covered the **doṣa** — what regulates the body. This chapter covers the other two members of the classical triad: **dhātu**, what the body is made of, and **mala**, what it throws away. Plus the thing that turns one into the other: **agni**.

Together, *doṣa · dhātu · mala* are the three categories into which classical Āyurveda analyses a person. A reader who has only the first has the famous part and not the system.

## 1. Two fires, one word {#twofires}

The corpus holds **two** nodes spelled *agni*, and the split is deliberate (charter §8).

| | `agni` | `agni-ayurveda` |
|---|---|---|
| what it is | the **Vedic fire-deity** of Ṛgveda 1.1, who carries the offering to the gods; *ṛtasya gopā*, guardian of *ṛta* in the ritual sphere | "**the entity that is responsible for all digestive and metabolic processes in the human beings**" |
| where | Ch 18/19; Ch 30's companion material on *ṛta* | this chapter |

**The continuity is real** and the corpus says so: Āyurveda's agni is a fire that *cooks an offering into something usable*, and the tradition is aware of the resonance. The typed edge between them is the charter's sanctioned pair — `shares-vocabulary-with` **and** `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent`.

**But a body's digestive capacity is not a god**, and keeping the nodes separate stops the deity's prestige from silently underwriting the physiology. That is the whole point of a tradition-split: not to deny the link, but to make the reader see it as a link rather than an identity.

This is the same discipline Ch 23 applied to *prakṛti* — the Āyurvedic word for an individual's fixed doṣa-constitution, and the Sāṃkhya word for the primal cosmic matter. Popular literature blurs "your Ayurvedic constitution" into "cosmic Prakṛti" as though they were one concept at two scales. They are an individual medical typology and a metaphysical first principle of a different darśana.

## 2. Thirteen fires {#thirteen}

### 2.1 The classification

Two sources describe the same scheme at different resolutions — **three kinds, thirteen tokens**. That is granularity, not disagreement:

| category | count | acts on |
|---|---|---|
| ***jāṭharāgni*** | **1** | food in the digestive tract, converting it to absorbable material; located "between the stomach and large intestine in the *pittadharā kalā*" |
| ***bhūtāgni*** | **5** | *pārthiva, āpya, taijasa, vāyavya, nābhasa* — **one per mahābhūta**, processing the elemental constituents of what has been absorbed |
| ***dhātv-agni*** | **7** | one resident in each tissue, transforming incoming material into that tissue and passing the rest on |

*Jāṭharāgni* is the master; the tradition holds that the other twelve depend on it, which is why almost all Āyurvedic dietetics addresses the first of the thirteen.

Note where the **pañca-mahābhūta** scheme enters: at the level of *absorption*, one fire per element. Ch 16 records the same five elements doing service in Vaiśeṣika ontology and Ch 23 records each doṣa as a pairing of two of them. Here they index a set of metabolic operations. **One classificatory scheme, three quite different jobs across three darśanas** — which is a fact about how Indian systems share vocabulary, not evidence that they share doctrine.

### 2.2 The four states — where agni becomes a clinical instrument

Digestive fire is not simply strong or weak. It has **four qualitatively different states**, each keyed to a doṣa:

| state | character | doṣa |
|---|---|---|
| ***samāgni*** | balanced, regular — "represents the normal state of metabolic fire and maintains the health of an individual" | equilibrium |
| ***viṣamāgni*** | **erratic** — irregular and sporadic; digests well one day and not the next | **vāta** |
| ***tīkṣṇāgni*** | **hyperactive** — too fast, with burning | **pitta** |
| ***mandāgni*** | **sluggish** — slow and weak, with accumulation of ***āma*** | **kapha** |

Two things make this more than a four-box table.

**First, it is genuinely non-linear.** A naïve "more fire is better" model has **one axis with a good end**. This one has **one good state and three distinct pathologies** — and crucially, one of the three is not a *quantity* at all. *Viṣamāgni* is **instability**: not too much, not too little, but *unreliable*. A model that can distinguish "too fast," "too slow" and "erratic" as three different disorders is doing something a one-dimensional model cannot do.

**Second, the states are constitutional.** "The *prakṛti* in every individual has a specific state of agni that gets fixed at the time of birth itself." So agni-state joins doṣa-constitution in a person's baseline typology, and treatment aims at *their* norm, not a universal one.

### 2.3 Āma, and the causal claim

***Āma*** is the unprocessed residue left when *mandāgni* fails to cook what it is given, and it is the tradition's general mechanism of disease-formation. The seat of agni is the ***grahaṇī***, and "*grahaṇī-vyādhi* is disease due to abnormal agni." The Caraka Saṃhitā at **Cikitsā Sthāna 15/51** makes *mandāgni* "a predominant factor behind various ailments."

State that claim carefully, because the popular rendering wrecks it. **"Toxins" imports a modern picture the classical concept does not carry.** *Āma* is defined by its **relation to a process** — it is *what agni did not finish* — and not by any chemistry. The claim is therefore:

> **A large class of disease is a failure of transformation, and the residue of that failure is itself the pathogen.**

That is a substantive and testable-in-principle etiology, stated in its own terms. It does not need to be biochemistry to be interesting, and calling it biochemistry makes it *less* interesting, because it replaces a claim about process-failure with a claim about substances.

## 3. Seven tissues, and an eighth {#dhatu}

***Dhātu*** — from √*dhā*, "to place, support" — is what food becomes. The order is not a list but a **production sequence**: each is formed *from* its predecessor.

| # | dhātu | conventional rendering |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ***rasa*** | plasma / lymph / chyle — "the substratum formed just after the digestion of food. The main function of this Dhātu is nourishment" |
| 2 | ***rakta*** | blood — "formed from the former Dhātu, Rasa Dhātu" |
| 3 | ***māṃsa*** | muscle — "the main function of covering the bones" |
| 4 | ***medas*** | fat |
| 5 | ***asthi*** | bone |
| 6 | ***majjā*** | marrow — bone **and** spinal |
| 7 | ***śukra*** | reproductive tissue |
| **(8)** | ***ojas*** | "known as the **eighth Dhātu, or Mahādhātu** (superior, or great dhātu)" — the refined residue once the seventh is fully nourished; the seat of immunity, strength and clarity |

⚠️ **The English renderings are labels, not equivalences.** *Rasa* is not "lymph"; *śukra* is not "semen" simpliciter; *majjā* covers what modern anatomy separates into two tissues. The corpus uses them to point and licenses no inference from them.

### 3.1 The word does other work elsewhere

*Dhātu* means a **verbal root** in Sanskrit grammar and an **element/sphere** in Buddhist Abhidharma. Same word, three technical senses in three disciplines. The corpus stores a non-equivalence against the Buddhist sense specifically, because that one invites a real confusion: both are analyses of what a person is made of, and

> the Āyurvedic dhātus are **substantial tissues in a nourishment sequence**; the Buddhist *dhātus* and *skandhas* are **momentary constituents in an argument against a self**.

One list is built to explain how a body is *sustained*; the other to show there is nobody there. They could hardly be doing more different work.

### 3.2 The rate relation

This is the sentence to carry away from the chapter:

> "The small portions of agni are present in each dhātu. **The increase or decrease of these agni leads to depletion or overproduction of dhātu respectively**" — A. Hṛ. Sūtra Sthāna 11/24

**The relation is inverse.** More tissue-fire yields *less* tissue, because the fire consumes what it processes. Āyurveda is not treating agni as a good to be maximised; it is treating it as a **rate** in a system where either direction of deviation is a disorder. §6 makes this the chapter's thesis.

And agni is made the condition of everything downstream:

> "Sustenance of life, complexion, strength, health, nourishment, luster, **ojas**, teja and prāṇa depend on the status of agni" — Ca. Sa. Cikitsā Sthāna 15/3

## 4. Three models, held at once {#nyayas}

Here is the most interesting fact in the chapter, and it is a fact about **method** rather than content.

Classical Āyurveda did not settle on one mechanism of tissue nourishment. Three ***dhātu-poṣaṇa-nyāya*s** ("maxims of tissue-nourishment") are transmitted, credited in the standard account to **Cakrapāṇidatta**, Caraka's commentator. They are genuinely different theories:

| nyāya | image | claim | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***kṣīra-dadhi*** — milk and curd | milk → curd → buttermilk → butter → ghee → *ghṛta-manda* | **total conversion** (*sarvātma-pariṇāma*): each dhātu is *entirely* converted into the next | a strictly serial pipeline. Explains the sequence; struggles to explain why all seven persist at once |
| ***kedārī-kulyā*** — fields and canals | irrigation channels feeding successive paddy fields | **transmission**: one nutrient stream carried through channels, filling each tissue in turn | a distribution network — vessels, a pump, an order of supply. Explains simultaneity and locality; makes the **channels** (*srotas*) the thing that can fail |
| ***khale-kapota*** — threshing floor and pigeons | pigeons flying from a granary to their own homes, each taking its own grain | **selection**: each *dhātv-agni* draws from a common nutrient pool exactly what its own tissue requires | a demand-driven model with no queue at all. Tissue failure becomes a failure of **selection**, not of supply |

### 4.1 Why this is not a defect

It would be easy to file this as muddle. It is better read as a **tolerance for multiple models**, each doing explanatory work the others cannot:

- the first explains **why the order is fixed**;
- the second explains **why all seven exist simultaneously**, and gives you a locus of failure (blocked channels) that the first cannot;
- the third explains **why different tissues take different things from one intake**, which neither of the others addresses.

The tradition uses them as complementary and does not adjudicate. **That is closer to how a mature descriptive science handles a process it cannot yet resolve than to a doctrine with one answer** — and it is why the corpus files `dhatu` as `contested` while noting that the contest is internal and by design, not a scholarly disagreement about what the texts say.

### 4.2 The honest caveat

The three-nyāya scheme's attribution to **Cakrapāṇidatta** rests on **one derivative source**, a practitioner-facing site that carries no *śloka* citations and no attribution to Suśruta, Vāgbhaṭa or Ḍalhaṇa. The *nyāyas* themselves are attested across the Āyurvedic academic press. **The names are solid; the attribution is thin**, and the node says so.

## 5. Mala, and the two rows that complicate everything {#mala}

***Mala*** — from √*mṛj*, "that which is to be cleaned or eliminated" — is the waste side of the same transformation. The same *dhātv-agni* that yields a refined tissue yields a residue, and classical Āyurveda tracks both.

| class | members |
|---|---|
| ***śārīrika mala*** — the **tri-mala** | ***purīṣa*** (faeces) · ***mūtra*** (urine) · ***sveda*** (sweat) |
| ***dhātu-mala*** (*kiṭṭa*) | one per tissue — below |

### 5.1 The dhātu-malas

| dhātu | its mala |
|---|---|
| **rasa** | ***kapha*** |
| **rakta** | ***pitta*** |
| māṃsa | *kha-mala* — the excreta of the orifices (ear, nose) |
| medas | *sveda* |
| asthi | *keśa* and *loma* — scalp and body hair |
| majjā | *akṣi-tvac-viṭ-sneha* — the unctuousness of eye, skin and stool |
| śukra | (not specified in the source consulted) |

**Read the first two rows again.** *Kapha* is the residue of *rasa*; *pitta* is the residue of *rakta*. **Two of the three doṣas appear here as tissue wastes.**

That means the tidy picture most introductions give — three humours governing seven tissues — is, from this angle, **the wrong way round**: the doṣas that regulate the body are *simultaneously products of the body's own metabolism*.

The corpus records this as a **real internal complication**, not a contradiction. The classical texts operate both descriptions and do not treat them as rivals — the same tolerance §4 shows for the three nyāyas. And it records the loose end honestly: **the third doṣa, *vāta*, has no dhātu-mala row at all**, which is worth noting rather than explaining away, and is a specific thing to check on an upgrade pass.

### 5.2 Malas are not refuse

The tradition explicitly refuses to treat them as mere waste:

> "**A certain amount of waste products is needed for maintaining the proper functioning of the body.**"

Malas *support* (the *dhāraṇa* function) as well as leave. Sweat maintains skin; the unctuous residues keep surfaces working. Hence the clinical consequence that runs through Āyurvedic reasoning: **both excess and deficiency of a mala are pathological**, and a great deal of diagnosis is mala-examination — *purīṣa-parīkṣā*, *mūtra-parīkṣā*. That is why stool and urine inspection occupy the place in Āyurvedic examination that they do; it is not squeamishness overcome but doctrine applied.

### 5.3 One word, two registers

Outside medicine, *mala* means **dirt, impurity, moral or ritual taint** — the sense Jain and Vedic usage carries. The Āyurvedic sense is **technical and non-moralised**: a residue with a job, not a stain. The corpus stores that as a non-equivalence against *pāpa*, because the moralised reading is the natural one for anyone who meets the word elsewhere first.

## 6. The thesis: a theory of rates {#rates}

Put the three sections together and one claim falls out of all of them.

| where | the observation |
|---|---|
| §2.2 | agni has **four** states, of which one is *instability* — so the variable is not a quantity |
| §3.2 | more *dhātv-agni* yields **less** dhātu — so the variable is a **rate**, and it is inversely related to its own product |
| §5.2 | **both excess and deficiency** of a mala are pathological |
| Ch 23 | health is *sāmya*, doṣa-**equilibrium**, not doṣa-minimisation |

> **Classical Āyurveda is an equilibrium theory, not a vitalist one.** Nothing in the system is a good to be maximised. Every central variable has a correct value with a disorder on each side, and the disorders on the two sides are different disorders with different treatments.

This deserves saying plainly because the popular literature reads the tradition the other way — agni as a vital force to be *stoked*, ojas as an essence to be *accumulated*, doṣas as impurities to be *purged*. Every one of those is a one-directional reading of a two-directional variable, and every one is contradicted by a sourced classical statement quoted in this chapter.

The system's shape is: **intake is transformed by tissue-specific fires into a refined line and a waste line, and health is the correct ratio at every stage.**

## 7. What this chapter refused to say {#refused}

One of this chapter's better sources — a verse-referenced scholarly digitisation of the Caraka Saṃhitā, which supplied three of its citations — states, in the same paragraph as its sourced classification, that *jāṭharāgni*

> "is responsible for all amylolytic, proteolytic and lipolytic enzymes secreted by various exocrine glands."

**That is a modern identification, not a classical claim, and the corpus does not adopt it.** The site is used for its verse citations and its thirteen-fold division; its enzyme gloss is left where it was found.

This is charter §4's warning working exactly as designed: **a source can be reliable for a term and unreliable in the next clause.** *Take the term, leave the editorialising.*

It is worth naming here rather than in a footnote because **dhātu, dhātv-agni and āma are among the most aggressively over-translated concepts in the whole corpus** — competing for that title with *paramāṇu* (Ch 28) and *anekāntavāda* (Ch 02). The honest statement is:

> Āyurveda has **a sequential, fire-driven, tissue-specific theory of bodily construction from food, with an equilibrium criterion at every stage.** That is a substantive proto-physiology. Saying so does not require it to be biochemistry — and the moment it is called biochemistry, it becomes a bad biochemistry rather than a good physiology.

Compare Ch 28 §7, which records the *opposite* error: deflating an ancient system because it is not modern science. §0 restrains both. **Āyurveda is neither vindicated nor refuted by biochemistry; it is a different kind of theory, and the interesting work is describing what kind.**

## 8. Vāgbhaṭa, and Ch 23's gap {#vagbhata}

Ch 23 named its own hole: the third classical authority had no node. He has one now, and is primary-covered in **Ch 29 §5.3**, because his most instructive feature is a **transmission** problem. In brief:

- Two works: the ***Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha*** (prose, **150 chapters**) and the ***Aṣṭāṅgahṛdayasaṃhitā*** (verse, **7120 ślokas**, **120 chapters**).
- **The AH is the one that travelled** — into **Tibetan, Arabic and Persian**. Indian medicine entered the Tibetan *gso ba rig pa* and the Islamicate medical literature through *this* text rather than through Caraka or Suśruta. **Concision travels.**
- Whether one man wrote both is unresolved: the traditional answer is an Elder and a Junior Vāgbhaṭa, grandfather and grandson; the philological finding is only the **negative** one, that the two texts are "unlikely to be the work of a single author." Ch 29 §5.3 keeps those apart.

For this chapter the relevant point is smaller and concrete: **A. Hṛ. Sūtra Sthāna 11/24, the inverse tissue-fire relation of §3.2 — the single most important sentence here — is his.** The corpus was citing his text before it had a node for him.
