# Chapter 35 — Dating a Literature Without Dates: The Āyurvedic Commentators

> **Primary concepts:** `haricandra` · `jejjaṭa` · `gayadāsa` · `candraṭa` · `aruṇadatta` · `hemādri` · `indu`
> **Folder:** `hindu/shastra/` — the technical-śāstra track, with [Ch 23 (Āyurveda)](23-ayurveda.md) and [Ch 31 (The Body as a Rate)](31-the-ayurvedic-body.md).
> **Reads after:** [Ch 34, The Commentator](../../cross-tradition/34-the-commentator.md), which asked what a commentator *does*. This chapter asks a different question — **how we know when any of them lived** — and answers it from the one part of the corpus where the machinery is fully exposed.

---

## How to use this chapter

Ch 34 built a *vertical* picture: root text, bhāṣya, ṭīkā, storey upon storey, and four or five things a commentator does to the text beneath him. This chapter builds the *horizontal* one. Seven men, none of whom left a date, arranged in an order — and the whole argument for that order is **who quotes whom**.

That sounds like a technicality. It is not. It is the load-bearing method for an entire literature, and this chapter exists because Batch 45 finally obtained the reference work that does it properly — **G. Jan Meulenbeld's *A History of Indian Medical Literature*** (Groningen Oriental Studies 15, 1999–2002) — and found that the method has **rules**, that the rules are violable, and that this corpus had already broken one of them and repaired it.

Three things to carry out of the chapter:

1. **Citation-bracketing** — the technique, its five instances here, and exactly what it buys.
2. **Meulenbeld's rule**: *a disputed date cannot anchor another date* — and its inverse, which is more useful: *being quoted is a harder fact than being dated.*
3. **A fifth thing a commentator does**, which Ch 34's list of four did not contain, and which we can now catch in the act at a locatable verse: **edit**.

⚠ A standing warning for this chapter above all others. Every date below is a **bracket** — "between X and Y" — not a year. Where this chapter writes a century, read "the bracket falls mostly inside that century." The literature does not support more, and the temptation to firm it up is the single most common failure in the popular Āyurvedic literature that these nodes were written against.

## 1. Seven men and one base library {#seven}

Āyurveda's authority rests on three saṃhitās — [Caraka, Suśruta](23-ayurveda.md), and the *Aṣṭāṅga* pair ascribed to [Vāgbhaṭa](../../cross-tradition/29-transmission.md#vagbhata). Every figure in this chapter is a commentator on one of them, or on a commentator on one of them.

| # | who | on what | condition of the work |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Haricandra** (*Bhaṭṭāra(ka)haricandra*) | *Carakasaṃhitā* — the ***Carakanyāsa*** | **a small portion survives** |
| 2 | **Jejjaṭa** | *Carakasaṃhitā* (*Nirantarapadavyākhyā*) **and** Suśruta — the oldest known commentary on the latter | largely **lost** |
| 3 | **Gayadāsa** | Suśruta — the ***Nyāyacandrikā Pañjikā*** | **one section, in one manuscript** |
| 4 | **Candraṭa** | his father **Tisaṭa**'s *Cikitsākalikā*; plus the *Yogaratnasamuccaya* | survives |
| 5 | **Aruṇadatta** | *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* — the *Sarvāṅgasundarā* | survives; printed |
| 6 | **Hemādri** | *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* — the *Āyurvedarasāyana* | survives; printed beside Aruṇadatta |
| 7 | **Indu** | *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha* **and** *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* — the ***Śaśilekhā*** in two versions | survives, **but the printed text is judged unreliable** (§9) |

Look down the right-hand column. **Three of the seven works are substantially lost, and a fourth is printed in a form its own reference work distrusts.** That is the normal condition of this literature, not an accident of preservation, and it determines everything that follows: if you cannot read the texts, you date the men by what other people say about them.

⚠ **A correction to Ch 23.** That chapter's §"A third authority, and a gap in the graph" says Vāgbhaṭa "is not a written concept node" and that the corpus "covers a tradition whose third pillar it has not yet built." Its footer already records that `vagbhata`, `dhatu` and `mala` were closed in Batch 42. It is now further out of date: **the corpus holds a named commentator for all three saṃhitās and, for the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*, both of them.** The hole Ch 23 describes has been filled twice over.

## 2. Citation-bracketing {#bracketing}

Ch 34 §5 set out three ways to date a commentator: a **regnal anchor** (Cakrapāṇidatta, whose family served the Gauḍa king Nayapāla, r. 1040–1070); **lineage arithmetic** (Sarvajñātman, from Śaṅkara's death plus a pontificate plus a discipleship); and **citation-bracketing** (Ḍalhaṇa). The first two need an institution outside the texts — a dynasty, a monastic succession. The third needs nothing but the texts, and it is the only one available for most of the seven.

**The technique.** Find the latest author X quotes: X is later than that author. Find the earliest author who quotes X: X is earlier than that one. You now have a bracket. You never have a year.

Five instances, and it is worth seeing them together, because the differences between them are where the method's real character shows:

| figure | *terminus post quem* (he quotes…) | *terminus ante quem* (…is quoted by) | bracket |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Haricandra** | — | quoted by **Jejjaṭa**; and Cakrapāṇidatta and Niścalakara held he **preceded Vāgbhaṭa** | **before A.D. 600** |
| **Candraṭa** | **Ravigupta**'s *Siddhasāra* (c. 650) | **Cakrapāṇidatta** used Tisaṭa's work (c. 1050) | **650–1050**, narrowed to **900–1050** |
| **Ḍalhaṇa** | **Cakrapāṇidatta** (fl. 1060) | **Hemādri** | late 11th – **c. 1309** (see §3) |
| **Indu** | **Jejjaṭa**; the *Dhanvantarīyanighaṇṭu* (1000–1100) | **Niścala**'s *Ratnaprabhā* cites an *Indumatī* | **before the later 12th c.** — disputed, §8 |
| **Aruṇadatta** | — | **contradicted** by Hemādri, who quotes him by name | c. 1220 |

Note the last row. Aruṇadatta is dated by having been **argued with** — Hemādri quotes him in order to disagree (`*maireyo* … "*kharjurāsavaḥ*" *iti aruṇadattaḥ*`). Disagreement is a stronger witness than agreement, because a later writer might copy an earlier one silently, but he does not invent an opponent to name and refute. Ch 34 §5 recorded this as a fourth dating method; it belongs, properly, as a *special case of citation-bracketing in which the citation is hostile* — and therefore the most reliable kind.

### 2.1 What the bracket costs {#cost}

It yields a **range**, and the range is only as tight as the two authors bounding it. Worse, it is **transitive**: if you date A by B, and B by C, then any error in C propagates to A. An entire chronology built this way is a single connected structure, and pulling on one node moves the others.

**This corpus has now watched that happen twice, in opposite directions.**

## 3. When the bracket moved: Ḍalhaṇa's upper wall {#dalhana}

Ḍalhaṇa was dated in Batch 43 to "between the late 11th and the early 13th century," on the bracket above. The upper wall was "Hemādri (fl. 1260)."

Batch 44 wrote `hemadri` — a node created only because Ḍalhaṇa's file *cited* him — and the wall moved.

**1260 is the first year of Hemādri's recorded career, not the date of his medical work.** He describes himself in the *Caturvargacintāmaṇi* as in charge of the state records of **Mahādeva of Devagiri (r. 1260–71)**, and he was archivist and chief minister to Mahādeva's successor **Rāmacandra (r. 1271–1309)** — confirmed by a contemporary inscription and by Aufrecht's *Catalogus Catalogorum*. P. K. Gode is explicit: "Evidently the *Āyurvedarasāyana* must have been composed **between 1271–1309 A.D.**"

A *terminus ante quem* is only as late as the citing work. So Ḍalhaṇa's upper bound moves from "early 13th century" to **c. 1309** — a loosening of nearly a century, produced not by new evidence about Ḍalhaṇa but by looking properly at the man on the other end of the bracket.

⚠ **And the hole in it, which is stated rather than papered over.** *No source consulted names which of Hemādri's works quotes Ḍalhaṇa.* The revised bound rests on the inference that it must be the *Āyurvedarasāyana*, his only medical work. That is a good inference. It is not a citation.

**The lesson, and it is the chapter's first rule:** *a bracket is only as firm as its weakest end, and the weak end is usually the one nobody has examined.* Ḍalhaṇa's date was written down with confidence for years while the man forming its upper wall had no node at all.

## 4. When the bracket was refused: Meulenbeld's rule {#rule}

The second case runs the other way — and it is the more instructive, because the standard reference **declines to use a bracket it could have used.**

Dating Candraṭa, Meulenbeld notes that Candraṭa's own preface shows him acquainted with **Haricandra's and Jejjaṭa's** commentaries. That is a perfectly good *terminus post quem* — except for one thing:

> "**As Jejjaṭa's date is disputed**, it is preferable to depart from another fact in establishing the upper chronological limit."

He goes instead to Candraṭa's quotation of **Ravigupta's *Siddhasāra*** (c. 650), which nobody disputes, and builds the bracket on that.

**This independently vindicates a Batch 44 finding.** `jejjata.md` was marked **contested** for a specific reason: the three dates then on record for Jejjaṭa (9th c., 7th–8th c., 6th c.) turned out to be *three dates for Vāgbhaṭa* with a generation added, all descending from the single claim that Jejjaṭa was Vāgbhaṭa's pupil. Move the anchor and the whole spread moves. Meulenbeld — writing in 1999–2002, without any knowledge of this corpus — reaches the same verdict and draws the operational consequence:

> **Rule 1. A disputed date cannot anchor another date.**

Meulenbeld also supplies a **fourth** date for Jejjaṭa — the **seventh or eighth century** — and it is the first that does *not* run through Vāgbhaṭa, because he rejects the discipleship outright (§7).

### 4.1 The inverse, which is more useful {#inverse}

Here is the part that keeps Jejjaṭa useful rather than discarding him.

Jejjaṭa's own date is contested. But **Haricandra is placed before A.D. 600 partly because Jejjaṭa quotes him**, and **Indu's *terminus post quem* comes from citing Jejjaṭa**. Both of those still work. Why?

Because *being quoted* is a different kind of fact from *being dated*. That Jejjaṭa quotes Haricandra is a fact about two texts, verifiable by reading them, and entirely independent of when either man lived. It fixes an **order**, not a position.

> **Rule 2. A figure whose own date is contested can still order other figures around himself.**

This is the deep structure of the whole method, and it is worth stating plainly: **citation evidence is fundamentally ordinal, not cardinal.** It produces a sequence. Dates enter only where the sequence touches something externally anchored — a reign, an inscription, a Tibetan lexicon. Everything in between is interpolation.

Look back at the table in §2 with that in mind. **Every absolute year in it entered from outside the medical literature**: Nayapāla's reign for Cakrapāṇi, Mahādeva's and Rāmacandra's for Hemādri, Ravigupta's for Candraṭa's floor. The Āyurvedic commentators do not date themselves. They date *each other*, and the sequence is then pinned to the outside world at three or four points.

## 5. The fragment and the reputation {#fragment}

Now the second theme, and it follows from the first. If a literature is ordered by quotation, then **an author who is quoted a great deal is heavily attested even if his book is gone.**

**Haricandra** is the extreme case. Meulenbeld calls him "a very early, probably even the earliest, commentator on the *Carakasaṃhitā*." Of the *Carakanyāsa*, "a small portion has been preserved." And yet:

- **Candraṭa**, opening his own commentary: "As there exist commentators like **Haricandra, Jejjaṭa, and Sudhīra**, it is presumptuous for anyone else to comment on the āyurveda."
- **Hemādri**, in the introduction to the *Āyurvedarasāyana*, calls him **the model of the commentators on the *Carakasaṃhitā***.
- An anonymous verdict: "The ignorant one who attempts to understand Caraka's thought without Haricandra's commentary is like a man who wants to drink up the ocean."
- **Śrīdāsapaṇḍita** makes him the foremost authority on internal medicine; **Narahari** gives him the epithets *munitulya*, "equal to a sage," *nayavid*, and *sakalavaidyamanyatama*.

Roughly twenty-five later authors quote him — Aruṇadatta, Ḍalhaṇa, Hemādri, Indu, Jejjaṭa, Cakrapāṇidatta, Candraṭa, Niścalakara, Śivadāsasena, Śrīkaṇṭhadatta, Vijayarakṣita, Vopadeva, and more.

**We know his reputation in far more detail than we know his book.**

### 5.1 Praise is weak evidence; argument is strong {#argument}

⚠ But encomia are the *least* useful part of that dossier, and this chapter weights them low. A tradition that only praised Haricandra would be telling us he had become a name — a thing to invoke, like an ancestor. What tells us his *positions were still live* is that people **disagreed** with him:

- **Niścalakara and Śivadāsasena** are "sometimes critical of his opinions."
- **Indu often rejects his interpretations.**

And because they argued with him, two of his actual doctrines survive: he recognised **eighteen types of *kṣaya***, and distinguished a *gulma* in women caused by *ārtava* from a *gulma* caused by blood occurring in both sexes. Those are the only pieces of Haricandra's medicine this corpus holds — **and they reached us through his opponents.**

This is exactly the §2.1 point about hostile citation, generalised: **disagreement preserves content that agreement does not.** A writer who accepts your view absorbs it and stops naming you. A writer who rejects it must state it first.

### 5.2 Gayadāsa, and the arithmetic of partial survival {#gayadasa}

**Gayadāsa** (11th c.) is the same shape one notch better off, and his case makes the logic explicit:

| what | condition |
|---|---|
| the *Nyāyacandrikā* on the **Nidānasthāna** | extant — "known in **one manuscript**," and edited |
| the *Nyāyacandrikā* on **everything else** | **lost as a text** |
| yet quotations from Gayadāsa appear "throughout **all sections** of the *Suśrutasaṃhitā*, especially in Ḍalhaṇa's commentary" | proving it **covered the whole text** |

Read that table slowly. **The evidence for the commentary's extent and the evidence for its wording come from entirely different places.** We know how big the book was from one source and what it said from another, and neither could have told us both.

### 5.3 Which makes the compiler the hero {#archive}

Ch 34 treated **Ḍalhaṇa**'s *Nibandhasaṅgraha* — a *nibandha-saṅgraha* is literally a "collection of compilations" — as a commentator who legislates. Batch 44 closed the hole that node had carried: *which* earlier commentators does it preserve? At least **Jejjaṭa and Gayadāsa**.

That reframes the work entirely. The *Nibandhasaṅgraha*'s compiling habit stops looking like a want of originality and starts looking like **the reason a century of Suśruta scholarship is recoverable at all.**

> **Rule 3. In a literature transmitted by quotation, compilation is not a failure of originality. It is a survival strategy — and usually somebody else's survival.**

The same shape recurs outside medicine, which is how we know it is structural and not a quirk of Āyurveda. [Nāthamuni](../darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md), first of the Śrī Vaiṣṇava *munitraya*, lost both his own works; his *Nyāyatattva* is being reconstructed from citations, of which **Vedānta Deśika alone supplies forty-four**. And the thing Nāthamuni is actually famous for is a **compilation** — the four thousand Tamil verses of the Āḻvārs.

## 6. A fifth thing a commentator does: edit {#edit}

Ch 34 §4 listed four operations: supply a mechanism the root text left out; extend a category; make a doctrine relative to the discipline asking; split an axis the school had collapsed. Batch 44 added a fifth from the Vedānta side — **constitute** the position you claim to be explaining (Appayya on Śrīkaṇṭha).

The Āyurveda trio adds one more, and it is the least comfortable: **a commentator changes the text.**

Meulenbeld, on Indu:

> "Indu is one of the few commentators who **accept Vāgbhaṭa's syncretistic attitude and do not cloak the Buddhist elements** in his works."

The evidence is specific, locatable, and comparative:

| passage | **Indu** | **Aruṇadatta** and many editors |
|---|---|---|
| *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* **Ci. 19.98** | reads ***Jina*** and ***Jinasuta***, glossing Jina as **the Buddha**, Jinasuta as **Avalokiteśa** | the names are **suppressed** and **replaced by Śiva and Śivasuta** |
| **Tārā** | named, and "recognised as a Buddhist deity" | "passed over in silence" |
| A.h. Ci. **1.177**, *daivatapūjā* | names not only Hari and Hara but **Āryāvalokita, Āryatārā, Parṇaśabarī, Aparājitā** | — |
| A.s. U. **1.19** | argues **Ratnaketu** is a Buddhist *dhāraṇī*; also knows the *Māyūrī* | — |

**This is not an interpretive disagreement.** Two commentaries on one verse print **different deities**, and the printed tradition followed the substitution. The corpus holds both commentators as nodes, which is the only reason the difference is visible at all: had Batch 44 written Aruṇadatta and stopped, there would have been nothing to compare him against.

### 6.1 Three things this does *not* establish {#edit-limits}

The finding is important enough to be worth fencing carefully, and the nodes fence it in three places.

**(a) The direction is not secure.** It depends on which man is earlier. Meulenbeld places Indu **before the later 12th century**; **P. V. Sharma** makes him **13th-century and posterior to Aruṇadatta** (§8). On Sharma's chronology, Indu did not *preserve* what Aruṇadatta removed — he **restored** it. What survives either way is that the two commentaries print different deities at Ci. 19.98. *That* is the finding; its direction is not.

**(b) Indu is not thereby a Buddhist.** Meulenbeld suggests his religion "may have been a mixture of Hinduism and Buddhism" — he names **both Manu and the Buddha** as founders of a *hitopadeśaśāstra* — but adds: "on the other hand … the *maṅgala*s of his commentaries are of a **Hindu type**." A commentator who leaves Buddhist names standing may simply be **the one not editing**.

**(c) Nobody in this corpus has opened the verse.** The claim is Meulenbeld's report. Checking *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* Ci. 19.98 in a critical edition with apparatus is the cheapest high-value test this chapter leaves undone.

⚠ Even fenced this tightly, the consequence stands and it is large: **the transmitted text of a medical classic was religiously shaped by the people who explained it.** Ch 23 and Ch 31 read the saṃhitās as reasonably stable objects. On this evidence they are stable *as printed*, and what got printed passed through hands with commitments.

## 7. Two negative arguments, and why they are better than the positive ones {#negative}

The most impressive reasoning in this material is not used to establish anything. It is used to **refuse** things — and the refusals are better evidenced than most of the assertions.

### 7.1 An author cannot comment on someone who quotes him {#niscala}

Some held that Bhaṭṭārakahariścandra also wrote a commentary on the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*. **Niścalakara refuses the attribution** by observing that **Vāgbhaṭa quotes Haricandra**.

That is a complete argument in one clause, and it is pure citation-direction reasoning — the same instrument as §2, turned to demolition. A medieval commentator is doing textual criticism, and doing it correctly.

### 7.2 An author's complaint about his predecessors dates him {#indu-preface}

The best of the lot. A tradition "in particular current among the physicians of **Kerala**" holds that Vāgbhaṭa travelled to Kerala for the last part of his life accompanied by his favourite pupils **Indu and Jejjaṭa**; a salutatory verse in **Nīlamegha's *Tantrayuktivicāra*** calls them his disciples; and Indu's own references to Vāgbhaṭa as *ācārya* are cited in support.

Meulenbeld's three counters, in ascending force:

1. ***Ācārya* proves nothing** — "it is common practice among commentators to mention well-known ancient authorities in this way."
2. **Indu cites Jejjaṭa**, "which excludes his being a contemporary." A fellow-pupil is not a cited authority.
3. **The decisive one comes out of Indu's own preface.** Indu says, in the introduction to *both* versions of the *Śaśilekhā*, that **Vāgbhaṭa's words had become obscured by bad commentaries**, and that this is why he wrote a new one. Meulenbeld: "**At least one generation of commentators must therefore have preceded Indu.**"

**Indu refutes his own discipleship in the act of justifying his book.** You cannot be your master's direct pupil *and* the man cleaning up after a generation of bad commentators on your master. Nobody had to find a new document; the refutation was sitting in the preface of the work whose authorship was never in doubt.

> **Rule 4. Read the preface. An author's account of why his book was necessary is evidence about who came before him — and it is evidence he had no motive to fake.**

## 8. Where the reference work is itself contested {#contested}

It would be wrong to leave the impression that Meulenbeld settles things. On Indu he is one of two named positions:

| reading | who | on what |
|---|---|---|
| **before the later 12th century** | **Meulenbeld** | quotes Jejjaṭa (7th–8th c.) and the *Dhanvantarīyanighaṇṭu* (1000–1100); cited by Niścala's *Ratnaprabhā* |
| **13th century** | **P. V. Sharma** | Indu quotes the *Medinīkośa* (12th c.); a quotation by **Hemādri** indicates Indu is **posterior to Aruṇadatta**; Maheśvara's *Viśvaprakāśa* (early 12th c.) influenced him |

Neither is adopted in this corpus. And underneath the disagreement sits a problem that undercuts both:

> Meulenbeld on the printed *Śaśilekhā*: the editions "present a large number of passages between brackets, which probably indicate **interpolations**" — and "other parts, **not** placed between brackets by the editors, should **also** be regarded as not belonging to the original text, since they derive from works by authors who are definitely posterior to Indu."

**Every one of Sharma's three arguments is an argument from what the printed Śaśilekhā quotes.** If the printed Śaśilekhā contains material by authors later than Indu, then "Indu quotes the *Medinīkośa*" may mean only that *somebody* in the transmission did. The dating argument is built on the text whose reliability is in question.

Meulenbeld's own remedy is the right one and is left here as an upgrade path: **study the quotations *from* Indu in later commentaries**, which are outside the contaminated transmission.

## 9. Regional traditions and repeated names {#hazards}

Two recurring hazards, both of which this corpus has now met often enough to name.

### 9.1 A local tradition claiming a figure {#kerala}

The Kerala tradition makes **two** claims about Indu, and Meulenbeld rejects both:

- that he was **Vāgbhaṭa's direct pupil** (§7.2);
- that he "possessed a thorough knowledge of traditional practices prevalent in Kerala" — **N. S. Mooss**'s view, from editing the 119 *paribhāṣā* verses appended to the *Śaśilekhā*. Meulenbeld: "This view need not be endorsed, as Indu merely refers to practices adopted by **some physicians, without specifying a particular region**."

Both would place Indu in Kerala. Meulenbeld places him in **Kashmir**. The same Kerala tradition supplies the Vāgbhaṭa-and-two-pupils journey, of which Meulenbeld writes flatly: "these traditions are **not worthy of credit**."

**A regional tradition claiming a distinguished figure for its own lineage is a recognisable evidence-type**, with a recognisable signature: it explains a connection the texts do not show, and it always runs toward the tradition doing the claiming. Compare Ch 33 and, in the Vedānta track, the Śrī Vaiṣṇava succession-by-sign.

### 9.2 Namesake-collapse {#namesakes}

The corpus has now recorded this **four** times, and it is time to state it as a standing condition rather than a series of surprises:

| node | the collapse |
|---|---|
| `aruṇadatta` | **three** men of the name, merged by the popular literature into one polymath (Gode) |
| `haricandra` | at least **six** others — a prose writer in Bāṇa's *Harṣacarita*, a Jain Digambara poet "undoubtedly different," a Bāhlīka physician in Śyāmilaka's *Pādatāḍitaka*, an 1205 inscription-writer at Devagiri, and more — plus a *Vṛddhahariścandra* |
| `indu` | an **Indunighaṇṭu** lexicographer; and Das Gupta's hypothesis that Indu is **Indukara**, father of the Mādhava of the *Mādhavanidāna* — rejected by D. Ch. Bhattacharyya because Indukara and Mādhava were **Bengalis** and Indu a **Kashmiri** |
| `vācaspati-miśra` | **two** men five centuries apart, both Maithil, both working across Nyāya and Vedānta |

> **Rule 5. In this literature a distinguished name attracts unrelated bearers, and the merging is done by later summarisers, not by the sources.** The reference works separate them; the handbooks recombine them. Any date built on "X was the pupil of Y" inherits *both* men's identity problems — which is precisely what happened to Jejjaṭa (§4).

### 9.3 And one claim that simply failed {#candrata-premise}

**Candraṭa entered the work queue as "Vāgbhaṭa's grandson."** He is not, so far as the reference work can tell. That Vāgbhaṭa "appears as the father of Tisaṭa … in the **untrustworthy colophons** of a number of MSS" — and is in any case a *different* Vāgbhaṭa from the author of the *Saṃgraha* or *Hṛdaya*, making a fourth man of that name on top of the two `vagbhata.md` already records.

There is internal evidence against it too. Tisaṭa's *Cikitsākalikā* opens by praising Sūrya, the Aśvins, Dhanvantari, Suśruta and **his own father**, and names eight authorities as its sources. Meulenbeld's comment: "**The name of Vāgbhaṭa is conspicuously absent.**"

A man does not omit his own father from his list of authorities. What is actually known about Candraṭa is one sentence long: *"The only fact known is that Candraṭa was the son of Tisaṭa."*

## 10. An observation this chapter refuses to develop {#surya}

Haricandra's surviving *maṅgala* is addressed to **Sūrya**. Tisaṭa's *Cikitsākalikā* opens in praise of **Sūrya**, the Aśvins, Dhanvantari and Suśruta. And Candraṭa "followed his father's footsteps in being a **sun-worshipper**," on the evidence of the *Yogaratnasamuccaya*'s first verse.

Three authors in this chapter, one deity.

⚠ **It is recorded as an observation and explicitly not as a finding.** Sūrya is a conventional and unsurprising deity for a physician to invoke — the Aśvins, the divine physicians, and Dhanvantari stand beside him in Tisaṭa's own verse — and **no source consulted draws any connection between these three men.** Two of them are father and son, so the sample is really two, not three.

Constructing a "solar tradition of Āyurvedic commentators" out of three *maṅgala*s would be precisely the move [Ch 28](../../comparanda/28-modern-physics.md) anatomises: take a real textual regularity, supply a connective the sources do not, and present the result as a discovery. It is logged so that a reader with better sources can test it, **and for no other purpose.**

## 11. What this chapter rests on, and what it does not {#provenance}

⚠ **An unusual and important caveat.** Five of the seven nodes lean on **one scholar in one work**. Meulenbeld's *HIML* is the standard critical reference — exhaustively footnoted, weighing each witness individually, printing rival scholars' positions rather than suppressing them — and that is why it can carry this much. But **source-kind is not source-plurality**, and no claim in §§5–9 has been confirmed against a second modern scholar.

What makes the dependence tolerable rather than dangerous is that Meulenbeld **cites the medieval witnesses individually**: Candraṭa's introductory verses, Hemādri's preface to the *Āyurvedarasāyana*, Niścalakara's *Ratnaprabhā*, Maheśvara's *Viśvaprakāśa*, Nīlamegha's *Tantrayuktivicāra*, Indu's own prefaces. Those are checkable by someone else. We are relying on his reading of them, not on his authority.

**One external, non-Indian anchor is worth its own line.** **Hariścandra is listed among the medical authorities in the *Mahāvyutpatti***, the Sanskrit–Tibetan lexicon compiled by order of **Khri-lde sroṅ-bcan (A.D. 816–838)**; his Tibetan name there is **Seṅ-ge zla-ba**. That is a datable, state-sponsored, foreign attestation, and it is the only item in this chapter that comes from outside the Indian textual tradition entirely. It does not date Haricandra. **It dates his fame** — and tells us it had crossed the Himalaya by the early ninth century.

**Retrieval note, recorded because it cost two batches.** *HIML* is free at Internet Archive item `Meulenbeld-HIML`, but two earlier attempts failed on the literal spaces in the filenames. The working method: take `server` and `dir` from `https://archive.org/metadata/Meulenbeld-HIML`, then request from that host directly with `%20` — e.g. `https://ia801900.us.archive.org/3/items/Meulenbeld-HIML/HIML%201A%20_djvu.txt`. All five volumes (≈13.3 MB) retrieve this way.

**The three cheapest tests this chapter leaves undone:**

1. ***Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* Ci. 19.98** in a critical edition with apparatus — settles §6, or at least shows what the manuscripts read.
2. **The surviving portion of the *Carakanyāsa*** — the only route from Haricandra's reputation to his method.
3. **N. S. Mooss's edition and translation of Indu's 119 *paribhāṣā* verses** — already in English, already annotated, and the most accessible piece of Indu there is.

## 12. Six questions {#questions}

1. Why is Aruṇadatta's dating — by having been **contradicted** — stronger evidence than a dating by having been quoted approvingly?
2. Meulenbeld refuses to date Candraṭa off Jejjaṭa but is happy to date Haricandra off Jejjaṭa. Is that inconsistent? (§4.1)
3. Ḍalhaṇa's upper bound moved by nearly a century without any new evidence about Ḍalhaṇa. What changed, and what does that tell you about how to audit a bracket?
4. Gayadāsa's commentary is known to have covered the whole *Suśrutasaṃhitā*, but only one section survives. Which evidence establishes which fact, and could either source have established both?
5. If P. V. Sharma's 13th-century date for Indu is right, what happens to §6's reading of the Jina/Śiva substitution — and what part of the finding survives regardless?
6. Three of this chapter's seven authors invoke Sūrya. Write the strongest case you can that this means something; then say precisely why §10 declines to make it.
