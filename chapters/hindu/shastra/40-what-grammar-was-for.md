## How to use this chapter

For forty-six batches this corpus discussed grammar without having a node for it. Twenty concept files invoked "Pāṇini" or "vyākaraṇa"; `avacchedaka.md` recorded the absence in its own prose and **refused to paper it over with a loose edge**; `patanjali.md` wrote that a caution had to stay in prose "since a typed edge needs a target." Batch 47 supplied the targets.

What the batch found was not what it queued. It expected a tradition of formal analysis, and found one — but the more useful result is about **how a technical literature knows itself**, and it is uncomfortable:

> **The Aṣṭādhyāyī performs operations it never defines.** Its most famous device has a name Pāṇini never used. Its central syntactic term is introduced under a heading and left unexplained. The definitions everybody learns are the commentators', supplied centuries later — and the tradition, uniquely, **wrote down a rule saying the commentators outrank the author.**

Five things to carry out:

1. **What grammar was actually for** (§1) — a conservative motive, not a scientific one.
2. **An inverted hierarchy of authority** (§2), and why it is not decadence.
3. **A date that moved 350 years**, and what moved it (§3).
4. **The machinery** (§4–§5): a real metalanguage, five sūtra counts, and a level of representation between meaning and morphology.
5. **Whether the Veda means anything at all** (§7) — the objection the whole enterprise had to answer.

⚠ **One standing caution before starting.** This chapter leans heavily on **K. V. Abhyankar's *Dictionary of Sanskrit Grammar*** (Gaekwad's Oriental Series), obtained as a full working scan and read at first hand. It is the best source the batch had, and §8.2 records where it is *wrong* — because the batch's own reading habits needed correcting, not just its sources.

## §1 A conservative science

*Vyākaraṇa* does not mean "grammar." It means **"separation, distinction, discrimination, analysis, explanation"** — from *vi-ā-√kṛ*, "to take apart." The name states a method.

It is one of the **six vedāṅgas**, the ancillary sciences of the Veda, with *śikṣā* (phonetics), *chandas* (metre), *nirukta* (etymology), *kalpa* (ritual) and *jyotiṣa* (calendar). And its stated purpose is not discovery but **preservation**: to "prevent sloppy usage and transmission of the Vedic knowledge."

**This matters for everything downstream.** Grammar existed here because a text had to be recited correctly. The theory of meaning came later, and — as Ch 41 shows — was argued for against resistance. A reader who starts from "the Indians had a science of language" has the order backwards: **they had a preservation problem, and a science grew on it.**

### §1.1 Pāṇini was not the first

Abhyankar, reading Pāṇini's own text, counts **ten** earlier grammarians whom Pāṇini names: Āpiśali, Śākaṭāyana, Gārgya, Śākalya, Kāśyapa, Bharadvāja, Gālava, Cākravarmaṇa, Senaka and Sphoṭāyana. "The works of most these authors are **lost**."

So the founder of the tradition is **the earliest grammarian whose book survives, not the earliest grammarian** — a distinction this corpus has drawn before for `kapila` and `kanada`, and one that should be the default assumption rather than a discovery.

⚠ And the gap left by that loss was filled in the way Ch 39 catalogues. Tradition names **Indra** "the first grammarian of the Sanskrit language"; Abhyankar judges "it is very likely that Pāṇini had no grammar work of Indra before him." **A founder supplied where the record has none** — Ch 39's mechanism, in a literature Ch 39 never examined.

## §2 The *munitraya*, and a rule that inverts the usual hierarchy

The tradition's canon is three men — the ***munitraya***, "the triad of sages." Abhyankar:

> "the famous three ancient grammarians **Pāṇini** (the author of the Sūtras), **Kātyāyana** (the author of the Vārttikas), and **Patañjali** (the author of the Mahābhāṣya)"

His **second** sense is the one to notice. *Munitraya* also names **Pāṇini's grammar itself**, "being the contribution of the reputed triad of Grammarians."

> **The text is identified with its commentators.** The Aṣṭādhyāyī as actually studied is not Pāṇini's book. It is the three-author composite.

### §2.1 *Yathottaraṃ*: the later sage has the greater authority

The tradition drew the consequence explicitly. A maxim attributed to **Kaiyaṭa** (c. 11th c.) — ***yathottaraṃ hi munitrayasya prāmāṇyam*** — holds that among the three, **the later has the greater authority**. Patañjali outranks Kātyāyana, who outranks Pāṇini.

**This inverts the assumption a modern reader brings**: that a commentary is subordinate to its text, and that the earliest witness is the best. Here the rule runs the other way, and it is *stated*, not merely practised.

⚠ **Provenance, flagged rather than buried.** The *munitraya* is verified at first hand in Abhyankar. **The ranking is not.** The maxim reaches this corpus through secondary report only; the primary scholarship (*Evolution of the Notion of Authority (Prāmāṇya) in the Pāṇinian Tradition*) returned HTTP 403, and the formula is **not in Abhyankar** under *yathottara* — checked directly in the scan. It is load-bearing for this chapter and it is unverified. **Carried as a follow-up, and you should read §2 knowing that.**

### §2.2 Why it is not decadence

Batch 46 found, in a wholly unrelated literature, that Navya-Nyāya's object of study "migrates upward": by the 17th century the live text is a commentary on a commentary (`tattvacintamani`). There it looked like drift — a tradition losing contact with its roots.

**Here the same structure has a stated warrant, and two mundane causes:**

1. **Kātyāyana does not survive as a book.** The Vārttikas are known *because the Mahābhāṣya quotes them*. The later sage is literally the only route to the earlier one.
2. **Pāṇini defines almost nothing** (§8.1). If you want to know what a technical term means, the commentator is not an inferior source; he is the *only* source.

**A tradition whose root text is a set of operations without definitions has to locate authority downstream.** The maxim is not reverence run amok — it is an accurate description of where the information is.

## §3 One date, and how it moved 350 years

Almost nothing about Pāṇini is recoverable. Abhyankar is blunt: "Very little is known unfortunately about his native place, parentage or personal history. The account given about these in the *Kathāsaritsāgara* and other books is only **legendary** and hence, it has very little historical value."

The dating proposals span roughly the 7th to the mid-4th century BCE — and **they do not scatter randomly.**

| dating | who | what the argument rests on |
|---|---|---|
| 6th–7th c. BCE, "if not earlier" | **Abhyankar** | "the **internal evidence**, supplied by his work" |
| 5th or 6th c. BCE | Scharfe (1977) | "proximity to the language of the Upanishads" |
| no earlier than 400 BCE | Cardona (1997) | a **floor**, not a date |
| **mid-4th c. BCE** | **von Hinüber (1989); Falk (1993)** | **numismatic** — Pāṇini's rules name coins (*rūpya*, *niṣka*) datable to the 4th c. |
| c. 350 BCE | Houben (2009) | "based on **unrefuted concrete evidence**" |

> **The early datings are linguistic impressions of how old the language feels. The late ones are datable physical objects the text happens to name.**

These are not two readings of one evidence base. They are **two kinds of evidence**, and only one of them can be checked by someone outside the discipline. The corpus records the numismatic argument as the stronger, without pretending it is closed — Cardona's own caution is that the evidence supports a *floor*.

### §3.1 Two witnesses that look decisive and are not

- ***yavanānī*** (A 4.1.49), often read as "Greek script," which would date Pāṇini after Alexander. Cardona notes it "has been interpreted as referring to either to Old Persian cuneiform, Greek script, or Ionian women." **A word admitting three readings dates nothing.**
- **The Achaemenid setting.** Pāṇini lived in Gandhāra under nominal Persian rule — but his "work shows **no influence from Old Persian**." The geography is real; the inference from it is not.

### §3.2 The corpus checking itself

`gangesha.md` recorded, in Batch 46, "a date that drifted one way for a century, with **every stage still in circulation**." Pāṇini is the same pathology at ten times the scale and in the opposite direction — **later**, not earlier.

In both cases the superseded estimate stays in print beside the current one. **A reader sampling several reference works receives the whole range and mistakes the spread for a consensus.** That was Batch 46's central generalisation about sources; it holds unchanged in a literature 1,500 years removed.

## §4 The machinery, and a real metalanguage

### §4.1 Five counts of one text

The Aṣṭādhyāyī is eight *adhyāyas* of four *pādas*, recitable end to end in two hours, memorised verbatim for millennia. **And its sources give at least five different totals for the number of rules:**

| count | source | what the number depends on |
|---|---|---|
| **3,983** | Abhyankar — the Kāśikā and Siddhāntakaumudī commentators, **and** "the traditional recital by Veda scholars" | the commentarial canon |
| 3,996 | Abhyankar, reporting "a verse current among Vaiyākaraṇa schools" | a mnemonic round figure |
| 3,981 | Wikipedia, "Mahābhāṣya" | unstated |
| 3,959 | Wikipedia, "Pāṇini" | unstated |
| **lower still** | **Patañjali** — who classes **nine** received sūtras as Vārttikas and **two** as Gaṇasūtras | a **doctrinal** decision |

> **The disagreement is not sloppiness. What counts as a sūtra *of Pāṇini* is a judgement, not an observation** — the received recitation contains lines the greatest commentator says are not his.

**The text is stable and its boundary is not.** This is §2's *munitraya* sense-2 in numerical form: counting the Aṣṭādhyāyī means deciding where one author stops and the next begins.

*(⚠ The scan gives Patañjali's figure as 3,979, but 3,983 − 9 − 2 = 3,972. The two do not reconcile and the discrepancy may be an OCR digit error. The exact figure is **not asserted** here — only the reason it is lower.)*

### §4.2 *Anubandha*: symbols that delete themselves

Abhyankar, at first hand, on the *it*-markers:

> a letter "added to a word before or after it, **only to signify some specific purpose**" — called ***it***, "lit. going or disappearing," because "they do **not** form an essential part of the word… the word in usage being always found **without** the *it* letter."

**This is a genuine metalanguage, and it can be stated exactly without any modern comparison.** Pāṇini tags affixes with symbols that exist in the *rule-language* and are guaranteed absent from the *object-language*. The derivation consumes them and deletes them. A diacritic layer, used systematically, with a formal guarantee of non-interference.

### §4.3 *Pratyāhāra*: the device with no name

The fourteen **Śivasūtras** list the sounds of Sanskrit in a contrived order, each group closed by a mute marker. A *pratyāhāra* then names any contiguous run by pairing its first sound with a closing marker — so whole phonological classes get two-syllable names. It is the most admired piece of engineering in the text.

And Abhyankar says this:

> "The term *pratyāhāra* is **not actually used by Pāṇini**… The term appears to have come into use **after** Pāṇini. **Pāṇini has not given any definition of the term.** He has simply given the *method* of forming the Pratyāhāras and he has profusely used them."

> **The most famous device in the Aṣṭādhyāyī is one its author uses constantly, never names, and never defines.**

## §5 *Kāraka*: a level between meaning and morphology

The single most interesting thing in the grammar is a layer that sits in the middle of it.

Abhyankar's gloss: a *kāraka* is "**the capacity in which a thing becomes instrumental in bringing about an action**," and "this capacity is looked upon as **the sense of the case-affixes which express it**." There are six — ***kartṛ*** (agent), ***karman*** (object), ***karaṇa*** (instrument), ***sampradāna*** (recipient), ***apādāna*** (source), ***adhikaraṇa*** (locus) — introduced at A 1.4.23 and worked out to 1.4.55.

⚠ **Kārakas are not cases, and that is the whole point.** Six roles, seven case-endings, and the mapping is neither one-to-one nor fixed: an agent is nominative in the active and instrumental in the passive; the instrumental also expresses *karaṇa*.

> **The Aṣṭādhyāyī does not go from meaning straight to ending. It goes meaning → role → ending** — and the role layer is where the generalisations live.

The tradition marked the distinction itself: endings assigned by a participant-role (*kārakavibhakti*) **outrank** endings assigned by government from a neighbouring word (*upapadavibhakti*), which are "looked upon as **weaker**." **A principled priority rule between two competing sources of one surface form** — structurally the same problem Ch 38 watched Navya-Nyāya solve with the *avacchedaka*, and a comparable solution: restrict the scope of an assignment instead of abandoning the rule.

## §6 Kātyāyana and Patañjali: what a commentary is for

### §6.1 A corpus whose size depends on an 18th-century definition

Kātyāyana's Vārttikas number "**well-nigh above 5000**" on the received classification. But on **Nāgeśa's** (18th c.) narrower two-class definition, "many of the Vārttikas given in the Mahābhāṣya as explaining and commenting upon the Sūtras **will not strictly be termed as Vārttikas**," and the total falls to "**about 1400 or so**."

And this is not a scholastic quibble: "There are **some manuscript copies which give this reduced number**." **A definitional decision propagated into the manuscript tradition.**

### §6.2 The commentator is dated better than his author

Three independent sources — Abhyankar, SEP, and Wikipedia ("by both Western and Indian scholars") — agree that Patañjali wrote in the **mid-2nd century BCE**. After §3's 350-year spread for Pāṇini, that is startling.

> **The commentator's date is fixed within about fifty years; the author's is open across three and a half centuries.**

The reason is mundane and worth keeping. Patañjali wrote **discursive prose** and illustrated grammatical points with the world around him — the Mauryas, contemporary events — and so timestamped his own book. Pāṇini wrote rules so compressed they name almost nothing outside the language.

**Precision of transmission and recoverability of date are unrelated, and can run in opposite directions.**

### §6.3 The Great Commentary covers less than a third of its text

The Mahābhāṣya, in **85 *āhnika*s**, treats **1,228 of 3,981 sūtras** — about **31%**.

> **The tradition's highest authority is silent on most of its own root text.**

Abhyankar counts the Pāṇinian literature at roughly **800 works by 400 authors**; a good part of that is covering the gap. **Selectivity, not comprehensiveness, is the genre.**

### §6.4 Five tiers

> Pāṇini's **sūtras** → Kātyāyana's **Vārttikas** → Patañjali's **Mahābhāṣya** → Kaiyaṭa's ***Pradīpa*** → Nāgeśa's ***Uddyota***

Nāgeśa, in the early eighteenth century, is writing **a commentary on a commentary on a commentary on a commentary on a rule-book** — and Abhyankar names the title (*Mahābhāṣyapradīpoddyota*) at first hand. Ch 38 found the same climb in Navya-Nyāya at two tiers. Here it reaches five, **and §2.1's maxim makes it respectable.**

## §7 Nirukta, and whether the Veda means anything

The sister vedāṅga divides the labour explicitly: "*Vyakarana* deals with linguistic analysis to establish the exact **form** of words… while Nirukta focuses on… the proper **meaning**." Its monument is Yāska's *Nirukta*, dated roughly 700–500 BCE, "before Gautama Buddha."

Yāska preserves two disputes, and the second is the most consequential thing in the ancillary literature.

**The first:** do all nouns derive from verbs? Yāska sides with **Śākaṭāyana** that they do — all naming is ultimately the naming of an *activity*. **Gārgya** objects that this over-generates: if nouns came solely from verbs, "every profession would share a name."

⚠ Two temptations to decline. This is *not* etymology in the modern comparative sense — neither party has sound-change or language families. And Yāska's siding does **not** settle it: **Gārgya's objection is the better argument, and the tradition preserved it anyway.** What is at stake is whether meaning is grounded in *processes* or in *things* — the same axis on which the Mahābhāṣya records Vyāḍi and Vājapyāyana dividing, one generation later and in the other discipline.

**The second:** Yāska records **Kautsa's** objection that the Vedic mantras are ***meaningless***, and answers it.

> **Stop on that.** §1 established that vyākaraṇa exists to prevent corrupt transmission of the Veda. **Kautsa attacks the premise of the entire programme**: if the mantras mean nothing, preserving their exact form is preserving noise.

**The two ancillary sciences of the Veda are, at their root, an argument about whether the Veda says anything** — and the discipline of *meaning* is where the objection had to be met. ⚠ **Yāska's actual answer was not read** and is not characterised here; the scholarly treatment (*J. Indian Philos.*, 2021, on *Nirukta* 1.15–16) is paywalled and is carried as a follow-up. **The chapter has the question and not the answer, and says so.**

### §7.1 A false friend

Abhyankar records that Yāska and Pāṇini use ***dravya*** "in a very general sense as **something in completed formation or existence**, as opposed to *bhāva* or *kriyā* or verbal activity."

**This is not Vaiśeṣika's *dravya*.** For the grammarians it is the nominal-versus-verbal contrast — exactly what §7's first dispute is about. For Vaiśeṣika it is the first ontological category. Same word, unrelated work. ⚠ The confusion is easy because Ch 41 shows Bhartṛhari *deliberately* borrowing the Vaiśeṣika category list — **but the borrowing post-dates the divergence**, and reading it backwards makes the grammarians into ontologists from the start.

## §8 What this chapter actually found

### §8.1 Pāṇini states operations, not definitions

Three times over, in his own text:

| what is missing | who supplied it | when |
|---|---|---|
| the name and definition of ***pratyāhāra*** | the post-Pāṇinian tradition; the term is in the Ṛk-Tantra | after Pāṇini |
| the definition of ***kāraka*** — A 1.4.23 is a *heading* | "later grammarians and logicians": *kriyānvayitvaṃ kārakatvam* | after Pāṇini |
| Pāṇini's **own biography** — read out of A 4.3.94 as "the man from Śalātura" | **Jinendrabuddhi** | centuries after |

He tells you what to *do* with a kāraka and never says what one *is*. **The metalanguage that makes the system teachable is the commentators' contribution** — which is why §2.1's authority-maxim runs upward. The definitions are downstream **by construction**.

⚠ **And the consequence is genuinely undecidable:** whether the commentarial definitions *recover* Pāṇini's meaning or *supply* one cannot be settled from the text, because the text is silent by design.

### §8.2 The mechanism is neutral — and the batch's own source proves it

Ch 39 catalogued six ways a hole in the record gets filled, and treated them as failure modes. **This chapter forces a correction.**

- Jinendrabuddhi reading Pāṇini's biography out of a rule → **false history.**
- Haradatta reading the whole sphoṭa doctrine out of the *name* Sphoṭāyana (Ch 41 §2) → **false history.**
- The tradition supplying a name and definition for *pratyāhāra* → **a perfectly good analysis.** The device is really there; the account of it is correct.

> **The mechanism is not inherently corrupting. It is inherently invisible.** The corpus's job is to notice that it operated, not to assume the result is wrong.

⚠ **And the same correction applies to this chapter's own best source.** Abhyankar is decisive where the encyclopedias are vague — on *pratyāhāra*, on the sūtra counts, on the Vararuci question (§8.3). He is also, on Bhartṛhari's century, simply **out of date** (Ch 41 §1.2). The lesson for the corpus's method:

> **"Read at first hand" and "specialist" are strong marks for what a discipline knows *internally* — vocabulary, manuscript contents, which attributions the tradition itself doubts. They are weak for anything depending on outside scholarship that has moved. Chronology above all.**

Abhyankar (1961) is authoritative on *pratyāhāra* and wrong about Bhartṛhari's century, and both are true of the same book.

### §8.3 A specialist lexicon that does not check itself

The identification **Kātyāyana = Vararuci** is standard. Abhyankar's dictionary contains both the endorsement and its refutation, **in two entries, without cross-reference**:

- *s.v.* **Vararuci**, opening: "it is **very likely** that Vararuci was the individual name of the scholar, and Kātyāyana his family name."
- *s.v.* **Vararuci**, conclusion, from the primary text: "There is **no reference at all** in the Mahābhāṣya to Vararuci as a writer of the Vārttikas; there is only one reference which shows that there was a scholar by name Vararuci known to Patañjali, **but he was a poet**." Verdict: "**Patañjali does not associate Vararuci with Kātyāyana at all**… it is only the **later writers** who identified" them.
- *s.v.* **Kātyāyana**: reports the identification, with no refutation.

Batch 46 concluded that reference sources "are unreliable **as a set of witnesses**, because they do not check themselves across articles." That was said of Wikipedia strata.

> **It holds inside a single-author scholarly dictionary. The pathology is not peculiar to crowd-sourced reference works — it is a property of the entry as a unit of writing.**

⚠ Stated as a qualification of the corpus's own finding, and **not** as a hit on Abhyankar: his Vararuci entry is the best sustained argument the batch encountered, and it is the entry that supplies the refutation.

*(A bonus, and a nice closing of a loop: Wikipedia's authority for the identification is the* Kathāsaritsāgara *— the very text Abhyankar elsewhere calls "only legendary… very little historical value." **One source's verdict, turned on another source's evidence.**)*

## §9 ⚠ Pāṇini and formal grammar — the prime-directive section

No text in this corpus attracts modern comparison like the Aṣṭādhyāyī. §0 applies at full strength, and the honest procedure is to state the comparison fully **and then state exactly what it does not license.**

**What is fairly said, all of it structural:**

- "the first **context-sensitive formal model of language**," showing "many features of a formal, computationally implementable system" **comparable to** the **Backus–Naur form**;
- its derivational technique "**compared to the rewrite systems** developed in the 1920s–30s by **Emil Post**" — "Sanskritists now accept that Pāṇini's linguistic apparatus is **well-described as an 'applied' Post system**";
- a comparison to a **Turing machine** as an idealised reduction of a computing device;
- **Chomsky**: "the first generative grammar in the modern sense was Pāṇini's grammar";
- and the *kāraka* system is **live**: a "Pāṇinian Grammar Framework" is used in present-day computational linguistics, applied to English and to Indian-language machine translation.

**What none of it licenses:**

1. **These are descriptions of the Aṣṭādhyāyī in modern terms by modern readers.** "Well-described as an applied Post system" is a statement about **the describer's vocabulary**, not about Pāṇini's. The text has no notion of a machine, of decidability, or of a hierarchy of formal power.
2. **Usability is not identity.** That engineers can borrow the kāraka scheme shows the analysis is *general*; it is not evidence that Pāṇini held a theory of universal argument structure. The kārakas are six, fixed, tuned to Sanskrit's morphology, **and Pāṇini never argues for the number.**
3. **And the transmission claim is false.** Cardona, from inside the discipline, on the genealogy most often asserted — that Pāṇini shaped modern structural linguistics through Saussure: of Saussure's work, "it shows **no direct influence** of Pāṇinian grammar. Indeed, on occasion, Saussure follows a path that is **contrary** to Pāṇinian procedure."

> **The resemblance is real and the descent is not.** That sentence is the whole of §0 in eight words, and the Aṣṭādhyāyī is the best case in the corpus for keeping the two apart — because here the resemblance is *unusually strong*, which is precisely when the inference is most tempting and least warranted.

⚠ **The corpus draws no edge from `kāraka` to any modern-linguistics node**, because it has none and the nearest targets would misstate the relation. Recorded in prose, on the precedent `avacchedaka.md` set when it faced the same choice about the grammarians — **and refused it.**

## §10 Where this goes next

Everything above is a *technical* discipline: a preservation problem, a rule-system, a commentarial hierarchy, and an argument with the etymologists about what words mean.

**None of it is philosophy yet.** SEP's verdict on the grammarians is that they were "more interested in language and communication than in ontology," and that they "do not develop a *pramāṇa* theory" at all.

That changed, in one man's hands, and it had to be *argued for*. **[Chapter 41](../darsana/41-the-word-as-the-world.md) is that argument** — which is why it sits in `darsana/` and this chapter sits in `shastra/`. **The two folders are the corpus's answer to a question its sources split on.**
