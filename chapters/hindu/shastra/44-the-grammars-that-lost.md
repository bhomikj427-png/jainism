# Chapter 44 — The Grammars That Lost: Kātantra, Cāndra, Prākṛt, and the Metalanguage Everyone Shared

> **Primary concepts:** `kātantra` · `sarvavarman` · `cāndra-vyākaraṇa` · `candragomin` · `prākṛtaprakāśa` · `paribhāṣā`
> **Folder:** `hindu/shastra/` — continuing [Ch 40](40-what-grammar-was-for.md) and [Ch 42](42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md). ⚠ **And the folder is wrong in an instructive way.** One of this chapter's two grammars is Buddhist, one of its authors was a layman, and its central finding is that these systems *left* the Brahminical frame that `hindu/` names. The file stays here because it is continuous with Chs 40 and 42 as **śāstra** — but the strain is the chapter's subject, and §3 is where it shows. The four **commentator** nodes from the same batch are in [Ch 45](../../cross-tradition/45-the-witness-problem.md), in `cross-tradition/`, because they are about transmission rather than about grammar.
> **Reads after:** [Ch 40](40-what-grammar-was-for.md) — this chapter tests its thesis and finds it half right.

---

## How to use this chapter

Chapters 40 and 42 built nine chapters' worth of Sanskrit grammar out of one lineage: Pāṇini, Kātyāyana, Patañjali, Kaiyaṭa, the Kāśikā, Bhaṭṭoji, Nāgeśa. At the end of Ch 42 the corpus noticed something uncomfortable. A grep found that **"Kātantra" scores 92 hits in the tradition's leading reference dictionary and 2 in this corpus.** There were other grammars. There were whole other *systems*, with their own sūtras, their own commentators, their own metarules — and the corpus had treated "Sanskrit grammar" as a synonym for one of them.

This chapter is what was on the other side of that gap. It has one governing question:

> **What did Sanskrit grammar look like to the people who actually had to learn it?**

Four things to carry out:

1. **The premise the batch opened with was wrong, and the correct version is more interesting.** The corpus assumed it had been reading *the winner's account*. There was no winner. There was a system that won prestige and a different system that won classrooms. (§1, §2)
2. **The rival systems are defined by dropping the Veda — and that finding is half false, and this chapter shows exactly where it broke.** (§3)
3. **These grammars are built in modules, and the modules come off.** Three books, three terminal appendices, three different fates: lost, detached, accreted. (§4)
4. **Underneath all of it, every system was running the same metalanguage** — nine systems, fifteen collections of metarules, five hundred maxims, many of them word-for-word shared. **At the level where grammar reasons about itself, the schools were one discipline.** (§7)

⚠ **This chapter contains a mistake the corpus made and then caught, two nodes later, inside the same batch.** §3 sets it out in full rather than quietly presenting the corrected version. That is deliberate: the error is more instructive than the fact.

---

## §1 The premise, and how it broke

The batch was queued with a frame: *the corpus has been reading the winner's account of Sanskrit grammar.* The test was the **Kātantra** — was it a genuine rival, or a simplification?

The answer to the narrow question is clear and comes from three independent directions. Abhyankar says Sarvavarman omitted the Vedic section "and all the intricacies and difficult elements… **with a view to making his grammar useful for beginners and students of average intelligence**." LinguIndic calls it "intended as a **practical grammar**." A modern survey calls it "**the shortest extant grammar**." It is a teaching book.

But the frame was the wrong shape, and the reason is in Abhyankar's own entry:

> The Kātantra "was very popular especially among those who wanted to study spoken Sanskrit with ease and **attained for several years a very prominent place among text-books on grammar especially in Behar, Bengal and Gujarat**. It has got a large number of glosses and commentary works, **many of which are in a manuscript form at present**."

**The Kātantra did not lose.** It has an enormous unpublished commentarial literature, it was the ordinary schoolbook of eastern India and Kashmir for centuries, and — on a claim this corpus flags as single-sourced and does not rely on — it "had a considerable influence on the Pali grammarian **Kaccāyana** and also on the **Dravidian grammarians**." If that holds, the grammatical tradition that reached Pali and the south is not Pāṇini's.

⭐ **So the honest correction is not "the corpus read the winner." It is that the corpus mistook prestige for reach.** Pāṇini won the commentarial tradition, the philosophical literature, and the reference works. The Kātantra won the students.

## §2 What the Kātantra actually is

**1,412 sūtras. Four adhyāyas.** Technical terms, sandhi, declension, syntax, compounds, taddhita affixes, conjugation, voice, verbal derivatives — in that order, which is **not** Pāṇini's order. Abhyankar:

> "a **systematic abridgment** of the Aṣṭādhyāyī… It ignores many unimportant rules of Pāṇini, adjusts many, and **altogether omits the Vedic portion and the accent chapter** of Pāṇini. It lays down the Sūtras in an order **different from that of Pāṇini**."

### §2.1 Pāṇinian in substance, pre-Pāṇinian in form

The arrangement is the interesting part, and Abhyankar draws the right inference from it:

> "As the arrangement of topics is **entirely different** from Pāṇini's order, inspite of considerable resemblance of Sūtras and their wording, it is probable that the work was **based on Pāṇini but composed on the models of ancient grammarians** viz. Indra, Śākaṭāyana and others whose works, although not available now, were available to the author."

Pāṇini organises **element by element** — technical terms, roots, case-relations, compounds, affixes, substitutes, accents, euphonic change. The older grammarians, Abhyankar says, organised **topic by topic** — sandhi, then declension, then conjugation, and so on, the way a language textbook still does. ⭐ **The "post-Pāṇinian" systems are, in method, pre-Pāṇinian.** They go back to the arrangement Pāṇini's genius superseded, because that arrangement is the one a student can walk through.

⚠ Note what Abhyankar is doing, though: inferring the *availability* of lost works from the *shape* of a surviving one. It is a real argument and it is unfalsifiable in the direction it runs — the lost grammars cannot be checked. He marks it "it is probable"; the corpus keeps his hedge.

### §2.2 A book with a design brief, and a legend that matches it

The Kātantra's origin story is told at length in the *Kathāsaritsāgara*, and Ch 45 will use it for a different purpose. Here is the part that bears on the text:

King Sātavāhana is humiliated by a Sanskrit error in front of his queens. **Śarvavarman promises to make him proficient in six months.** Guṇāḍhya wagers that if he does, Guṇāḍhya will abandon his knowledge of Sanskrit, Prākṛt and the local language. Śarvavarman performs austerities; **Kārttikeya gives him the grammar and names it**; Śarvavarman wins.

Now set the legend beside the book. Short. No Vedic section. No accents. Topic-wise. Aimed at "students of average intelligence." ⭐ **The legend and the text specify the same object: a grammar you can teach an adult non-specialist in six months.**

⚠ **Two readings, and the corpus asserts neither.** Either the legend preserves a real commission and the book's shape is its trace — or, more cheaply, **the legend was built backwards out of the book's obvious character.** Anyone who opened the Kātantra could see it was a crash course; the story supplies a reason. The second is the corpus's default suspicion, and nothing here decides it. What is recorded is the fit, flagged as a fit.

## §3 ⚠ "The rival systems dropped the Veda" — the batch's own error, in full

This section presents a mistake rather than a fact, because the mistake is the more useful object.

### §3.1 What the corpus concluded

Abhyankar's **preface** — not an entry, and so not written to defend any headword — surveys the whole post-Pāṇinian field at once:

> Pāṇini "was followed by a number of grammarians who wrote **popular treatises** on grammar… These treatises, written by **Sarvavarman, Candragomin, Devanandin, Pālyakīrti Śākaṭāyana, Hemacandra, Kramadīśvara, Jumaranandin, Supadma** and others… came to be looked upon as **different systems of grammar**." And: "**The special feature of all these grammars was that they entirely omitted the Vedic peculiarities and accents.**"

Set that against Ch 40's thesis. Ch 40 is called *What Grammar Was For*, and its answer is: grammar is a **vedāṅga**, an ancillary science of the Veda, whose reason for existing is to preserve and expound śruti.

The inference was immediate and it looked excellent. **Eight named authors, six centuries, four religions — Brahminical, Buddhist, Jain — and the one thing they share is the one thing that would disqualify them as vedāṅgas.** The corpus wrote it up as the batch's headline finding: *what makes a grammar "a different system" is that it stops being a vedāṅga.*

### §3.2 What broke it

Two nodes later, writing up the Cāndra grammar, the corpus met **Ramhari Timalsina** of the Heidelberg Academy of Sciences, in a study of exactly this question. The Cāndravyākaraṇa did not have six chapters. **It had eight.**

> Chapters **7 (Vedic rules) and 8 (svara — pitch accent)** stood at the end, "as the author placed these at the end of his work rather than following Pāṇini's practice of distributing them throughout." **"These two chapters were lost at some point, and the published texts of the Cāndravyākaraṇa and available manuscripts contain only the first six chapters."**

Abhyankar's "six chapters… no section on Vedic Grammar and accents" is an accurate description of the **received** text and a false one about the **composed** text.

⭐⭐ **A transmission accident had been read as an authorial choice** — by the tradition's leading lexicographer, and then by this corpus, for exactly two commits.

### §3.3 What survives, stated precisely

> Every named post-Pāṇinian system, **as received**, lacks Vedic and accentual material. In at least one case that is demonstrably a **transmission loss and not an authorial choice**, and the corpus therefore does not know, for any of the others, which it is.

That is weaker and better. Note what it does **not** say. It does not say Abhyankar was wrong about the Kātantra: no source claims the Kātantra ever had Vedic chapters, and his *Sarvavarman* entry has the omission as deliberate and motivated. ⭐ **What is shown is that his class-level generalisation was assembled from received texts and cannot bear the weight of an authorial claim.** That is a different and more precise charge — and it is the third time in three batches that this corpus has caught a reference work making a general statement its own entries would not license.

### §3.4 Why this belongs in a chapter rather than a footnote

Ch 42 §8 collected six sources caught being wrong, and the sixth was this corpus. The habit is deliberate. A batch that reports only the tests it passes is not running tests.

But there is a second reason here. **The error was not careless.** The evidence was a specialist's own summary of his whole field, stated as a generalisation, in a preface. Nothing about it looked thin. What defeated it was a single modern scholar who had gone and counted the chapters — which is [Ch 45](../../cross-tradition/45-the-witness-problem.md)'s subject, and the reason these two chapters are a pair.

## §4 The terminal module, and the three things that happen to it

The correction in §3 turned out to open a real structural finding, which is the best kind of correction.

Candragomin put the hard, specialised material **at the end**: Vedic rules, then accent, and — separately — **86 paribhāṣās "given at the end of his grammar work."** That is not a quirk. It is how these books are built.

| work | core | terminal module | what happened to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cāndra-vyākaraṇa** | 6 adhyāyas, classical Sanskrit | ch. 7 Vedic, ch. 8 svara; plus 86 paribhāṣās | ⭐ the Vedic pair **LOST**; the metarule appendix survived |
| **Siddhahemacandra** (Hemacandra) | 7 books, Sanskrit, "based practically upon Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī" | **book 8 = Prākṛt**, "a Grammar of **all** the Prakrit dialects" | ⭐ **DETACHED** — and became the part he is read for |
| **Prākṛtaprakāśa** | 8 (or 9) chapters, Māhārāṣṭrī | 3 dialect chapters: Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, Paiśācī | ⭐ **ACCRETED** — the text grew from 8 chapters to 12 |

⭐⭐ **Same design, three fates.** And this is the same fact as the pedagogy in §2.1: **Pāṇini interleaves; the rivals modularise; a topic-wise book has separable topics.** The organisation that makes a grammar teachable is the organisation that makes its parts detachable. The Vedic chapters go missing, the Prākṛt book becomes a classic in its own right, and the dialect sections multiply — three consequences of one architectural decision.

⚠ **Held as a pattern with three instances, not a law.** No source states it; it is the corpus's synthesis across four first-hand entries and two independent reports. And the corpus deliberately does **not** now go back and claim the Kātantra or the Haima grammar also lost Vedic chapters — only that the possibility is open where before it had not occurred to anyone here.

## §5 The Cāndra-vyākaraṇa: a Buddhist grammar, and the road not taken

**Candragomin, c. 450 CE, Bengal.** Three-quarters the length of the Aṣṭādhyāyī. Its author's stated virtues for it are "**abrevity, lucidity, and comprehensiveness**." Abhyankar calls him "a famous Buddhist Sanskrit grammarian whose grammar, existing in the **Tibetan script**, is now available in the Devanāgarī script" — the northern Buddhist transmission, not the Indian manuscript tradition.

And it is described as "**the first great revised edition of the Aṣṭādhyāyī, embodying all the suggestions and corrections of Kātyāyana and Patañjali**." LinguIndic gives the concrete case: the Cāndrasūtra incorporates **a vārttika of Kātyāyana as a sūtra**.

⭐⭐ **Set that beside Ch 42.** Nine centuries of accumulated correction had piled up on Pāṇini's text, and the Pāṇinian tradition's answer was a **hierarchy**: sūtra, vārttika, bhāṣya, each tier commenting the last, with Kaiyaṭa's maxim to rank them — *yathottaraṃ munitrayasya prāmāṇyam*, and Ch 42 §2 showed the ladder stopping at Patañjali. **Candragomin's answer was to rewrite the root text so the corrections are in it.**

Two responses to one problem: **rank the commentators, or absorb them.** ⚠ **No causal or polemical relation is asserted** — no source says Candragomin was reacting against the commentarial method. But the corpus had only ever seen one of the two answers, and Ch 42 was written as though it were the only one available.

### §5.1 And he arrived with his own commentary

The *Cāndravṛtti* is **Candragomin's own**. ⭐ Where the Pāṇinian line needed nine centuries and four tiers to gloss its root text, this one shipped with the gloss attached. Whether that is a virtue depends entirely on what you think Ch 42's ladder was for.

## §6 Who wrote these books

Two facts, and the corpus notes their recurrence without building on it.

**Candragomin was an *upāsaka* — a layman, not a monk.** (On Mark Tatz's division, discussed in Ch 45 §7, the layman who debated Candrakīrti for seven years may be a *different*, later Candragomin; but both readings agree the name belongs to a layman.)

**Sarvavarman, in his legend, is a layman who teaches a king in six months.**

And Abhyankar's judgement on the Kātantra is that it was written "for **beginners and students of average intelligence**" — the same register in which Ch 42 §1.1 found him describing the Kāśikā, the only full commentary on Pāṇini, as "excellent help to **beginners**."

⭐ Three of this chapter's nodes are about grammar leaving the institution that owned it: the **Veda** in §3, the **monastery** in §5, and the **seminary** throughout. ⚠ **This is a pattern the corpus is noticing, not a thesis it is arguing.** The sample is a handful of nodes selected *because* they were the gap, which is precisely the selection effect that manufactures patterns.

### §6.1 The divine warrants, and a chronology the corpus had assumed without checking

Pāṇini's Śivasūtras are traditionally traced to **Śiva's drum**. The Kātantra is called ***Kaumāra*** because Sarvavarman received it from **Kumāra-Kārttikeya**, at Śiva's direction — and *Kalāpa* because, in the god's own words in Tawney's translation, "**it shall be called Kātantra, and Kālāpaka, from the tail (*kalāpa*) of the peacock on which I ride**."

The tidy reading — *Pāṇini gets the father, the Kātantra gets the son, and the deities stand in the hierarchy the grammars do* — is available, and the corpus wrote it, and then had to qualify it. Because Deshpande's finding (Ch 42 §2.4) is that **Haradatta gives the first attestation *in the Pāṇinian tradition*** of Pāṇini receiving the Śiva-sūtras from Maheśvara — and Haradatta is 11th–13th century, i.e. **contemporary with or later than Somadeva**, the *Kathāsaritsāgara*'s author.

⭐ **In the surviving record, neither divine warrant is demonstrably older than the other.** Surfacing in the record is not origination, and the corpus claims nothing about which legend is actually older — only that **the seniority it had been tacitly assuming is not there.** What survives is the weaker and safer point: **divine attribution is not a Pāṇinian peculiarity. It is how a Sanskrit grammar was made authoritative at all.**

## §7 ⭐ The metalanguage everyone shared

This is the chapter's strongest section and it answers the batch's sharpest question. If the rival systems were genuinely separate schools, one would expect them to reason about their own rules in their own way. **They did not.** They all used ***paribhāṣā*** — the metarule: not a rule of the language, but a rule about how the rules are to be read.

Abhyankar states the finding himself, and marks it as remarkable:

> "In the different systems of grammar there are different collections of Paribhāṣās… **It is a noticeable fact that many Paribhāṣās are common, with their wordings quite similar or sometimes identical in the different systems.** … **The total number of Paribhāṣās in the different systems of grammar may well-nigh exceed 500.**"

And he did more than say it. The ***Paribhāṣāsaṃgraha*** is "a collection of independent works on Paribhāṣās **in the several systems of Sanskrit Grammar**, **compiled by M. M. K. V. Abhyankar**." ⭐ **The dictionary this whole chapter is built on was written by the man who edited the collected metalanguage of every school.** Fifteen collections, nine systems:

| collection | count | system |
|---|---|---|
| Vyāḍi's *Paribhāṣāsūcana*, "who lived **before Patañjali**" | 93 | ⭐ pre-Patañjali |
| bare text, Vyāḍi's school | 140 | Vyāḍi |
| attributed to Śākaṭāyana | 98 | Śākaṭāyana |
| **Candragomin**, at the end of his grammar | 86 | Cāndra |
| Durgasiṃha | 65 | **Kātantra** |
| Bhāvamiśra | 62 | **Kātantra** |
| anonymous | 96 | **Kātantra** |
| anonymous | 118 | Kālāpa |
| ⭐ **Abhyankar's own gloss**, on Abhayanandin's *Mahāvṛtti* | 108 | Jainendra |
| **Bhoja**, *Sārasvatīkaṇṭhābharaṇa* I.ii | 118 | Bhoja |
| Hemahaṃsagaṇi — where they are called ***nyāya*** | 140 | Haima |
| Puruṣottamadeva | 120 | Pāṇini |
| Sīradeva | 130 | Pāṇini |
| Nīlakaṇṭha | 140 | Pāṇini |
| Haribhāskara | 132 | Pāṇini |

An independent report reaches the same order of magnitude from another direction: "over five-hundred paribhāṣās written, with 19 independent treatises available."

⭐⭐ **At the level where grammar reasons about itself, there was no winner and no loser. There was one discipline.**

### §7.1 Watching one maxim travel

Abhyankar cites two individual maxims across systems, and the same pattern appears both times: **the content is shared, the numbering is not.**

| maxim | Vyāḍi | Cāndra | Kātantra | Kālāpa |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *uktārtha* — do not repeat what is already expressed | 51 | 28 | **46** | **46** |
| the mute-indicatory *adarśana* maxim | 11 | 14 | **54** | **71** |

⭐ Look at the bottom row against the top. Kātantra and Kālāpa **agree exactly** on one maxim and **diverge widely** on the other — which is the profile of two recensions of one list, separately augmented. That is consistent with Abhyankar's judgement that Kātantra and Kālāpa are "different versions of the other." ⚠ It is offered as *consistent with*, not as evidence for: **two data points cannot establish a recension history.**

### §7.2 Stronger than shared maxims — a shared way of making them

Abhyankar, on *jñāpaka* — an "indicator," a feature of a text's wording taken to imply a convention its author never states:

> "The Paribhāṣā works on other systems of grammar such as the Kātantra, the Jainendra and others have **drawn similar Jñāpakas from the wording of the Sūtras *in their systems*.**"

⭐ So the schools were not merely borrowing each other's maxims. **Each was independently manufacturing new maxims out of its own text, by one shared technique.**

⚠ And note what that technique *is*. It is reading a doctrine out of a text's incidental features — the same move that Ch 42 and Ch 45 flag as a source of false history: **Haradatta reading the sphoṭa doctrine out of the name *Sphoṭāyana*; Jinendrabuddhi reading Pāṇini's birthplace out of a rule in Pāṇini's own grammar.** ⭐ **Here it is not an error at all. It is a licensed, named, technical procedure with its own vocabulary and its own literature.** The corpus's standing formulation gets its clearest case: *the mechanism is not corrupting, it is invisible* — and in the paribhāṣā literature it is not even invisible. **It is institutionalised.**

### §7.3 And it leaves grammar entirely

[Ch 35](35-dating-a-literature-without-dates.md) covers the Āyurvedic commentator **Indu**, who appended **119 numbered *paribhāṣā* verses** to his medical work — edited and translated by N. S. Mooss, and argued over by Meulenbeld.

⭐ **The corpus met the form in medicine two batches before it met it in grammar.** ⚠ **No borrowing is claimed**: no source connects Indu's paribhāṣās to the grammatical ones, and the word is ordinary Sanskrit for a governing convention. But the *form* is the same form — a numbered appendix of interpretive conventions attached to a technical text, of the same order of size as the grammarians' collections. **The batch's question turns out to have been too small: *paribhāṣā* is not shared across the grammatical systems, it is shared across śāstra.**

## §8 Prākṛt: the grammar of the languages that were not Sanskrit

The corpus had **no node for Prākṛt grammar at all** before this batch, which is a large thing to have been missing from nine chapters about *vyākaraṇa*.

The ***Prākṛtaprakāśa*** is "probably the earliest surviving Prakrit grammar," attributed to **Vararuci** — and specifically to Abhyankar's **Vararuci (2)**, the one who "also was **Kātyāyana by Gotra name**," "a grammarian **later than Patañjali**," and co-author of the Kātantra's fourth adhyāya. It treats **Māhārāṣṭrī** at length and then **Śaurasenī, Māgadhī and Paiśācī** — and, per §4 above, it grew from 8 chapters to 12 by acquiring them.

A rival for "oldest" exists: **Caṇḍa's *Prākṛtalakṣaṇa***, edited by **Hoernle**, Calcutta 1880, whose date is "very controversial."

### §8.1 A resonance, recorded and refused

Here is a pattern the corpus met, wrote down, and declined to use — and the refusal is the point.

In the legend of §2.2, Guṇāḍhya loses the wager, abandons Sanskrit and Prākṛt, goes to the Vindhyas, and composes the *Bṛhatkathā* in **Paiśācī**. And the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*, attributed to **Vararuci**, is one of the very few texts that **codifies Paiśācī**. And **Vararuci is a character in that same legend.**

It is an extremely satisfying loop. ⭐ **It is also entirely manufactured by a name collision.** Ch 42 §6 established, from Abhyankar, that **four different men** bore the name Vararuci; the Prākṛt grammarian is #2, explicitly "later than Patañjali," while the *Kathāsaritsāgara*'s Vararuci belongs to the legendary layer. **The man in the Paiśācī origin-story is not the man who wrote the Paiśācī grammar.**

⚠ **This is recorded because noticing it and refusing it is the finding.** It is exactly what the collision *does* to a reader — and Ch 42 documented the tradition itself succumbing to precisely this, repeatedly. The corpus is not immune to a mechanism just because it has written a chapter about it.

## §9 The editors

One more thing this chapter's sources have in common, and it is worth saying out loud.

The **Kātantra** and the **Cāndra-vyākaraṇa** were both put into modern circulation by **Bruno Liebich** — Abhyankar gives him a headword: "a European grammarian belonging to **Breslau**… He made a critical study of Sanskrit grammar and **edited the Candra Vyākaraṇa** and the *Kṣīratáraṅgiṇī*." The ***Prākṛtaprakāśa*** reaches us through **E. B. Cowell** (Hertford, 1854, with an English translation and Bhāmaha's *Manoramā*), and Caṇḍa through **Hoernle** (1880). **Pischel** gets a headword too.

⭐ **The entire non-Pāṇinian shelf reaches a modern reader through a handful of nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century European editors, and the tradition's own leading lexicographer lists them among the ācāryas.**

⚠ And there is a recursion in that which no source names: **Liebich's edition is the reason a "six-chapter" Cāndravyākaraṇa is the text everyone describes** — including the man whose generalisation §3 had to correct.

## §10 What is still not known

This chapter describes six texts and **has read none of them.** That is stated plainly rather than buried:

- **Not one sūtra of the Kātantra** has been read. Two editions are free on archive.org (Calcutta 1927; Saini 1987), and **Liebich's 1919 monograph** is the standard Western study.
- **Not one sūtra of the Cāndrasūtra.** And the chapter's load-bearing correction in §3 rests on **Timalsina's article, which was located and could not be retrieved** — the publisher's site returned an access-denied page. **The single most important source in this chapter has not been read at first hand.**
- **Not one rule of the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*** — though **Cowell's 1854 edition includes an English translation** and has been out of copyright for a century. This chapter knows the text's shape, attribution, dialects and editorial history, and **nothing about its contents**.
- **No Paribhāṣāpāṭha** in any system. Abhyankar's own *Paribhāṣāsaṃgraha* collects fifteen of them in one volume, and **Kielhorn translated the Paribhāṣenduśekhara into English**.
- The two peer-reviewed articles directly on where paribhāṣās come from — *Asiatische Studien* 2018 — returned **HTTP 405** and **403**.
- **Andrew Ollett's *Language of the Snakes***, the named modern authority on how the grammarians constituted Prākṛt as a language, was located and could not be retrieved.

⚠ **And one specific thing the chapter reports without endorsing:** the claim that the Kātantra shaped Pali and Dravidian grammar is **single-sourced**. If it is right, §1's correction is much larger than stated. If it is wrong, §1 still stands on the manuscript evidence alone.

## §11 What to carry into Chapter 45

This chapter kept running into a wall that is not about grammar: **the corpus could not tell what a text originally contained.** The Cāndra's chapters 7 and 8. The Kātantra's relation to the Kalāpa. Whether the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*'s twelve chapters are its author's. Whether Abhyankar's "six chapters" describes a book or a bookshelf.

[Chapter 45](../../cross-tradition/45-the-witness-problem.md) is about that wall. Its four figures are the **commentators on the Kāśikā and the Vākyapadīya** — and its governing fact, from working editors of the text, is one sentence:
