## How to use this chapter

Chapter 04 introduced the Hindu tradition at the broadest level — brahman, ātman, karma-vedic, mokṣa-advaita. This chapter goes deeper into a specific family: **the Vedānta schools**, all of which trace their authority to exactly the same three texts. Their disagreements are not about which sacred texts to trust — they disagree about what those texts *mean*. This makes Vedānta the clearest case study in the corpus of what philosophers call the *interpretation problem*: authority does not settle philosophical disputes, it relocates them.

The chapter is structured to build in stages. §1 lays the canonical foundation. §2 frames the central question. §§3–5 present the three major answers in order of decreasing "non-duality." §6 covers the three paths that appear across all schools. §7 maps the Hindu deity world that Vedānta theology grounds. §8 draws the cross-tradition contrasts with Jain and Buddhist positions.

**Frequent conflation alert you should know before starting:**

*Vedānta ≠ Advaita.* The word "Vedānta" means "end of the Vedas" (the Upaniṣads) and covers three philosophically incompatible schools. When journalists or popular books say "Vedāntic philosophy means everything is one," they are describing *Advaita Vedānta*, one of three, and implicitly presenting Rāmānuja's and Madhva's opposite conclusions as non-existent. Throughout this chapter, "Vedānta" refers to the family; "Advaita" refers to the specific school.

## 1. The triple canonical base — *prasthānatrayī* {#prasthanatrayi}

*→ concept files: [upanishad.md](../../concepts/upanishad.md) · [brahma-sutra.md](../../concepts/brahma-sutra.md)*

Every Vedānta school accepts exactly three texts as its scriptural authority — the *prasthānatrayī* ("triple canonical base"):

| text | Sanskrit name | content | approx. date |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Principal Upaniṣads | *mukhya-upaniṣad* (12 by Vedānta consensus) | direct revelation (*śruti*) — the philosophical core of the Veda | 800–200 BCE |
| The Brahmasūtra | *Brahmasūtra* or *Vedāntasūtra* (Bādarāyaṇa, ~200 CE) | 555 aphoristic sūtras systematising the Upaniṣads | ~200 CE |
| The Bhagavad Gītā | *Bhagavad Gītā* (BG) | 700 verses; Kṛṣṇa's teaching to Arjuna; part of the Mahābhārata | ~200 BCE–200 CE |

Bādarāyaṇa's BS 1.1.1 opens: *"then, therefore, the desire to know Brahman"* (*athāto brahmajijñāsā*). BS 1.1.2 identifies Brahman as "the source of the origin, etc. of this world" (*janmādyasya yataḥ*). These two sūtras are the most-commented sentences in Indian philosophy — Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, and Madhva each wrote a substantial *bhāṣya* (commentary) on the entire 555 sūtras, and the three commentaries reach opposite conclusions.

**The Upaniṣads contain four *mahāvākyas* (great sayings)** that each school interprets differently:

| mahāvākya | source | literal meaning | Advaita reads | Viśiṣṭādvaita reads | Dvaita reads |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *prajñānam brahma* | Aitareya Up. 3.3 | "consciousness is Brahman" | identity | Brahman's essential nature is consciousness | analogical, not strict identity |
| *aham brahmāsmi* | Bṛhadāraṇyaka Up. 1.4.10 | "I am Brahman" | strict: ātman=Brahman | "I belong to Brahman" (body-soul) | inappropriate pride if taken literally |
| *tat tvam asi* | Chāndogya Up. 6.8.7 | "that thou art" | you (ātman) = that (Brahman) | "that of which you are the body" | God and soul share *sattva* quality, not identity |
| *ayam ātmā brahma* | Māṇḍūkya Up. 1.2 | "this self is Brahman" | direct non-duality | the individual self has Brahman as its inner controller | relational: the self belongs to Brahman |

The same four sentences. Three mutually incompatible readings. The dispute cannot be resolved by citing more of the same texts.

## 2. The central question {#central-question}

*→ concept files: [brahman.md](../../concepts/brahman.md) · [atman-vedanta.md](../../concepts/atman-vedanta.md)*

All three schools start from the same two concepts:

**Brahman** (*neuter; from √bṛh,* "to grow / be great"): the ultimate metaphysical absolute — infinite, eternal, self-subsisting. The Upaniṣadic formula: *Sat-Cit-Ānanda* — being, consciousness, bliss. Brahman is *not* a personal creator-God (that is Brahmā, the Trimūrti deity); Brahman is the *ground of all being*.

**Ātman**: the self — the inner conscious subject within every person. The Upaniṣads repeatedly assert that the ātman is the real self beneath the body-mind complex. The philosophically explosive claim: *the ātman is identical to Brahman*.

The Vedānta schools disagree on three things:
1. **Is the ātman-Brahman identity strict or qualified?**
2. **Is the phenomenal world real or apparent?**
3. **What mechanism links Brahman, ātman, and world?**

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Vedānta *ātman* ≠ Jain *jīva***
>
> Both are individual conscious selves that survive physical death. But:
> - **Jain *jīva***: genuinely individual, permanently distinct from all other jīvas and from any cosmic absolute. Liberation is the individual jīva's own achievement at the universe's summit (*siddha-śilā*).
> - **Vedānta *ātman***: in Advaita, ultimately non-different from the single Brahman — "individual" identity is māyā. Even in Viśiṣṭādvaita/Dvaita (where individuality is preserved), the ātman is *eternally dependent on Brahman* in a way Jain jīvas are not.
>
> The deepest structural difference: Jainism is **pluralist** (irreducibly many real jīvas, no absolute subsuming them); Advaita Vedānta is **monist** (ultimately one Brahman only). Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita preserve real plurality but within Brahman's framework — still incompatible with the Jain position. See [jiva.md](../../concepts/jiva.md) and [atman-vedanta.md](../../concepts/atman-vedanta.md).

## 3. Advaita Vedānta — Śaṅkara's non-dualism {#advaita}

*→ concept files: [advaita-vedanta.md](../../concepts/advaita-vedanta.md) · [maya-advaita.md](../../concepts/maya-advaita.md) · [vivartavada.md](../../concepts/vivartavada.md) · [moksha-advaita.md](../../concepts/moksha-advaita.md)*

Śaṅkara (~700–750 CE) is the systematiser of **Advaita** ("non-two"). His key claims:

1. **Brahman alone is ultimately real** — pure, undifferentiated consciousness without properties (*nirguṇa*) and without any second thing.
2. **The ātman is *identical* to Brahman** — not similar, not connected, but strictly the same. The four mahāvākyas say this directly.
3. **The world and individual selves are not ultimately real** — they appear due to *māyā* (cosmic appearance-power) and *avidyā* (ignorance at the individual level). This is not the same as saying the world does not exist in practical experience — it means the world does not possess *ultimate ontological independence*.

### 3.1 Māyā — the hardest Advaita concept {#maya}

*anirvachanīya* ("indescribable") is Śaṅkara's word for māyā's status: **neither real nor unreal nor both**. The rope-snake analogy: you see a coiled rope in dim light and mistake it for a snake. The snake has experiential presence but no independent reality. When you bring a lamp (= jñāna), the snake disappears and only the rope remains. The world has this *prātibhāsika* (appearance-level) reality.

**Critical scholarly point (corpus source: maya-advaita.md):** The *āvaraṇa/vikṣepa* (concealment/projection) two-function analysis of māyā does NOT appear in Śaṅkara's own *Brahmasūtra Bhāṣya* — this is a post-Śaṅkara elaboration. Śaṅkara's own usage is the *anirvachanīya* formula. Writers who present the two-function model as "Śaṅkara's māyā" are conflating the school with its later commentators.

### 3.2 Vivartavāda — appearance-transformation {#vivarta}

*→ concept file: [vivartavada.md](../../concepts/vivartavada.md)*

The world *appears to change* but does not actually change — like the rope "becoming" the snake. This is **vivartavāda** (appearance-without-real-transformation). It contrasts with pariṇāmavāda (§4.1 below), which holds that Brahman/Prakṛti undergoes *real* transformation into the world. Vivartavāda is Advaita's position on causation: the cause (Brahman) remains unchanged; only the *appearance* of an effect arises.

**Attribution caveat (corpus source: vivartavada.md):** Scholars including Paul Hacker and Nicholson dispute whether Śaṅkara himself held a consistent vivartavāda. The full-blown vivartavāda doctrine may be a post-Śaṅkara systematisation.

### 3.3 Liberation in Advaita — mokṣa as epistemic shift {#moksha-advaita}

*→ concept file: [moksha-advaita.md](../../concepts/moksha-advaita.md)*

Mokṣa is not a journey to a place or a new state — it is the *removal of avidyā* that was falsely making Brahman appear as multiple individual selves. "Liberation" is realising what was always already the case: ātman = Brahman. This can happen *while living* (*jīvanmukti*) — the liberated person continues to act in the world, but the ignorance that drove identification with the body-mind is dissolved.

The means: the three stages of *śravaṇa* (hearing the mahāvākyas from a teacher) → *manana* (sustained rational reflection) → *nididhyāsana* (deep contemplative absorption). This is the **jñāna-mārga** (knowledge path; §6.1 below) as Śaṅkara's preferred route.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Advaita *mokṣa* ≠ Jain *mokṣa***
>
> Both use the word *mokṣa* for liberation. The ontological destinations are opposite:
> - **Advaita**: the individual ātman merges back into Brahman — individuality dissolves.
> - **Jain**: the individual jīva reaches the *siddha-śilā* at the universe's summit — permanently individual, with its own distinct knowledge/bliss/consciousness. No merger, no dissolution.
>
> Jain soteriology is *pluralist-destination*; Advaita soteriology is *monist-dissolution*. See [moksha.md](../../concepts/moksha.md) vs [moksha-advaita.md](../../concepts/moksha-advaita.md).

## 4. Viśiṣṭādvaita — Rāmānuja's qualified non-dualism {#vishishtadvaita}

*→ concept files: [vishishtadvaita.md](../../concepts/vishishtadvaita.md) · [parinamavada.md](../../concepts/parinamavada.md)*

Rāmānuja (~1017–1137 CE) accepted that the Upaniṣads teach non-duality — but argued that Śaṅkara's reading destroys devotion (*bhakti*). You cannot devotionally love a featureless absolute. His alternative: **Viśiṣṭādvaita** ("qualified non-two") — Brahman is *one*, but a *qualified* one, internally differentiated:

- **Brahman (= Īśvara, personal God)** is the only truly self-subsisting reality.
- **Individual selves (*cit*)** and **matter (*acit*)** are real, but they exist as Brahman's *body* (*śarīra*). Brahman is the *śarīrin* (soul to the body); selves and matter are the *śarīra* (body to the soul).

The body-soul (*śarīra-śarīrin*) relation is the key metaphor: just as the ātman and body are distinct yet inseparable, so Brahman and the world of selves-and-matter are distinct yet inseparable aspects of one reality. This preserves real individuality (against Advaita's dissolution) while maintaining unity.

### 4.1 Pariṇāmavāda — real transformation {#parinama}

*→ concept file: [parinamavada.md](../../concepts/parinamavada.md)*

Against Advaita's *vivartavāda*, Rāmānuja holds **pariṇāmavāda**: Brahman/Śakti *really* transforms into the world. The effect is genuinely new; the cause genuinely changes (while Brahman's essential nature remains). This grounds real devotion — the world is genuinely Brahman's expression, not an illusion to be seen through.

Pariṇāmavāda has three variants:
| variant | holder | what transforms |
|---|---|---|
| Sāṃkhya pariṇāmavāda | Sāṃkhya | Prakṛti transforms (Puruṣa unchanged) |
| Brahma-pariṇāmavāda | Rāmānuja | Brahman-as-qualified-whole transforms |
| Śakti-pariṇāmavāda | Śaiva Siddhānta | Śakti (not Śiva's essential nature) transforms |

### 4.2 Liberation in Viśiṣṭādvaita {#moksha-vishishtadvaita}

Mokṣa is **eternal participation in Brahman** — the liberated soul dwells in Brahman's presence with full consciousness and bliss, but retains its distinct identity. The mechanism is *bhakti* (loving devotion) + *prapatti* (surrender). Lakṣmī as mediator: devotion to Lakṣmī (*Śrī*) is the gateway to Viṣṇu (Brahman) — she intercedes on the devotee's behalf. See [lakshmi.md](../../concepts/lakshmi.md).

## 5. Dvaita Vedānta — Madhva's dualism {#dvaita}

*→ concept file: [dvaita-vedanta.md](../../concepts/dvaita-vedanta.md)*

Madhva (~1238–1317 CE) is the most counter-Advaita: he holds **Dvaita** ("two-ness") — Brahman/Viṣṇu and individual souls are **eternally, ontologically distinct**. His *pañcabheda* (five eternal differences):

| pair | difference |
|---|---|
| Viṣṇu ↔ individual soul | Lord and dependent — eternally distinct |
| Viṣṇu ↔ matter | Lord and world — eternally distinct |
| Individual soul ↔ individual soul | each soul distinct from every other |
| Individual soul ↔ matter | each soul distinct from matter |
| Matter ↔ matter | each material thing distinct from other things |

**The bimba/pratibimba doctrine**: individual souls are *reflections* (*pratibimba*) of Brahman/Viṣṇu (*bimba*), not fragments or modes. Reflections are real but always remain distinct from what they reflect.

**Controversial: eternal damnation.** Madhva is unique in Indian philosophy in positing a class of souls (*tāmasa-jīvas*) destined for eternal hell — they cannot be liberated. Most Vedānta and Buddhist traditions hold that all beings have liberation potential.

**Liberation in Dvaita**: mokṣa = **eternal service of Viṣṇu** in his heavenly realm. The soul retains full individuality and consciousness but is entirely dependent on Viṣṇu's grace. The path is devotion (*bhakti*) + direct knowledge (*aparokṣa-jñāna*) of the five differences.

> ⚠️ **Dvaita is the closest Vedānta analogue to Jain pluralism** — real, eternal, irreducible individual souls. But they are not the same:
> - Jain jīvas are **self-sufficient** — they liberate through their own effort in a creator-less cosmos.
> - Madhva's souls are **eternally dependent** on Viṣṇu's grace — they cannot liberate by their own power.
> The contrast between *self-effort* and *divine-grace* as the mechanism of liberation is one of the deepest divides in Indian soteriology.

## 6. The three paths — *trimārga* {#trimarga}

*→ concept files: [jnana-marga.md](../../concepts/jnana-marga.md) · [karma-marga.md](../../concepts/karma-marga.md) · [bhakti.md](../../concepts/bhakti.md)*

The Bhagavad Gītā (and the broader Vedānta tradition) recognises three ways of approaching liberation:

| path | Sanskrit | key text | primary school | mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge path | *jñāna-mārga* | BG 4.38; BṛU 4 (*śravaṇa/manana/nididhyāsana*) | Advaita | removal of *avidyā* (ignorance) through hearing, reflection, meditation |
| Action path | *karma-mārga* | BG 2.47–49 + 3.19 (*naiṣkāmya-karma*) | Mīmāṃsā; accessible to all | action without attachment to results — *naiṣkāmya-karma* (desireless action) |
| Devotion path | *bhakti-mārga* | BG Chapter 12; ŚvetUp 6.23 | Viśiṣṭādvaita; Dvaita | loving devotion to a personal God; complete surrender (*prapatti*) |

The BG presents all three but is read differently by each school:
- **Advaita** (Śaṅkara): the BG ultimately points to *jñāna* as the highest; devotion and action are secondary supports.
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita** (Rāmānuja): *bhakti* is the primary path; *jñāna* is bhakti's prerequisite, and karma-yoga purifies for bhakti.
- **Dvaita** (Madhva): devotion + direct knowledge together; Viṣṇu's grace is indispensable.

> **The karma-mārga name-trap**: The BG's *karma-mārga* is a *path to liberation through desireless action*, not "a path that produces karma." The two uses of "karma" are opposite: karma as *bondage* (karma-bandha in the Jain/Hindu soteriological sense) vs karma as *right action in the world* (the BG's karma-mārga). Performing the karma-mārga — if done without attachment — reduces karma-bondage, not increases it. See [karma-marga.md](../../concepts/karma-marga.md).

## 7. The Hindu deity world {#deity-world}

*→ concept files: [trimurti.md](../../concepts/trimurti.md) · [vishnu.md](../../concepts/vishnu.md) · [brahma.md](../../concepts/brahma.md) · [sarasvati.md](../../concepts/sarasvati.md) · [lakshmi.md](../../concepts/lakshmi.md) · [avatara-vedanta.md](../../concepts/avatara-vedanta.md) · [shaivism.md](../../concepts/shaivism.md) · [vaishnavism.md](../../concepts/vaishnavism.md)*

The Vedānta schools do not exist in a theological vacuum — they operate within a rich pantheon. The two major organising frameworks:

### 7.1 Trimūrti and Tridevi {#trimurti-tridevi}

The **Trimūrti** (Brahmā / Viṣṇu / Śiva) assigns three cosmic functions:

| deity | function | devotional tradition | consort (Tridevi) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brahmā** | creation | none major (rarely worshipped) | Sarasvatī (knowledge) |
| **Viṣṇu** | preservation | Vaiṣṇavism (~1 billion adherents) | Lakṣmī (prosperity) |
| **Śiva** | dissolution | Śaivism (~260 million) | Pārvatī / Śakti (power) |

**Critical point**: the Trimūrti is a Purāṇic synthesis (~4th–12th c. CE), *not* an ancient Vedic doctrine. Most devotional schools do not accept it as their primary theological framework — Vaiṣṇavas hold that Viṣṇu is the supreme, not just the preserver; Śaivas hold that Śiva performs all five cosmic actions. The Trimūrti is most at home in **Smārta** theology (Śaṅkara's synthesis treating all five deities as forms of Brahman). See [trimurti.md](../../concepts/trimurti.md).

### 7.2 Avatāra doctrine — Viṣṇu's descent {#avatara}

*→ concept file: [avatara-vedanta.md](../../concepts/avatara-vedanta.md)*

BG 4.7–4.8 (Kṛṣṇa speaks):
> *"Whenever righteousness (*dharma*) declines and unrighteousness rises, I send forth myself."*
> *"For the protection of the good, for the destruction of the wicked, for the sake of establishing *dharma*, I come into being age after age."*

Viṣṇu "descends" (*avatārate*) into the world in embodied forms. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa lists 10 principal descents (Daśāvatāra): Matsya, Kūrma, Varāha, Narasiṃha, Vāmana, Paraśurāma, Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Buddha, Kalkin. The avatāra doctrine is the theological *answer* to cosmic crisis — Brahman/Viṣṇu is not absent from history but actively intervenes.

> ⚠️ **Avatāra ≠ Tīrthaṅkara**
>
> Both are revered figures who appear regularly to restore right conduct. But the structure is opposite:
> - **Avatāra**: the *supreme deity himself descends* into matter and history to intervene.
> - **Tīrthaṅkara**: a *human being*, through their own effort, ascends to omniscience and liberation. No deity is involved; no one "descends." The universe is beginningless and self-regulating.
>
> One tradition's holy figures are divine descents; the other's are human achievements. The conflation is irresistible and wrong. See [tirthankara.md](../../concepts/tirthankara.md) vs [avatara-vedanta.md](../../concepts/avatara-vedanta.md).

## 8. Cross-tradition: Vedānta, Jain, and Buddhist {#cross-tradition}

The three major Indian philosophical traditions now have enough coverage to state the key structural contrasts:

| question | Advaita Vedānta | Jain | Theravāda Buddhist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ultimate reality | One Brahman (conscious, without parts) | Six real substances (plural, eternal) | No single ultimate; dependent arising only |
| Status of world | Appears real, ultimately reducible to Brahman | Real — six substances interact | Conventionally real, empty of self-nature |
| Individual self | Ultimately = Brahman (ātman=Brahman) | Real, permanently distinct jīva | No permanent self (anattā) |
| Liberation | Identity-realisation (ātman=Brahman recognised) | Individual jīva's own effort, at siddha-śilā | Cessation of craving; nibbāna |
| Creator-God? | No personal creator in Advaita; Saguna Brahman in theism | No — universe is beginningless and self-regulating | No — dependent origination, not creation |
| Primary path | Jñāna (Advaita); Bhakti (Vaiṣṇava) | Right faith + knowledge + conduct (ratnatraya) | Noble Eightfold Path |

These three rows of "ultimate reality / individual self / liberation" are the diagnostic questions the corpus uses to map traditions. Every tradition has an answer to each. No two of the three agree.
