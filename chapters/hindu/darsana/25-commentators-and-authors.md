## How to use this chapter

Chapter 24 closed with a section (§10) on commentary as a philosophical form, and made a claim in passing that this chapter exists to cash out: **in both the Greek and the Indian worlds, for centuries, the way to do original philosophy was to write about someone else's book.**

That raises a question the doctrinal chapters could postpone and this one cannot. If the philosophy lives in the commentary, then *who the commentator was* is a first-order philosophical question — not a bibliographic footnote. And when you ask it of the six figures here, the answers degrade in a remarkably orderly way.

Start with **Vātsyāyana** (§2), where three independent sources agree and the only trouble is a surname shared with the author of the *Kāmasūtra*. Then **Śaṅkara** (§3), whose works are securely known and whose *life* is a 14th-century construction. Then **Maṇḍana Miśra** and **Sureśvara** (§4–§6), two philosophers whom one tradition says are one philosopher. Then the **"Vyāsa" of the *Yoga-Bhāṣya*** (§7), who may never have existed. And finally **Vindhyavāsin** (§8), who exists only in the writings of the Buddhists he defeated.

Six figures, six different ways of dissolving, one scale from *secure* to *barely there*. §9 tabulates them; §10 turns the result back on this corpus's own method, which treats commentary as its anchor signal; §11 sets the Indian pattern against the Greek one from Ch 24 and finds the two civilisations failing in *different* ways.

This chapter assumes Ch 11 (Advaita's doctrine — here you get its *people*), and reads better after Ch 16 (Nyāya), Ch 15 (Sāṃkhya-Yoga), Ch 17 (Mīmāṃsā), and especially Ch 24 §13. Leave `> stuck` on its own line where you get lost; paste the teaching prompt and it will re-explain in place.

## 1. Why the commentator is the philosopher {#commentary}

A *sūtra* is not a book in the sense we mean. It is a string of compressed mnemonic aphorisms — sometimes two or three words — designed to be memorised and then unpacked by a teacher. Read cold, a sūtra text is very nearly unreadable, and it was never meant to be read cold.

So the tradition builds layers on top:

| layer | what it is |
|---|---|
| ***mūla*** / *sūtra* | the root aphorisms — terse to the point of opacity |
| ***bhāṣya*** | the primary commentary; supplies the argument, the opponents, the worked examples |
| ***vārttika*** | a critical examination of a bhāṣya, line by line |
| ***ṭīkā*** | sub-commentary on any of the above, and so on downward |

The consequence is the premise of this chapter. **The philosophy you can actually read is in the bhāṣya.** Gautama's *Nyāya Sūtra* is a list of topics; Vātsyāyana's *Nyāya-Bhāṣya* is an epistemology. Bādarāyaṇa's *Brahma-Sūtra* is famously so compressed that four incompatible Vedānta schools each claim to be reading it straight (Ch 11); what Advaita actually asserts, it asserts in Śaṅkara's *bhāṣya*. Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtra* gets its metaphysics from the *Yoga-Bhāṣya*.

This is also why CLAUDE.md's trust method makes commentary its **second signal and its anchor** — a reading that skips the commentarial tradition and reads modern meaning off bare Sanskrit is flagged as a red flag. The method is sound. But it has a presupposition, and §10 returns to it: *the anchor holds only as firmly as the commentator's identity does.*

One clarification before the cases. "Commentary" here does not mean derivative work. Sureśvara's *vārttikas* argue with the master they gloss. Vātsyāyana sets the agenda for a thousand years of Nyāya. And the most consequential fact about the *Yoga-Bhāṣya* is that scholars cannot decide whether it is a commentary at all, or the second half of the work it comments on (§7). The form is capacious enough to hide almost anything.

## 2. Vātsyāyana — the control case {#vatsyayana}

*→ concept file: [vatsyayana.md](../../../concepts/vatsyayana.md) — `converged`, confidence **high** (the only high-confidence node in this chapter)*

Begin with what a *solid* attribution looks like, so the later dissolutions have something to be measured against.

**Pakṣilasvāmin Vātsyāyana** (c. 5th c. CE) wrote the ***Nyāya-Bhāṣya***, the **earliest extant commentary** on Gautama Akṣapāda's *Nyāya Sūtra*. What he did with it is exactly the §1 pattern: the bhāṣya turns terse aphorisms into a working epistemology of the **pramāṇas** — perception, inference, comparison, testimony — and sets the agenda that the entire later Nyāya tradition (Uddyotakara, Vācaspati Miśra, and onward) proceeds to gloss. He is the layer through which the *Nyāya Sūtra* became a living philosophical system rather than a syllabus.

**Why the confidence is high.** Three genuinely independent sources agree on the attribution:

1. **Matthew R. Dasti**, *Vātsyāyana's Commentary on the Nyāya-sūtra: A Guide* (Oxford UP, **2023**) — a dedicated critical monograph;
2. **Mrinalkanti Gangopadhyaya** (tr.), *Nyāya Philosophy: Gautama's Nyāyasūtra and Vātsyāyana's Bhāṣya* (1967) — an independent translation and critical edition;
3. **Wikipedia**, corroborating.

A 2023 Oxford monograph and a 1967 Calcutta translation are not reworkings of one another. That is what independence looks like, and it is why this node clears a bar that five of its neighbours do not. Note what is *still* loose even here: the **dating** is only "c. 5th century" (he must post-date Gautama's sūtra, c. 150 CE), and older summaries pushed him earlier. Loose dating does not touch the attribution — a distinction worth keeping, because §3 shows the same pattern at much greater scale.

**The one real trap, and the repo's policy on it.**

> ⚠️ **This Vātsyāyana is not the author of the *Kāmasūtra*.** *That* Vātsyāyana is **Vātsyāyana Mallanāga** — different person, different work, shared surname. The confusion is common enough that the concept file records it in prose.

The corpus's handling is worth stating because it is a general policy: the Kāmasūtra author gets **no node** (out of scope), so the caution lives in prose on the node that does exist, and the bare key `vatsyayana` is reserved for the logician. If the other were ever added, it would have to take a disambiguating key. This follows the **`patañjali` precedent** (Ch 15): a persistent tradition identifies the Yoga-sūtra Patañjali with the *grammarian* Patañjali of the *Mahābhāṣya*, and the identification is almost certainly false — Louis Renou showed significant differences in language, grammar and vocabulary, and the equation is first made only in **Bhojadeva's *Rājamārtaṇḍa*, around the 10th century**. There too, no node exists for the grammarian, so the caution is recorded in prose, because *a typed edge needs a target*.

That last clause is a small but real constraint on how this project's honesty layer works. The graph can only draw a `NOT-equivalent` line between two nodes it has. Where one side of a confusion is out of scope, the warning has to live in prose or it does not exist at all — and a reader who only ever looks at the rendered graph will not see it. Two of this chapter's most common confusions (Kāmasūtra-Vātsyāyana, grammarian-Patañjali) are invisible to the graph for exactly this reason.

**Keep the shape of this case in mind:** *the works are secure; the dates are loose; the only confusion is a name-collision with someone out of scope.* Everything that follows is a departure from this baseline in one direction or another.

## 3. Śaṅkara — two layers with two different evidential standings {#shankara}

*→ concept file: [shankara.md](../../../concepts/shankara.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium***

**Śaṅkara** (Ādi Śaṅkara, Śaṅkarācārya) is, in SEP's phrase, "the most authoritative philosopher of Advaita Vedānta" — the systematiser standing behind almost every Vedānta concept in this corpus: [advaita-vedanta](../../../concepts/advaita-vedanta.md), [atman-vedanta](../../../concepts/atman-vedanta.md), [brahman](../../../concepts/brahman.md), [maya-advaita](../../../concepts/maya-advaita.md), [moksha-advaita](../../../concepts/moksha-advaita.md), [vivartavada](../../../concepts/vivartavada.md). Chapter 11 was written with his name all over it and no node beneath it; the node was finally written in the corpus's most recent concept batch, which is why this chapter is where he gets a chapter home.

The finding on him is not about his doctrine. It is that **his philosophical authority and his biography are two separate layers with two very different evidential standings**, and that almost every popular account fuses them.

### 3.1 Layer one: the works, which are reasonably secure

Both independent sources converge on the authentic list:

| authentic (converged) | the problem |
|---|---|
| **Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya** — the masterpiece | over **300 further texts** travel under his name |
| bhāṣyas on the **ten principal Upaniṣads** | "most of these are not authentic works of Shankara" |
| bhāṣya on the **Bhagavad Gītā** | SEP: many likely "composed in later centuries by monastic heads" holding the *Śaṅkarācārya* title |
| **Upadeśasāhasrī** — one independent treatise | — |

Note the mechanism in that right-hand column, because it is specific and it explains the number. **Śaṅkarācārya is an office, not only a person.** The heads of the maṭhas bear the title, and works composed by later title-holders accumulate under the name of the first. This is not forgery in the Pseudo-Dionysius sense (Ch 24 §12); it is an institutional title doing what institutional titles do to attribution over a thousand years.

> ⚠️ **A live caution for this repository, not a general remark.** The much-loved **Vivekacūḍāmaṇi** is "questioned and mostly rejected by scholarship" as Śaṅkara's. This matters here specifically: **two existing concept files — `atman-vedanta.md` and `brahman.md` — list the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi as a future confidence-upgrade fetch, *as Śaṅkara's*.** When that fetch happens it must be treated as **attributed**, not authentic, or the upgrade will import a misattribution into two of the corpus's most-linked nodes. This is recorded in `shankara.md` and repeated here so it is visible from the teaching layer too.

### 3.2 Layer two: the life, which is very largely construction

"Reliable information on Shankara's actual life is scanty." Set against that, consider what the standard biography contains: birth at **Kālaḍi** in Kerala; the *digvijaya*, the "conquest of the quarters," a tour of India defeating all rivals in debate; the founding of the **four cardinal maṭhas** (Dvārakā, Purī, Śṛṅgeri, Badrī); and the celebrated **debate with Maṇḍana Miśra**, who loses, converts, and becomes his disciple Sureśvara (§6).

Where does that come from? From **at least fourteen hagiographies written centuries to a millennium after his death** — "politically motivated," and "abound[ing] in legends and improbable events." The most-cited of them, **Mādhava's *Śaṅkaradigvijaya*, is 17th-century**. That is a gap of roughly nine hundred years between subject and source.

And the historical record points the other way:

> **Śaṅkara "was relatively unknown during his life-time, and probably for several centuries after."** He goes unmentioned by Buddhist, Jain, and 9th–10th-century Hindu philosophers. In his own period he was "probably **overshadowed by his older contemporary Maṇḍana Miśra**," whose *Brahmasiddhi* was then the normative Advaita treatise.

His towering fame is datable, and it is late. It is substantially a **construction of the 14th-century Vijayanagara period** — Vidyāraṇya's promotion of him as a "divine folk-hero" — and it was amplified again in the 19th–20th centuries by Neo-Vedānta and by Western Orientalism, which wanted a single towering Indian philosopher and found one ready-made.

### 3.3 The dating, which is a genuine spread

| claim | who holds it |
|---|---|
| **788–820 CE** | early Indological scholarship |
| **c. 700–750 CE** | Nakamura; the modern mainstream (both sources) |
| late 7th–early 8th c. | Potter (arguing via Maṇḍana Miśra's dating) |
| **5th century BCE** | the traditional Advaita maṭhas' own chronology |

The last row is worth pausing on rather than dismissing. It is not a rounding error; it is off by **twelve hundred years**. And it is not an accident either: an earlier Śaṅkara is a Śaṅkara who precedes and therefore cannot have borrowed from the Buddhists — which bears directly on the *pracchanna-bauddha* charge (§3.4). A dating claim is doing doctrinal work. The corpus records the maṭhas' chronology as a position held rather than an error to be corrected, and records why the mainstream does not hold it.

### 3.4 The crypto-Buddhist charge

Vaiṣṇava opponents called Śaṅkara a ***pracchanna-bauddha*** — a "Buddhist in disguise." The charge has a real basis: his Advaita "showed influences from Mahāyāna Buddhism, despite Shankara's critiques" — he was, after all, the intellectual descendant (through Govinda) of **Gauḍapāda**, whose *Māṇḍūkya-kārikā* is saturated with Buddhist vocabulary.

The tradition's rebuttal is doctrinal rather than genealogical, and the corpus has already typed it as an edge on `advaita-vedanta`: **a positive, permanent Brahman is not śūnyatā's denial of any self-existent substrate** (Ch 12). Influence is not identity. The reply is a good one — and note that it concedes the influence in order to locate the difference precisely, which is the same move this project's §0 asks for in every direction.

### 3.5 What to take from the Śaṅkara case

Not "Śaṅkara is overrated." The *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* is what it is regardless of when its author became famous, and the doctrine Ch 11 sets out stands on the text. What the case shows is that **philosophical stature and historical fame are produced by different machinery, and can come apart by a thousand years.** The bhāṣyas were written in the 8th century; the reputation was built in the 14th and rebuilt in the 20th. Someone who takes the *Śaṅkaradigvijaya* as biography is not reading a source about Śaṅkara — they are reading a source about what Vijayanagara-era Advaita needed Śaṅkara to have been.

The corpus's structural comparison files this alongside its Jain counterpart: `shankara structurally-parallel-to kundakunda` — **each tradition's most authoritative philosopher-monk, whose attributed corpus far exceeds his authentic one, and whose biography is hagiographic accretion around a thin historical core** (Ch 10). The parallel is about the *shape of the record*, not about the doctrines, which have almost nothing in common.

And one flag that is pure name-collision, drawn because the graph should teach it:

> ⚠️ **Śaṅkara the philosopher is not Śaṅkara the god.** *Śaṅkara* ("beneficent") is a standard name of **Śiva** (Ch 18), and devotional literature exploits the pun freely — the philosopher is even said in the hagiographies to be an incarnation of Śiva. The corpus stores `shankara often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent shiva` for exactly this reason. Note how the hagiographic move and the name work together: the pun is not decoration, it is the vehicle of the divinisation.

## 4. Maṇḍana Miśra — the rival who may have been primary {#mandana}

*→ concept file: [mandana-mishra.md](../../../concepts/mandana-mishra.md) — `contested`, confidence **medium***

**Maṇḍana Miśra** (fl. 9th c. CE) is the figure the standard story turns into a foil, and the record suggests he was something more.

**What he did.** He is the philosopher who **bridges Mīmāṃsā and Advaita Vedānta**: a student of the great Mīmāṃsaka **Kumārila Bhaṭṭa** (Ch 17) who then wrote the ***Brahmasiddhi*** — "the establishment of Brahman" — a foundational Advaita treatise. He also defended the *sphoṭa* theory of language in the *Sphoṭasiddhi*, and wrote the *Bhāvanāviveka*. That trajectory alone is notable: the ritualist school's most rigorous product moving to non-dualism, and taking his technical apparatus with him.

**His distinctive doctrine.** Maṇḍana holds that *avidyā* (ignorance) is ***anirvacanīya*** — inexpressible, neither real nor unreal — and, crucially, that **the individual *jīva* is its locus**. That is a different account from Śaṅkara's, and §5 shows how much turns on it.

**The claim that reframes the period.** A scholarly thesis holds that **until the 10th century it was Maṇḍana, not Śaṅkara, who was the dominant Advaitin** — his *Brahmasiddhi*, not the *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya*, being the normative treatise of the school. Set beside §3.2's "relatively unknown during his life-time," the two findings are the same finding seen from two sides.

The corpus marks the node `contested` on two questions:

| question | the split | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Is Maṇḍana = Sureśvara?** | tradition: he lost the debate to Śaṅkara and *became* Sureśvara · scholarship: "authenticity is doubtful," many hold them **distinct** authors with incompatible doctrines | one converted person, or two lifelong opponents (§6) |
| **Whose Advaita is primary?** | the debate-legend subordinates Maṇḍana to Śaṅkara · the textual record suggests he may have been **more influential for a century** | either a hierarchy the tradition asserts, or one the evidence inverts |

Notice that these two questions are not independent. **The debate-conversion legend does exactly the work that the primacy question would otherwise leave undone.** If Maṇḍana was the more important Advaitin of the 9th century, then a story in which he is defeated by Śaṅkara and absorbed into Śaṅkara's lineage as a disciple resolves the awkwardness completely: the rival becomes a trophy, and his authority becomes Śaṅkara's authority. This chapter does not claim the legend was fabricated for that purpose — that would be an assertion beyond the sources. It observes that the legend has that effect, and that its earliest full attestations are the same late hagiographies §3.2 already discounted.

## 5. Sureśvara — the Vārttikakāra, and where ignorance lives {#sureshvara}

*→ concept file: [sureshvara.md](../../../concepts/sureshvara.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium***

**Sureśvara** (fl. 8th–9th c. — see the tension below) is a **direct disciple of Śaṅkara** and the most prolific writer among his immediate pupils: the tradition's ***Vārttikakāra***, "meticulously and critically examining Śaṅkara's work." Tradition holds that Śaṅkara entrusted to him his first monastic institution, the **Sringeri Sharada Peetham**.

**Works.** The ***Naiṣkarmyasiddhi*** ("The Attainment of Actionlessness") — an independent treatise of **423 verses in four chapters** distilling Advaita, whose third chapter treats the locus and object of ignorance and analyses the mahāvākya ***tat tvam asi*** — plus massive ***vārttikas*** on Śaṅkara's Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Taittirīya bhāṣyas, and minor works (the *Pañcīkaraṇa-vārttika*, a *Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra* commentary, the *Manasollāsa*). His exegetical school, the **Vārttika-prasthāna**, is one of the two channels (with Padmapāda's) through which Advaita was transmitted.

Look at what "commentary" means here: a *vārttika* is a line-by-line critical examination **of a bhāṣya**. Sureśvara is writing commentary on commentary, and the *Naiṣkarmyasiddhi* — his most-read work — is an independent treatise besides. §1's point, at its most concrete.

### 5.1 The locus of avidyā — a technical dispute with everything riding on it

Here is the question, and it is worth setting up carefully because the answer divides Advaita into lineages that persist for a millennium.

Advaita says the world-appearance rests on **ignorance** (*avidyā*). Fine — but ignorance is always someone's ignorance *of* something. So there are two distinct slots to fill:

- the ***viṣaya***, the **object** of ignorance: what is it ignorance *of*?
- the ***āśraya***, the **locus** of ignorance: where does it *reside*?

**All parties agree on the object: Brahman.** Ignorance is ignorance of Brahman. The fight is entirely about the locus:

| | **Maṇḍana Miśra** (and the later Bhāmatī line) | **Sureśvara** |
|---|---|---|
| **locus of avidyā** | the individual **jīva** | **Brahman itself** — "avidyā regarding brahman is located in brahman," and Sureśvara claims this is "in accordance with the Bhāṣya" |
| **role of meditation** | meditative realisation is **required** | liberating knowledge is **directly** liberating; meditation is "at best a useful aid" |

Why does the locus matter so much? Because of a dilemma that has no comfortable horn.

**Put avidyā in the jīva**, and you owe an account of where the jīva came from — since on Advaita the jīva's separateness is *itself* a product of avidyā. Ignorance now presupposes the very thing it was invoked to explain.

**Put avidyā in Brahman**, and you have located ignorance in the pure, non-dual, self-luminous consciousness that Advaita says is the one reality — which sounds close to incoherent, and is exactly what the opposing side presses.

Sureśvara takes the second horn and grounds it in the master's text. The Bhāmatī line takes the first. Neither is a lazy option, and the persistence of the argument is evidence that the dilemma is real rather than a failure of one side to think clearly.

The second row of the table has the more visible consequence. **If knowledge is directly liberating, then hearing and understanding the mahāvākya *is* the event** — no further practice required. If meditative realisation is required, then Advaita needs a *sādhana*, a discipline. That is the difference between a philosophy and a path, and both descriptions of Advaita are in circulation today because both positions were held by its founding generation.

### 5.2 The dating tension, flagged rather than fixed

> ⚠️ Wikipedia dates Sureśvara **800–900 CE**, "a 9th-century philosopher," while also calling him Śaṅkara's **direct disciple** — under a Śaṅkara now standardly dated **c. 700–750**. Other reference streams simply say "8th century."

A direct disciple born after his master's likely death is not impossible to explain away, but nothing in the sources explains it. The corpus records the discrepancy as **a symptom of how soft all these dates are**, not as a resolvable fact. It is a small thing that tells you something large: the chronological frame inside which the entire Advaita founding generation is placed has enough play in it to swing a lifetime.

## 6. Are Maṇḍana and Sureśvara the same person? {#identity}

This is the chapter's central case, and the corpus's answer is that **the question is open** — recorded on both nodes independently, with an `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edge between them so that the map teaches the legend rather than assuming it.

**The traditional account.** Maṇḍana Miśra, the great Mīmāṃsaka-turned-Advaitin, is defeated in debate by Śaṅkara — in the fuller versions, with Maṇḍana's wife Ubhaya Bhāratī serving as judge and then extending the contest herself. Defeated, Maṇḍana takes sannyāsa, becomes Śaṅkara's disciple, and is thereafter known as **Sureśvara**.

**The scholarly split.**

| position | who | the argument |
|---|---|---|
| **distinct persons** | **Kuppuswami Sastri** | the doctrinal oppositions of §5.1 are the evidence: a convert who then contradicts, in print, the positions he held before is possible but strained |
| **not proven either way** | **R. Balasubramanian** | "there is no conclusive evidence available to prove" their separateness — the case for distinctness is not decisive |
| the identification is **doubtful** | the general run of scholarship | "authenticity is doubtful"; the identification is legend-borne |

The strongest single consideration is the one Kuppuswami Sastri presses, and it is worth stating at full strength because it is a nice piece of reasoning:

> **If Maṇḍana became Sureśvara, then one man wrote the *Brahmasiddhi*, arguing that avidyā resides in the jīva and that meditation is required — and then, as Sureśvara, wrote the *Naiṣkarmyasiddhi* and the vārttikas, arguing that avidyā resides in Brahman and that knowledge alone liberates.** A conversion that reverses *both* of a thinker's signature positions, on the two axes where he was most distinctive, is a very large thing to ask of one biography.

Not impossible — people do change their minds, and a conversion narrative is precisely a story about changing one's mind. But notice the direction the legend runs. It converts the *rival* into the *disciple*, and it does so on exactly the two doctrines where the rival disagreed. If you were designing a story to absorb a competing school, that is the story you would design.

**What this chapter does with that.** It does not conclude that the identification is false. Balasubramanian's point stands: the evidence for distinctness is not conclusive either. The corpus's move — keeping **two nodes**, storing the conflation as a typed NOT-equivalent edge, and recording the split on both files — is the accurate representation of a question that the sources leave open. Collapsing them into one node would assert the legend; deleting the edge would hide it.

> ⚠️ **Do not resolve this by preference.** The legend is more satisfying (a great rival converted!) and the two-person reading is more parsimonious about the doctrines. Neither of those is evidence. The honest output is a mapped disagreement, which is what §4 of this project's method calls for when the signals split.

## 7. The *Yoga-Bhāṣya*'s "Vyāsa" — a commentator who may not exist {#yogabhasya}

*→ concept file: [vyasa-yogabhasya.md](../../../concepts/vyasa-yogabhasya.md) — `contested`, confidence **low***

The ***Yoga-Bhāṣya*** (c. 400 CE) is the foundational commentary on Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtra*, and it is not optional reading: it supplies the Sāṃkhya-Yoga metaphysics, the analysis of the *cittavṛtti*, and the meditative architecture through which the whole Yoga darśana is read (Ch 15). Whatever "Pātañjala Yoga" means, it means something the *Yoga-Bhāṣya* largely determined.

Its author is a live three-way problem:

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| a distinct commentator "**Vyāsa**" wrote a bhāṣya on Patañjali's sūtra | the traditional view — but stated clearly as *separate* authorship only from the **14th-c. *Sarvadarśanasaṅgraha*** onward | two authors; sūtra and bhāṣya distinct works |
| sūtra **and** bhāṣya are **one work** — the ***Pātañjalayogaśāstra*** — by a **single author, Patañjali**; "two authors" is a post-~1100 CE misreading | **Philipp A. Maas** (philology), now widely cited | **no separate "Vyāsa" at all**; the commentary is the author's own |
| the bhāṣya is by **Vindhyavāsin** (late 4th c. — §8) | a minority scholarly view | a third, different attribution |

The middle row is the striking one, and it is worth seeing what it claims. Not that Vyāsa was someone else, but that **there is no commentator** — that what the tradition split into a root text and a commentary on it was composed as a single work by one hand, and that the split is a later reading habit imposed on it. If Maas is right, then the most important commentary in the Yoga tradition is not a commentary.

**The flag that holds in all three cases:**

> ⚠️ **The "Vyāsa" of the *Yoga-Bhāṣya* is not the Mahābhārata Vyāsa.** The identification with the legendary compiler-sage (Ch 19) is a **later honorific overlay** — the name attaching itself to an authoritative text, as it does across the tradition. The corpus stores this as `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent: vyasa`, and it holds whichever of the three readings is correct.

### 7.1 The independence trap, caught in the act

The most methodologically valuable thing in this node is not its content but the reason its confidence is capped at **low**:

> The dominant modern thesis (single-author *Pātañjalayogaśāstra*) traces to **one scholar, Maas**. Wikipedia and the wisdomlib essay both merely **report** Maas. They are therefore **not independent confirmations.**

This is CLAUDE.md §4 signal 1 doing its job — "many translations are reworkings of one earlier source; copies are not confirmation" — and it is worth dwelling on because the failure is invisible from the outside. Two respectable, unrelated sources agree. They cite different pages, use different words, appear in different places. And the agreement is worth nothing, because they are both downstream of the same argument by the same philologist.

Compare §2. Vātsyāyana's node reaches **high** confidence because Dasti (2023) and Gangopadhyaya (1967) are genuinely separate acts of scholarship. Two sources agreeing is not the criterion; **two sources agreeing *independently*** is. The difference between `vatsyayana` at high and `vyasa-yogabhasya` at low is not how many sources were found — it is whether they were counting the same thing twice.

Note also that this is *not* a verdict against Maas. The single-author thesis may well be right, and it is "now widely cited" for good reasons. Low confidence here means **"this corpus has one source for it, however good"** — a statement about the state of the evidence in hand, not about the quality of the argument.

## 8. Vindhyavāsin — surviving in your opponents' records {#vindhyavasin}

*→ concept file: [vindhyavasin.md](../../../concepts/vindhyavasin.md) — `converged` on identity, confidence **low***

**Vindhyavāsin** (also *Vindhyavāsa*; fl. c. 4th c. CE) was an early-classical **Sāṃkhya teacher** who left **no surviving work** and is known almost entirely at second hand.

**What the reports say.** He was a **pupil of Vārṣagaṇya**, and a **reformer of the Sāṃkhya system**, revising the *Ṣaṣṭitantra* line whose older versions were ascribed to Kapila and Pañcaśikha (Ch 15). And his fame rests on a debate: per **Paramārtha's *Life of Vasubandhu***, he travelled to Ayodhyā and **defeated Vasubandhu's Buddhist teacher** (Buddhamitra — or Manoratha, per Kuei-chi), under a king named Vikramāditya, often identified with Candragupta II. The Yogācārin **Vasubandhu** (Ch 20), on returning, wrote the now-lost ***Paramārthasaptati*** to refute Vindhyavāsin's *Sāṃkhyaśāstra*.

Now count what survives of that episode. Vindhyavāsin's *Sāṃkhyaśāstra*: **lost**. Vasubandhu's refutation of it: **lost**. What remains is a Buddhist biography of Vasubandhu, reporting that Vasubandhu's teacher lost to a Sāṃkhya opponent and that Vasubandhu avenged the defeat in a book.

> **So a Sāṃkhya philosopher survives principally in the record of the Buddhists he defeated** — a source with an evident interest in the shape of the story, in which the loss is a setup for the school's own champion to put right.

That is why the confidence is **low** despite the two signals converging: **weak independence** (both the Encyclopedia of Buddhism entry and the *Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies*' Frauwallner reconstruction ultimately lean on Paramārtha's hostile biography plus one reconstruction), **no extant work**, and genuinely contested dates and king-identity (Vikramāditya = Candragupta II? Skandagupta?). Even the relation to **Īśvarakṛṣṇa** is open — a recent thesis uses Vindhyavāsin to fix the *Sāṃkhyakārikā*'s earliest possible date, which makes this shadowy figure load-bearing for the chronology of the one Sāṃkhya text that *does* survive.

### 8.1 A prior the corpus checked and dropped

One line in the concept file deserves to be lifted into the teaching layer, because it is the method working visibly:

> **"Note (against the batch assumption).** A common modern guess links Vindhyavāsin to the disputed Yoga**bhāṣya** authorship; **no source consulted here supports that** — it is not asserted."

The batch that wrote this node went in with a plausible prior — that Vindhyavāsin was the *Yoga-Bhāṣya*'s author — and the sources did not support it, so the file records the prior, records that it failed, and points at `vyasa-yogabhasya` for the real debate. (The minority view in §7's table is a *third* attribution reported there; it is not evidence for the guess, and the two files are careful not to prop each other up.)

This is CLAUDE.md §1 in practice: *edition names, dates, and attributions you "remember" are priors to verify by fetching, not facts.* The cheap move would have been to quietly let the guess stand — it is plausible, it tidies two loose ends into one, and nobody would have checked. Writing down that you checked and it failed costs a paragraph and buys the reader the ability to trust the rest.

## 9. Six ways an author dissolves {#dissolution}

The synthesis. Read the table top to bottom as a slope, not a set of categories:

| # | figure | confidence | how the attribution fails — or doesn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Vātsyāyana** | **high** | *It doesn't.* Three independent sources agree. The only trouble is a **name shared with someone out of scope** (the *Kāmasūtra*'s Vātsyāyana Mallanāga) |
| 2 | **Śaṅkara** | medium | **Works secure, life constructed.** 4 authentic texts vs 300+ attributed under an institutional title; the biography comes from 14 hagiographies written up to a millennium later |
| 3 | **Maṇḍana Miśra** | medium | **Absorbed by a legend.** Probably the dominant Advaitin of his century; the tradition remembers him mainly as the man Śaṅkara defeated |
| 4 | **Sureśvara** | medium | **May not be a separate person at all** — and the dating that would settle it is internally inconsistent (§5.2) |
| 5 | **"Vyāsa" of the Yoga-Bhāṣya** | **low** | **May not exist.** On the leading modern thesis there is no commentator: sūtra and bhāṣya are one work by one author. And the modern thesis has **one source**, reported twice |
| 6 | **Vindhyavāsin** | **low** | **Exists only in his opponents' records.** No extant work; known through a hostile Buddhist biography and one reconstruction |

Three things follow from reading it as a slope.

**First, the failures are of different kinds, not different degrees.** A name-collision (1) is a reader's problem. Institutional accretion (2) is an archival problem. A legend that reassigns a rival (3, 4) is a tradition's self-narration. A genre misreading (5) is a philological problem. Survival only through enemies (6) is an accident of preservation. Lumping these together as "ancient authorship was unreliable" throws away everything informative.

**Second, insecurity about the *author* is mostly independent of security about the *text*.** The *Yoga-Bhāṣya* exists, is stable, and is read the same way regardless of who wrote it. The *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* is what it is whether or not the *digvijaya* happened. What the author-question genuinely affects: **dating** (which sequences influence), **doctrinal attribution** (is this position Maṇḍana's or Sureśvara's?), and **the boundaries of a corpus** (does the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi count as evidence for Śaṅkara's views? — §3.1 says: not as authentic).

**Third, the one high-confidence case tells you what the others are missing** — and it is not more sources. It is **independent** sources. §7.1 is the clean demonstration: two agreeing reports of one philologist score lower than one monograph plus one unrelated translation.

## 10. What this does to the corpus's own method {#method}

This is where the chapter turns on the project that contains it.

CLAUDE.md §4 makes **commentarial tradition the anchor signal** — "mūla + bhāṣya + ṭīkā," with the explicit warning that a reading which skips commentary and reads modern meaning off bare Sanskrit is a red flag. Chapters 03, 05, 20 and 24 are all built on that principle, and it is right: the bhāṣya is where the philosophy is (§1).

But §9 has just shown that the commentators themselves are, in five cases out of six, less securely identified than the root texts they gloss. So does the anchor hold?

**Mostly yes, and for a reason worth stating precisely.** The anchor is doing a **semantic** job, not a biographical one. When the corpus consults commentary to fix what a term means, what it needs is *how the tradition that used this vocabulary understood it* — and the *Yoga-Bhāṣya* delivers that whether it was written by Vyāsa, by Patañjali, or by Vindhyavāsin. An anonymous commentary from within the tradition constrains a reading just as tightly as a signed one.

**But three things change**, and they should be applied consistently across the corpus:

| the anchor still works for… | it does **not** support… |
|---|---|
| **what a term meant** to the tradition that used it | **who first held** a doctrine |
| the **internal logic** of a system | the **sequence of influence** between thinkers |
| the **range of readings** a school entertained | **dating** anything by its author's dates |

And a fourth, sharper point. When two commentators disagree — Maṇḍana and Sureśvara on the locus of avidyā (§5.1) — the corpus records **both readings with attributions**. If those two might be one person, then the attributions are doing less work than they appear to. The *positions* are securely attested; **which name goes with which position** is secure only for as long as the identity question stays where it is. The right response is not to drop the names. It is to keep the `contested` marker visible on both nodes so that anyone reasoning from "Sureśvara held X" can see what that claim rests on — which is exactly what the two files do.

There is a pleasing symmetry here with Ch 24 §4.4 and §10. There, the Chaldean Oracles survived only through the school that authorised them, and two-thirds of the Presocratics survive only through Simplicius's citation habits. Here, a Sāṃkhya reformer survives only through his Buddhist opponents. **In both civilisations, some of the most load-bearing evidence reaches us through a channel with an interest in the outcome** — and in both, the corpus's obligation is the same: name the channel, and size the confidence to it.

## 11. Two civilisations, two failure modes {#civilisations}

Ch 24 §13 set out the strategies by which texts acquire authority and found the Greek/Latin and Indian materials moving in **opposite** directions — pseudepigraphy and misattribution *maximising* the author, *apauruṣeyatva* *eliminating* the author. This chapter's six cases let that be sharpened, because now the comparison can be made on **how the attribution fails**, not just on what the tradition wanted.

| | **the Greek/Latin cases** (Ch 24) | **the Indian cases** (here) |
|---|---|---|
| **Ammonius Saccas** / **Kapila** | founder wrote nothing; a pledge of silence; doctrine unrecoverable | founder with no securable work; system reconstructed from a later kārikā |
| **Pseudo-Dionysius** | a **deliberate forgery**: an anonymous author claims an apostolic name, undetected for a millennium | — no close Indian analogue in this corpus |
| ***Liber de Causis*** | **misattribution by readers**: Proclus taught as Aristotle for a century until Aquinas identified the source | **"Vyāsa" of the Yoga-Bhāṣya**: a name attaches to an authoritative text as honorific overlay |
| **Marinus's *Life of Proclus*** | biography written as a **demonstration** that its subject achieved *eudaimonia* | **the Śaṅkara hagiographies**: fourteen texts, up to a millennium later, "politically motivated" |
| **Simplicius / the Presocratics** | a school survives through **one channel's citation habits** | **Vindhyavāsin** survives through his **opponents'** records |
| **Śaṅkarācārya as an office** | — | **300+ texts** accumulate under a title borne by successive maṭha heads |

Two observations the table makes available.

**The Greek failures are more often *deliberate*; the Indian failures are more often *institutional*.** Pseudo-Dionysius chose a name and used it to acquire authority he did not have. Nothing in this chapter is an act like that. What happens instead is that a **title** absorbs the works of everyone who holds it; a **legend** absorbs a rival into a lineage; an **honorific name** drifts onto a text that deserves respect. These are processes rather than decisions, and they need no author to intend them. The nearest Indian analogue to a forgery in this corpus is not a forgery at all — it is the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi, which nobody appears to have attributed to Śaṅkara in bad faith; it simply became his, the way things become a famous person's.

**The one shared failure is the founder-shaped hole.** Kapila, Ammonius Saccas, and (contested) Caraka and Suśruta: four traditions, two civilisations, and in each case the figure at the origin is the one about whom least can be established. Ch 24 §2.3 offered the explanation — schools acquire founders retrospectively, and retrospective founders are furnished with whatever doctrine the school later needed. This chapter adds a case that tests it from the other side. **Śaṅkara is not a founder** — he is a systematiser four centuries into a tradition with a securely-attested body of work — and the hagiographic machinery attached itself to him anyway, a thousand years later. So the retrospective-founder account is not the whole story: the machinery goes to work on whoever the tradition later needs to be central, founder or not.

And a caution carried forward from Ch 23. Ch 19 explained the Indian authorship pattern by the prestige of authorlessness in scriptural culture; Ch 23 found the same pattern in Āyurveda, which has **no** *apauruṣeya* doctrine; Ch 24 found an inverted pattern in a civilisation with a different scriptural theory entirely. This chapter adds a fourth datum — the same pattern operating on a **philosopher**, in a genre (bhāṣya) that is not scripture at all and makes no claim to be authorless. That is now three cases where the theological explanation does not apply and the pattern shows up regardless. **The corpus should probably now favour the other horn of the fork Ch 24 §13 left open**: that attribution drift is a general property of long-transmitted textual traditions, which each tradition then rationalises with whatever theology it has to hand. That is stated here as the direction the evidence has moved, not as a settled finding — a fifth case could move it back.

## 12. Why this matters {#why}

**Because the doctrine chapters wrote cheques this one has to honour.** Chapter 11 says "Śaṅkara holds that…" on nearly every page. That sentence is well-founded — it rests on the *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya*, which is securely his. But the *same-looking* sentence "Sureśvara holds that avidyā resides in Brahman" rests on an attribution that is contested at the level of whether Sureśvara is a separate person. Both sentences are worth writing. They are not worth the same confidence, and a reader deserves to be told which is which.

**Because "the tradition says" and "the record shows" are different claims, and the gap between them is where most bad comparative history lives.** The tradition says Śaṅkara toured India winning debates and founded four maṭhas. The record shows a philosopher unmentioned by his contemporaries whose fame was built six centuries later. Neither statement is a debunking of the other — the hagiography is excellent evidence about 14th-century Vijayanagara, and terrible evidence about the 8th century. Knowing which question a source answers is most of the skill.

**Because the independence test has teeth, and this chapter is where you can watch it bite.** §7.1 and §2 are the same test producing opposite verdicts, and the difference is invisible if you only count sources. Anyone who takes one lesson from this chapter should take that one: *ask not how many sources agree, but whether they could have disagreed.*

**And because none of this diminishes the philosophy.** The locus-of-avidyā dilemma (§5.1) is a genuinely hard problem, and it stays hard no matter how many people wrote about it. The *Nyāya-Bhāṣya* built an epistemology that ran for a millennium. The *Yoga-Bhāṣya* defined how a darśana is read. Those achievements are properties of the texts. What this chapter unsettles is the *biographical frame* around them — and the frame was mostly added later anyway.

## 13. Flags {#flags}

| flag | why the confusion is tempting | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Vātsyāyana the logician ≠ Vātsyāyana Mallanāga** | identical surname, both famous | different people, different works; the *Kāmasūtra* author is out of scope and holds **no node** — the caution lives in prose because a typed edge needs a target |
| **Yoga-sūtra Patañjali ≠ the grammarian Patañjali** | one name, two towering works | Renou showed significant differences in language, grammar and vocabulary; the equation appears first in **Bhojadeva (~10th c.)** |
| **Śaṅkara the philosopher ≠ Śaṅkara the god** | *Śaṅkara* ("beneficent") is a standard name of **Śiva**, and the hagiographies make him Śiva's incarnation | a name-collision the devotional literature exploits; stored as a typed NOT-equivalent so the graph teaches it |
| **the 300+ attributed works ≠ Śaṅkara's corpus** | they circulate under his name | **four** works are authentic; the rest are largely by later heads holding the *Śaṅkarācārya* **title** |
| **the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi ≠ an authentic source for Śaṅkara's views** | it is the most-read "Śaṅkara" text in circulation | "questioned and mostly rejected" — and **two concept files in this repo queue it as a Śaṅkara fetch**; it must be treated as *attributed* |
| **hagiography ≠ biography** | the *digvijaya* is vivid, detailed, and universally retold | **fourteen** sources, up to a **millennium** later, "politically motivated"; the most-cited is **17th-century** |
| **Maṇḍana Miśra ≠ Sureśvara** *(contested)* | the tradition says the debate-loser converted and became him | they hold **opposite** positions on both signature doctrines; a conversion would require reversing both. But Balasubramanian holds distinctness unproven — **the map keeps two nodes and stores the conflation** |
| **avidyā's *object* ≠ avidyā's *locus*** | both are "what ignorance is about" | all parties agree the **object** is Brahman; the whole dispute is the ***āśraya*** — jīva (Maṇḍana) or Brahman (Sureśvara) |
| **the Yoga-Bhāṣya's "Vyāsa" ≠ the Mahābhārata Vyāsa** | the same honorific name, on two foundational texts | a **later honorific overlay**; and the identification holds under none of the three attribution readings |
| **"two sources agree" ≠ independent confirmation** | Wikipedia and wisdomlib both report the single-author thesis | both are reporting **Maas**. Copies are not confirmation — this is precisely why the node is capped **low** |
| **low confidence ≠ a bad argument** | "low" sounds like a verdict on the scholarship | it is a statement about **the evidence in this corpus's hands** — Maas's thesis may well be right |
| **Vindhyavāsin's defeat ≠ neutral testimony** | it is the one vivid, datable episode in his life | it survives in **Paramārtha's *Life of Vasubandhu***, a source whose interest is the Buddhist champion who avenged it |
| **a plausible prior ≠ a finding** | linking Vindhyavāsin to the Yoga-Bhāṣya tidies two loose ends into one | the batch checked and **no source supported it**; the failed guess is recorded rather than quietly dropped |
| **Advaita's Brahman ≠ śūnyatā** | the *pracchanna-bauddha* charge has a real basis in Gauḍapāda's vocabulary | a **positive, permanent** Brahman against the **denial of any self-existent substrate**; influence conceded, identity refused |
| **contested authorship ≠ a contested text** | it feels like the whole thing is unstable | the *Yoga-Bhāṣya* is stable and read the same way regardless; author-uncertainty hits **dating, attribution, and corpus-boundaries**, not the content |
