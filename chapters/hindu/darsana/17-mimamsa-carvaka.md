# Chapter 17 — The Veda's Defenders and Its Deniers: Mīmāṃsā & Cārvāka

**Concepts covered:** jaimini · mīmāṃsāsūtra · kumārila-bhaṭṭa · prabhākara · cārvāka
**Confidence:** all medium throughout. Primary-text access: exactly **one sūtra** in this cluster is verbatim-verified — Mīmāṃsāsūtra 1.1.2 (*codanā-lakṣaṇo 'rtho dharmaḥ*), given identically by two independent sources. Kumārila's Ślokavārttika and Prabhākara's Bṛhatī were not fetched from editions; and for Cārvāka there is **no primary text to fetch at all** — the Bṛhaspati Sūtra is lost, and everything known comes through the school's opponents, a structural sourcing ceiling the concept file flags explicitly (its famous "borrow money and drink ghee" verse is *omitted* from this corpus because no source for it was verified).
**Source ceiling note:** Jaimini is an anchor-by-attribution (dating spread: 4th c. BCE to 50 CE, semi-legendary), like Kapila (Ch. 15) and Gautama/Kaṇāda (Ch. 16). Even the *order* of the two great Mīmāṃsā sub-school founders is unsettled — see §4.

---

## How to use this chapter

This chapter completes the six āstika darśanas (Vedānta Ch. 11, Sāṃkhya-Yoga Ch. 15, Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika Ch. 16 — Mīmāṃsā here) and closes the darśana sequence with the school every other tradition defined itself against: Cārvāka, classical India's materialism.

The pairing is deliberate, because the two schools are **the opposite poles of exactly one question: what can words — specifically, the Veda's words — make you know?** Mīmāṃsā answers: *everything that matters most*; dharma is knowable **only** through Vedic injunction, and the Veda is eternal, authorless, and self-validating. Cārvāka answers: *nothing at all*; testimony collapses into inference, inference is a leap in the dark, and only perception survives. Every other school covered in this corpus lives somewhere between these poles.

**Remit note.** The Mīmāṃsā *epistemology* — the five/six pramāṇas, arthāpatti (postulation), śabda-pramāṇa, and svataḥ-prāmāṇya as a theory of knowledge — is already primary-covered in **Ch. 05**, set against Nyāya and the Buddhists. This chapter is the **ritual-exegesis system and its people**, plus the materialist challenge; where the epistemology is needed it is summarized and cross-referenced, not re-derived.

**A surprise worth flagging before starting:** the arch-*orthodox* school of this chapter is functionally **godless**. Mīmāṃsā needs no creator, no revealer, no divine guarantor — "there is no divine revealer; the Veda itself is authoritative." India's spectrum thus contains two entirely different atheisms: the heterodox one that rejects the Veda (Cārvāka), and the ultra-orthodox one that reveres the Veda *so much* that God would only get in the way.

## 1. Jaimini and the Mīmāṃsāsūtra — dharma by injunction {#jaimini}

*→ concept files: [jaimini.md](../../../concepts/jaimini.md) · [mimamsa-sutra.md](../../../concepts/mimamsa-sutra.md)*

**Jaimini** (dating spread: c. 4th c. BCE per one line of reference, ~300–200 BCE per another, as late as 50 CE per a third — recorded as a table in the concept file, not resolved) is the attributed author of the **Mīmāṃsāsūtra** (Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-sūtra), the founding text of the school of Vedic exegesis. Two name-cautions the tradition itself teaches: the philosopher is **not** the legendary epic Jaimini (Vyāsa's disciple, of the Jaiminīya-Bhārata) — a traditional, not historical, identification; and *Pūrva* ("prior") Mīmāṃsā — inquiry into the Veda's ritual portion — pairs with Bādarāyaṇa's *Uttara* ("later") Mīmāṃsā, i.e. Vedānta (Ch. 11): two exegetical projects on two portions of one scripture.

The text is **by far the largest of the root-sūtras**: ~2,700 sūtras in twelve adhyāyas of sixty pādas — an order of magnitude beyond the Vaiśeṣika's ~370 or the Nyāya's ~528 (Ch. 16). It opens *athāto dharma-jijñāsā* — "now, therefore, the inquiry into dharma" — and its master-definition follows at once, **the one verbatim-verified sūtra of this chapter (MS 1.1.2):**

> *codanā-lakṣaṇo 'rtho dharmaḥ* — "dharma is that object/purpose marked out by Vedic injunction (*codanā*)."

Three theses radiate from it:

1. **Dharma is invisible to perception.** What you ought to do is not an observable fact anywhere in the world; *only* the injunction reveals it. This gives Vedic authority a precisely delimited supremacy — supreme exactly where perception is silent.
2. **Apauruṣeyatva.** The Veda is *authorless* — uncreated, eternal, with **no divine revealer**. Its authority derives from no one, which is exactly why it cannot be impeached: an author (human or divine) would be a fallible point of failure. Paired with **śabda-nityatva**, the eternality of the word and of the word–meaning relation (not convention but permanence).
3. **Apūrva.** The unseen potency bridging ritual act and eventual fruit — how a sacrifice performed now produces its result later. The system's answer to a causal gap its own ritualism creates.

Early Mīmāṃsā is overwhelmingly **ritual-and-dharma-focused rather than liberation-focused** — whether mokṣa is even attainable enters the school only as a later question. Among the darśanas covered in this corpus it is the outlier whose original *telos* is not liberation at all but correct performance of commanded action.

> ⚠️ **Name-collision alert (the map must teach it):** Mīmāṃsā *dharma* = ritual duty marked out by injunction. Jain *dharma-dravya* (Ch. 09) = a passive medium of motion. Vaiśeṣika *dharma* (Ch. 16 §2) = moral-causal merit. One Sanskrit word, three unrelated referents — the concept files carry the typed `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edges.

## 2. The commentarial spine — and a school that splits at the commentary layer {#spine}

The Mīmāṃsā lineage runs **Jaimini (mūla) → Śabara's Śābarabhāṣya (the only complete extant commentary) → two rival sub-commentarial schools**: Kumārila's **Bhāṭṭa** and Prabhākara's **Prābhākara** (Gurumata). Everything later Mīmāṃsā argues about is argued *through* Śabara — the sub-schools are sub-commentaries on his commentary, two layers below the sūtra.

This corpus has now seen each configuration of the mūla/commentary power balance: Vaiśeṣika, where one commentary (Praśastapāda's) *replaced* the sūtra as the anchor (Ch. 16 §8); the Tattvārtha tradition, where two recensions carry rival commentaries of equal weight (the Jain translation reading-room); and here Mīmāṃsā, where a single agreed bhāṣya becomes the trunk from which the tradition then forks. Where a school's identity lives — sūtra, bhāṣya, or sub-bhāṣya — is a variable, not a constant, of Indian intellectual history.

## 3. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa — intrinsic validity and the Buddhist duel {#kumarila}

*→ concept file: [kumarila-bhatta.md](../../../concepts/kumarila-bhatta.md)*

**Kumārila Bhaṭṭa** (fl. ~660 CE) founded the **Bhāṭṭa** sub-school through three subcommentaries on Śabara — the **Ślokavārttika** (the philosophical one, in verse), Tantravārttika, and Ṭupṭīkā.

**Svataḥ-prāmāṇya — his signature thesis.** A cognition is **valid intrinsically, by its own arising**, and stands unless positively defeated. The argument is a regress-stopper: any demonstration that a cognition is valid would itself presuppose reliable ways of knowing, so validity cannot be conferred from outside — it must be the default. Deployed on the Veda, this yields Mīmāṃsā's rational apologetics: an eternal, authorless, self-validating scripture whose authority it is *rational* to accept, since the demand for external validation is incoherent in principle. (The epistemological detail — and how Nyāya answers with *parataḥ-prāmāṇya*, extrinsic validation — is Ch. 05's territory.)

**The Buddhist duel.** Kumārila mounted sophisticated attacks on Buddhist epistemology, particularly **Dignāga** — intrinsic-validity realism against constructed cognition and *apoha* semantics (Ch. 05). The two are direct antagonists on shared terrain: perception, inference, the status of words. Tradition credits this confrontation with contributing to Buddhism's decline in India — a credit the concept file marks as "probably over-credited," and this chapter repeats that caution.

## 4. Prabhākara — the rival whose priority is itself contested {#prabhakara}

*→ concept file: [prabhakara.md](../../../concepts/prabhakara.md)*

**Prabhākara Miśra** (fl. ~6th–7th c. CE), nicknamed "Guru," founded the **Prābhākara** school through the **Bṛhatī**, his commentary on Śabara (later glossed by Śālikanātha's Ṛjuvimalā).

**Even the chronology is a live dispute.** Tradition makes Prabhākara Kumārila's student; but scholars including Gaṅgānātha Jhā argue the reverse — that the Prābhākara school is *older*, "nearer to the spirit of the original Mīmāṃsā," and Prabhākara "a more original thinker than Kumārila." The teacher/student order is recorded in the concept file as contested and unresolved — a founder-level uncertainty of a different kind from the usual dating spreads: here we do not even know which rival came first.

**The three splits** (the substance of the rivalry, tabled in the concept file):

| issue | Prābhākara | Bhāṭṭa (Kumārila) |
|---|---|---|
| sentence-meaning | *anvitābhidhāna* — words denote only as already syntactically connected; sentence-meaning comes first | *abhihitānvaya* — words denote separately; sentence-meaning is *assembled* from them |
| theory of error | *akhyātivāda* — **there is no positive erroneous cognition**; "error" is mere failure to notice the difference between two individually-valid contents ("this" + remembered silver) | *viparīta-khyāti* — error is a real positive mis-relating of one thing as another |
| pramāṇa count | five | six (adds *abhāva*, non-apprehension) |

Prabhākara's error-theory is the radical one, and it shows how far svataḥ-prāmāṇya can be pushed: if all cognition is intrinsically valid, then strictly speaking **nothing is ever mis-perceived** — illusion is only non-discrimination between two true cognitions. The school bought perfect intrinsic validity at the price of denying that error exists.

## 5. Cārvāka — the materialist pole {#carvaka}

*→ concept file: [carvaka.md](../../../concepts/carvaka.md)*

**Cārvāka** (also **Lokāyata**, "[the philosophy] prevalent among the people"; also Bārhaspatya, from its mythical founder Bṛhaspati) is classical India's materialist and empiricist school — the *nāstika* outlier that denied soul, karma, rebirth, liberation, and God.

**The sourcing situation IS the first fact to learn about it:** the school's own texts are **lost**. The *Bṛhaspati Sūtra* survives nowhere; everything known comes through **hostile summaries by opponents**. Every statement below is reconstruction through adversaries — a structural ceiling on confidence that no additional research can lift, only ameliorate.

**Ontology.** Exactly four gross elements — earth, water, fire, air — and pointedly **no ākāśa** (imperceptible, therefore inadmissible; contrast the five-element schemes of Ch. 16 §6, where only the Buddhists similarly demote space, and for different reasons). No immaterial soul: **consciousness is an emergent product of matter**, arising when elements combine in a living body — as intoxicating power arises from fermenting ingredients that are individually non-intoxicating — and vanishing at death. No transmigrating self; karma "as a moral law governing future lives is therefore meaningless."

**Epistemology.** Perception (*pratyakṣa*) is the **only** pramāṇa. The centerpiece is the attack on **vyāpti**, the universal concomitance ("wherever smoke, there fire") that grounds all inference (Ch. 05): (a) no one observes all instances across all time and space, and confirming the universal *by inference* is circular; (b) the **upādhi** objection — any observed regularity may conceal an unnoticed conditioning factor, and ruling out all hidden conditions would require exactly the universal knowledge in question. Inference is a "leap in the dark"; testimony fails derivatively (assessing a speaker's reliability itself requires inference); scripture's unperceived gods, karma and soul are epistemically baseless.

**Ethics.** With no afterlife to optimize for, the good is **pleasure in this life** — a this-worldly hedonism that opponents caricatured as crude egoism. (How crude it actually was is unrecoverable — the caricature is by the adversaries who are also our only sources.)

> ⚠️ **Two conflations the concept file types explicitly:**
> - **Cārvāka ≠ Democritus** (Ch. 14), despite both being ancient materialisms with mortal, matter-produced consciousness. They split exactly on whether the unperceived can be known: Democritus reasons *inferentially* to imperceptible atoms and void; Cārvāka rejects the inferential move itself. One materialism is built by inference, the other forbids it.
> - **Cārvāka ≠ Buddhist anattā** (Ch. 12), despite both denying a substantial self. Cārvāka's denial is *annihilationist* — consciousness simply ends, no karma, no rebirth; Buddhism denies the substantial self while *affirming* karmic continuity as a dependently-originated stream. The Buddha's tradition classed annihilationism among the wrong views, not among its own.

## 6. One question, every answer: the authority-of-testimony spectrum {#spectrum}

Set this chapter's two schools at the ends, and the corpus's other traditions arrange themselves in between — the spectrum Ch. 05 built, now bookended:

| position on the Veda / testimony | school |
|---|---|
| Veda eternal, authorless, self-validating; dharma knowable *only* thus | Mīmāṃsā (this chapter) |
| Veda authoritative because God is its reliable author | later Nyāya (Ch. 05/16) |
| testimony valid, but the Veda's authority not assumed | Buddhist & Jain epistemologies (Ch. 05) — śabda admitted, scripture's authority grounded in the enlightened speaker |
| testimony collapses into inference, inference into the dark; perception alone | Cārvāka (this chapter) |

And note where the pramāṇa-counting war ends up: Cārvāka 1 (perception) — Buddhists 2 — Sāṃkhya 3 — Nyāya 4 — Prābhākara 5 — Bhāṭṭa 6. The count is never bookkeeping; each increment is a substantive claim about what kinds of knowing are irreducible (Ch. 05 walks the increments).

## 7. Cross-tradition: where Mīmāṃsā and Cārvāka sit {#cross-tradition}

Extending the diagnostic table of Ch. 11/15/16:

| question | Mīmāṃsā | Cārvāka | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika (Ch. 16) | Jain (Ch. 01–03) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| God? | **no creator/revealer needed** — the Veda's authority would be *weakened* by an author | no | yes — Īśvara combines the atoms | no |
| Scripture | eternal, authorless, supreme on dharma | baseless (unperceivable content) | authoritative via its divine author | authoritative via the omniscient Jina |
| Self | real (ritual requires an agent who reaps apūrva-fruit later) | none — consciousness is fermentation-like emergence, gone at death | real ātman-substance, plural | real jīva, plural, karma-bound |
| Karma/afterlife | yes — via apūrva | denied outright | yes | yes (karma as literal matter) |
| Primary goal | correct performance of commanded ritual (mokṣa a latecomer question) | pleasure in this life | liberation through correct knowledge of categories | liberation through shedding karma-matter |
| Error possible? | Prābhākara: strictly no (akhyāti); Bhāṭṭa: yes (viparīta-khyāti) | perception yes; inference is *systematically* untrustworthy | yes — extrinsic validation sorts true from false | yes — naya-relative one-sidedness (Ch. 02) |

The deepest structural observation the pairing affords: **Mīmāṃsā and Cārvāka agree against everyone else that no God underwrites knowledge or duty — and disagree about everything that follows.** Strip the guarantor from scripture and you can conclude either that scripture must be self-validating and eternal (Mīmāṃsā) or that it is noise (Cārvāka). The two godless schools of classical India are the most scripture-committed and the least.
