# Chapter 43 — Meaning Read In: Helārāja, Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa, and What Commentary Adds

> **Primary concepts:** `helārāja` · `kauṇḍabhaṭṭa` · `sphoṭasiddhi`
> **Folder:** `hindu/darsana/` — continuing [Ch 41](41-the-word-as-the-world.md), not [Ch 42](../shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md). **The split is the same argument Ch 40/41 made:** the commentators who worked on grammar's *technique* are in the śāstra chapter; the three here worked on its *theory of meaning*, which is where grammar made its claim to be a darśana. **Same batch, same century in two of three cases, different category.**
> **Reads after:** [Ch 41 (The Word as the World)](41-the-word-as-the-world.md), whose §5 deliberately withheld a modern comparison, and [Ch 38 (The New Logic)](38-the-new-logic.md) §4.1, whose central historical claim this chapter has to qualify.

---

## How to use this chapter

Chapter 41 argued that the grammarians claimed darśana status and that the claim was contested. This chapter is about the three figures through whom that claim is actually *transmitted* — and it has an uncomfortable governing question:

> **When you read a philosophical text through its commentary, whose philosophy are you reading?**

The corpus has met this before. Chapter 41 §2.1 found that *sphoṭa*'s apparent antiquity "is an **artefact of the reading apparatus**": the word is absent from the Prātiśākhyas and present in their commentaries, so anyone reading them as the tradition does — root and gloss as one object — finds the doctrine in the oldest phonetic literature and concludes it is primordial.

**Batch 48 caught the same mechanism operating on the corpus's own citation practice.** That is §1, and it is the reason this chapter exists.

Three things to carry out:

1. **Helārāja reads a tenth-century map of philosophy out of a fifth-century list of synonyms** — and the corpus reaches one of its most-cited claims through him. (§1)
2. **The traffic between grammar and logic reverses.** Grammar supplied Navya-Nyāya its central device; five centuries later the grammarians restate their own semantics in Navya-Nyāya's idiom. (§2)
3. **A treatise that argues by preserving both sides** — and whose author is refuting his own teacher. (§3)

## §1 Helārāja, and a commentator who is two-thirds lost

**Helārāja** (Kashmir, second half of the 10th century, son of Bhūtirāja) is the commentator through whom Bhartṛhari's third *kāṇḍa* is read — by the tradition, by modern scholarship, and by this corpus. IEP calls him Bhartṛhari's "first major commentator." His ***Prakīrṇaprakāśa*** is printed beside the verses in every standard edition of kāṇḍa III.

⚠ **He wrote on all three kāṇḍas and only the third survives.** The corpus's most load-bearing commentator is himself two-thirds lost — the same transmission pattern Chapter 41 §4 recorded for Bhartṛhari's own *Mahābhāṣyadīpikā*, which survives in one manuscript covering fifty-five sūtras.

### §1.1 He was reachable after all

Batch 48 queued Helārāja expecting him to fail. Abhyankar's dictionary mentions him **twice**, both times bare — no date, no biography — and the queue explicitly warned he might have to be marked `blocked`, which would have left the corpus's grammar-to-logic claim resting on a single secondary citation.

Instead: **Charles Li's Cambridge PhD (2018) is an open-access critical edition *and* annotated translation** of the *Dravyasamuddeśa* with Helārāja's commentary, CC BY-NC-SA. There is also K. A. Subramania Iyer's 1963 Deccan College critical edition of kāṇḍa III, and a *Journal of Indian Philosophy* article by Vergiani on Helārāja's citation practice.

> ⭐ **The corpus now reaches Helārāja through a critical edition rather than through one line of Matilal.** That is the kind of move `DRIFT.md` D1 exists to count — and it is the first one this corpus has scored in several batches.

### §1.2 ⭐⭐ And reading him immediately qualified an edge already drawn

Li, §2, "Helārāja on *dravya*: an all-encompassing doctrine":

> At the beginning of the *Dravyasamuddeśa*, Bhartṛhari presents a list of **five synonyms** of the word *dravya*. **Whatever his own intention was, Helārāja interprets each of the words to refer to a specific school of thought**: Ātmādvaita, Buddhism, Sattādvaita, Sāṃkhya, and Cārvāka.

The decisive detail is chronological. Li cites **Bronkhorst**: Advaita was "**conspicuously absent** in listings of philosophical schools during Bhartṛhari's time" — "and yet here, in Helārāja's 10th-century commentary, it is mentioned **twice**." Li's own judgement: the list "does not correspond to any common list of philosophical schools… the choices Helārāja makes are **very idiosyncratic**, and give us a sense of which philosophical opponents **he** was most concerned with."

> ⭐ **Bhartṛhari wrote a list of synonyms. Helārāja read a map of tenth-century philosophy out of it.**

And the purpose is not polemical but **absorptive**: "unlike many other doxographies, Helārāja is not so much concerned with **refuting** rival doctrines as with showing that **all of them can be subsumed** by the kind of non-dual philosophy that Bhartṛhari proposes."

### §1.3 ⚠ What this does to Chapter 38

Chapter 38 §4.1 — the corpus's most-cited cross-tradition result — established that **Navya-Nyāya's signature device, the *avacchedaka*, has grammatical ancestry.** The evidence is Matilal citing *Vākyapadīya* III, "Adhikaraṇādhikāra," verses 2–3 **with Helārāja's commentary**, for the pervasive (*abhivyāpaka*) / partitive (*aikadeśika*) locus distinction. Chapter 41 closed the gap Chapter 38 had recorded as unlinkable.

**That claim passes through a commentator now known to read later doctrine back into these verses.** The caveat is now on the edge in `avacchedaka.md`, and the exact wording matters:

- **The edge is not withdrawn.** Li's demonstration concerns the ***Dravyasamuddeśa*** — his thesis **does not treat the *Adhikaraṇasamuddeśa* at all**, and the corpus checked. Matilal is a careful reader. The distinction may well be in Bhartṛhari's verses.
- **But the corpus has not seen those verses apart from the commentary.** The terminology it cites is quoted from a page where verse and gloss are printed together.

> ⭐ **This is Chapter 41 §2.1's mechanism in a second literature — and this time the corpus caught it operating on itself rather than on someone else.** Chapter 41 had even predicted the shape without the evidence: "Bhartṛhari is read through Helārāja, as Gaṅgeśa is through the Dīdhiti." **The prediction was right and it now has a case.**

### §1.4 ⚠ The corollary that keeps this honest

Chapter 40 §8.2 corrected an earlier over-reading, and the correction applies here with full force:

> **The mechanism is not inherently corrupting — it is inherently *invisible*.**

Haradatta reading the sphoṭa doctrine out of the bare *name* Sphoṭāyana produced **false history**. The tradition supplying a name and definition for *pratyāhāra* produced **a perfectly good analysis** ([Ch 42](../shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md) §9). Li himself treats Helārāja as "**highly influential in shaping the interpretation of the text by later authors**" — a statement about influence, not about error.

**Noticing that the mechanism operated is the job. Assuming the result is false is not.**

### §1.5 A debt narrowed in passing

Batch 47 asked whether the *Vṛtti* on kāṇḍas I–II is Bhartṛhari's own. **Abhyankar** (first hand) gives that commentary to **Puṇyarāja**, adding that "some scholars hold the view that the commentary on the **first kāṇḍa was written by Bhartṛhari himself**." The modern challenge now has a name attached — **Ashok Aklujkar** — and Puṇyarāja a date (Kashmir, c. 800 CE), with his kāṇḍa-I commentary reported gappy and his kāṇḍa-II *Ṭīkā* complete. ⚠ **Not settled. Narrowed, with a bibliography to chase.**

## §2 Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa, and the direction of borrowing

**Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa** (early 17th century) was the son of Raṅgojī and the **nephew of Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita** ([Ch 42](../shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md) §5). His *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇa* glosses his uncle's verse text; his *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇasāra* is his own abridgement of it, and is the version everyone reads.

Abhyankar's valuation of him is unusually direct for a lexicographer, and it is why this node exists:

> He "was **one of the few writers on the *Arthavicāra* in the Vyākaraṇaśāstra**" — and "his ***Bhūṣaṇasāra* ranks next to the *Vākyapadīya* of Bhartṛhari**."

> ⭐ **Second place in a philosophical literature whose first place is twelve centuries older, with nothing named in between.** And the discipline in question is *arthavicāra* — the investigation of **meaning**, inside a science otherwise organised around **form**.

### §2.1 ⭐⭐ The traffic reverses

Put beside Chapter 38, this produces the chapter's second finding.

| period | direction | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 5th–13th c. | **grammar → logic** | Navya-Nyāya's *avacchedaka* descends from Bhartṛhari's pervasive/partitive locus distinction (Ch 38 §4.1, and §1.3 above) |
| 17th c. | **logic → grammar** | Bhaṭṭoji and Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa "respond to the views expressed by **Naiyāyikas and Mīmāṃsakas** by reformulating the association of speech forms with semantic conditions… **in terms of cognition**" |

> ⭐ **The discipline that supplied Navya-Nyāya its central instrument ends up re-describing its own semantics in Navya-Nyāya's vocabulary.**

⚠ **This is not a contradiction and must not be drawn as one.** They are two separate transfers, five centuries apart, in opposite directions, each independently sourced. The corpus records both and asserts **no net "influence"** in either direction. What it does show is that the "grammar → logic" story Chapter 38 told is one leg of a two-way exchange, and that a corpus which had only the first leg had half the picture.

### §2.2 The school that was a household

The Stanford Encyclopedia names **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa and Nāgeśabhaṭṭa** as the pair who **rejected Bhartṛhari's unitary sentence-meaning model** — evidence Chapter 41 already used for its claim that "the tradition did not simply absorb him."

They are an uncle's son and a grandson's pupil in one Varanasi family ([Ch 42](../shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md) §5.2).

> ⚠ **What this licenses, and what it does not.** It explains how a position *travelled*. It says nothing about whether the position is right, and it does not show the kinship *caused* the agreement — both men work from Bhaṭṭoji's verse text, which is a sufficient explanation on its own. **A striking fact about transmission, not an argument.**

### §2.3 ⚠ Two things left open rather than tidied

- Abhyankar says the *Bhūṣaṇa* glosses Bhaṭṭoji's ***Vaiyākaraṇasiddhāntakārikā***; the modern editions say ***Vaiyākaraṇamatonmajjana***. These are **very likely one text under two names** — but no source consulted says so, and **the corpus does not assert it.**
- Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa wrote a ***Sphoṭavāda***. So, per LinguIndic, did **Nāgeśa**. Uncle's son and grandson's pupil, a century apart, one lineage, one doctrine. **Probably two works; neither has been read.** Flagged before it can be made — the **eighth** name-collision the corpus has flagged, and the first generated by a *doctrine*-name rather than a person or a place.

⚠ Both are deliberately the opposite of the Batch 47 failure mode. The tempting move is to smooth two titles into one and never mention it.

## §3 The *Sphoṭasiddhi*: a treatise that preserves its opponent

**Maṇḍana Miśra** (fl. 9th c.) is a man the corpus already knew as a Mīmāṃsaka turned Advaitin, Kumārila's student, and Śaṅkara's rival. His ***Sphoṭasiddhi*** is a third career.

Its method is what earns it a node: **it argues by assembling both sides out of quotations.** It presents "the main arguments of **Kumārila** against the sphoṭa doctrine **supported by quotations from the *Ślokavārttika***," and "the arguments of **Bhartṛhari** to establish the existence of sphoṭa, **with quotations from the *Vākyapadīya***" — and then establishes sphoṭa.

> ⭐⭐ **The author is refuting his own teacher.** Maṇḍana was Kumārila's student in Mīmāṃsā. Here he takes his teacher's position as the *pūrvapakṣa* and defeats it using the grammarians' arguments. **A Mīmāṃsaka defends a Vyākaraṇa doctrine against the greatest Mīmāṃsaka.**

### §3.1 A shape the corpus keeps meeting

The text **preserves the position it was written to defeat.** Kumārila's case against sphoṭa reaches a reader through the treatise built to demolish it.

That is the shape Chapter 40 §6 records at its sharpest — the Vārttikas surviving only inside the commentary that discusses them — recurring with **no commentarial relation at all**: not a commentary preserving its root text, but a **polemic preserving its opponent**.

⚠ **Drawn no further.** Unlike the Vārttikas, the *Ślokavārttika* survives independently, so nothing here is known *only* through Maṇḍana. **The parallel is one of method, not of dependence.**

### §3.2 ⚠ Why this section is in the chapter, and what it cost

Batch 47 recorded, as a discovery, that the *Sphoṭasiddhi* was "absent from `mandana-mishra.md`." **It was not absent** — it is in that file's front-matter and its gloss. [Ch 42](../shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md) §8.2 treats that failure as a method finding; here it is enough to say that the node was written on better grounds than the ones that queued it.

**And a limit stated plainly: no line of the *Sphoṭasiddhi*'s argument has been read.** Not Kumārila's objections, not Maṇḍana's answers. This section reports **that** the text does this and **from which sources** it quotes; "establishes the existence of sphoṭa" is the sources' summary, not a reading.

> ⭐ **The corpus knows the architecture of an argument it has not read.** That is exactly the D1 condition `DRIFT.md` measures, and here the remedy is unusually cheap: **K. A. Subramania Iyer published the Sanskrit with a facing English translation in 1966** — the same editor as the standard *Vākyapadīya* III with Helārāja. **One scholar's output would supply two of this chapter's three unread primary texts.**

## §4 What this chapter found

1. **Helārāja is reachable at first hand, and reading him qualifies the corpus's own most-cited cross-tradition claim.** He reads five philosophical schools out of five synonyms, two of them a school absent from listings of Bhartṛhari's own time. The `avacchedaka` edge stands; the caveat that the corpus has never seen those verses without his gloss now stands with it. (§1)
2. **Grammar and logic borrowed from each other in both directions, five centuries apart.** Chapter 38 had one leg. (§2.1)
3. **The tradition's last philosophical school was one family** — which explains transmission and argues nothing. (§2.2)
4. **A ninth-century Mīmāṃsaka defended the grammarians' theory of meaning against his own teacher, by quoting him.** (§3)
5. ⚠ **Three of this chapter's central texts are unread**, and one editor published two of them. (§3.2)

## §5 ⚠ Where this chapter deliberately did not go

Chapter 41 §5 contrasted its own restraint with Chapter 38 §7, where a modern comparison **earned** its place because Matilal and Ganeri converged independently. **The difference is evidence, not squeamishness.** Two applications here:

- **Helārāja's "non-dual theory of reality," subsuming Buddhism and Sāṃkhya**, invites comparison with every modern monism on offer. **No such edge is drawn.** The corpus records that Helārāja imported *Ātmādvaita* and *Sattādvaita* into a text that predates their currency as school-labels — which is a reason for **more** caution about the doctrine's shape, not less.
- **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa's semantics "in terms of cognition"** looks like a cognitive turn. **It is not described as one here.** The corpus has read none of his arguments, and the phrase is a modern summariser's, not his.
