# Chapter 16 — Counting What There Is: Nyāya & Vaiśeṣika, the Realist Sister-Schools

**Concepts covered:** gautama-akṣapāda · kaṇāda · nyāyasūtra · vaiśeṣikasūtra · dravya-vaiśeṣika · paramāṇu-vaiśeṣika · pañcamahābhūta · asatkāryavāda · praśastapāda
**Confidence:** all medium throughout. Primary-text access: exactly **two sūtras** in this whole cluster have been fetched verbatim from a citable edition — *Vaiśeṣika Sūtra* 1.1.4 (the category-list sūtra, via Nandalal Sinha's 1923 translation with Śaṅkara Miśra's Upaskāra) and *Nyāyasūtra* 1.2.4 (the five-fallacy sūtra, verified in `hetvabhasa.md`, covered in Ch. 05). Everything else rests on encyclopedic secondary sources (Wikipedia, IEP, SEP, New World Encyclopedia). Every underlying concept file explicitly withholds "high" pending a critical-edition fetch (Jha's 1916 Vaiśeṣika Sūtra, a full Nyāyasūtra edition, Praśastapāda's Padārthadharmasaṃgraha) that has not yet been done.
**Source ceiling note:** Gautama and Kaṇāda are both, in their own concept files' words, anchors-by-attribution rather than documented individuals — Karl Potter's verdict on the Nyāyasūtra ("we have not the vaguest idea who wrote the Nyāyasūtras or when he lived") applies in spirit to both founders. This chapter treats them with that caution, not more confidently.

---

## How to use this chapter

Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika are the second traditionally-paired set of sister darśanas (after Sāṃkhya-Yoga, Ch. 15), and the pairing has the same shape: complementary specialisations that later fused into a single school, **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika**. Nyāya supplied the *method* — the pramāṇa epistemology, the five-membered inference, the debate-theory; Vaiśeṣika supplied the *metaphysics* — the category-scheme of being and the atomism.

**Remit note — read this first.** The Nyāya *epistemology* (pramāṇa-nyāya, anumāna, vyāpti, hetvābhāsa, tarka, jāti) is already primary-covered in **Ch. 05**, where it is set against the Buddhist and Mīmāṃsā alternatives; the atomicity-of-manas argument was already showcased in **Ch. 15 §9**'s five-tradition manas table. This chapter deliberately does *not* re-derive that material. Its subject is the side of the merged school the corpus has not yet given a reading-view: the realist **ontology** — what exists, in how many categories, made of what — and the causal axiom (*asatkāryavāda*) that the whole edifice presupposes. Where the epistemology is needed, this chapter points to Ch. 05.

§§1–2 introduce the two founder-anchors and their root texts. §§3–4 lay out the Vaiśeṣika category-scheme and its nine substances. §5 is the atomism. §6 widens out to the five great elements (*pañcamahābhūta*), a cross-tradition scheme the Vaiśeṣika substances plug into. §7 is asatkāryavāda — the causal doctrine that makes this family the photographic negative of Ch. 15's Sāṃkhya. §8 covers Praśastapāda, the "commentator" whose compendium quietly replaced the text it commented on. §9 closes with the cross-tradition contrast table.

**Frequent conflation alert you should know before starting:**

*Vaiśeṣika paramāṇu ≠ the modern atom, despite a century of enthusiastic equation.* Kaṇāda's own concept file carries the warning at the top: the Vaiśeṣika atom is a **metaphysical-categorial posit** reached by a priori argument — not an empirical particle, not the result of any experiment, and it combines by dyads and triads under an unseen moral force (*adṛṣṭa*), not by physical interaction. "Kaṇāda anticipated Dalton" is precisely the §0 failure-mode this project exists to prevent. The structural parallel is real and worth drawing (§5 draws it, against both the Jain and the Greek atom); the identity is false.

## 1. Two founders, one shared predicament {#founders}

*→ concept files: [gautama-aksapada.md](../../../concepts/gautama-aksapada.md) · [kanada.md](../../../concepts/kanada.md)*

**Akṣapāda Gautama** (also Gotama; epithet Dīrghatapas) is credited with the **Nyāyasūtra**; **Kaṇāda** (also Kaṇabhakṣa, Kaṇabhuj, Ulūka, Kāśyapa) with the **Vaiśeṣikasūtra**. Both are dated only within embarrassingly wide bands — Gautama anywhere from the 6th century BCE (a contemporary of the Buddha and Mahāvīra) to the 2nd century CE; Kaṇāda's doctrines "as early as the 6th c. BCE" by one line of argument (his sūtra names Sāṃkhya and Mīmāṃsā but never Buddhism), the text's systematisation c. 200 BCE–early CE by another, c. 100 BCE by the SEP. For both, whether one person wrote the text or it grew by accretion is itself unsettled. They are what Ch. 15 called Kapila: **anchors by attribution** — the names a school projects backward onto its origin — not recoverable individuals.

**"Akṣapāda" means "one with eyes on his feet"** — a traditional image of ground-watching analytic acuity that later legend literalised.

**The Gautama name-collision warning, which the tradition itself issues.** "Gautama" is one of the most overloaded names in Indian thought. The logician is **not** Gautama Buddha — for Siddhārtha, *Gautama* is a clan name, not a philosophical signature, and the two are doctrinal *opponents*: the Nyāyasūtra's Book 3 defends a substantial **ātman** that Buddhist anattā flatly denies. Nor is he the author of the *Gautama Dharmasūtra*, a separate legal-ritual writer. Same name, three unrelated bodies of work.

Kaṇāda carries a symmetrical caution: he is paired with **Kapila** in founder-lists so often that the two blur, but Vaiśeṣika (realist, pluralist, atomist) and Sāṃkhya (puruṣa/prakṛti dualism, no atoms anywhere) could hardly be more different systems — the concept file types this `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent`.

## 2. The two root texts — sixteen categories of arguing, six categories of being {#root-texts}

*→ concept files: [nyaya-sutra.md](../../../concepts/nyaya-sutra.md) · [vaiseshika-sutra.md](../../../concepts/vaiseshika-sutra.md)*

Both root texts make the same startling opening move: **liberation is attained by correctly knowing an enumerated list of categories.** What differs is which categories.

**The Nyāyasūtra** (~200 CE as extant; five adhyāyas, each in two āhnikas, ~528 sūtras; read through Vātsyāyana's Nyāyabhāṣya, ~5th c., the earliest surviving complete commentary) opens (NS 1.1.1) by declaring that the highest good (*niḥśreyasa*) follows from correct knowledge of **sixteen padārthas** — and the list reveals what kind of book this is: pramāṇa (means of knowledge), prameya (objects of knowledge), saṃśaya (doubt), prayojana (purpose), dṛṣṭānta (example), siddhānta (established tenet), avayava (members of inference), tarka (hypothetical reasoning), nirṇaya (ascertainment), vāda (honest debate), jalpa (wrangling), vitaṇḍā (destructive cavil), hetvābhāsa (fallacies), chala (quibbling), jāti (futile rejoinders), nigrahasthāna (points of defeat). Ten of the sixteen concern *the conduct and pathology of argument*. This is a manual for reasoning and debating your way to liberation: NS 1.1.2 grounds apavarga in the elimination of false knowledge (*mithyā-jñāna*). The machinery — four pramāṇas, the five-membered pañcāvayava inference with its fire-on-the-hill example, the fallacy catalogue — is worked through in Ch. 05.

**The Vaiśeṣikasūtra** (10 adhyāyas, each in two āhnikas, ~370 sūtras) opens *athāto dharmaṃ vyākhyāsyāmaḥ* — "now, therefore, an exposition of dharma" — and defines dharma functionally (VS 1.1.2) as that from which prosperity (*abhyudaya*) and the highest good (*niḥśreyasa*) arise. Then comes the category-list sūtra, **VS 1.1.4, the one sūtra in this chapter fetched verbatim from a citable edition**:

> *dharmaviśeṣaprasūtāt dravyaguṇakarmasāmānyaviśeṣasamavāyānāṃ padārthānāṃ sādharmyavaidharmyābhyāṃ tattvajñānānniḥśreyasam*
> "The Supreme Good results from the knowledge, produced by a particular dharma, of the essence of the predicables — Substance, Attribute, Action, Genus, Species, and Inherence — by means of their resemblances and differences." (Nandalal Sinha tr., 1923)

Six categories of *being* — not of debate. Vaiśeṣika is the unusual thing VS 1.1.4 makes it: **a soteriology whose means is correct ontological taxonomy.** You are liberated by sorting reality correctly, by likeness (*sādharmya*) and difference (*vaidharmya*).

**The two texts' relationship, typed precisely.** The concept files carry the sanctioned two-edge pattern between them: `structurally-parallel-to` (both ground liberation in correct knowledge of an enumerated category-set) *paired with* `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` (they are distinct works with different remits — the Nyāyasūtra is epistemology-and-logic, the Vaiśeṣikasūtra ontology; the later merger of the schools does not make the books one book).

## 3. The six padārthas — and the viśeṣa the school is named for {#padarthas}

*→ concept file: [vaiseshika-sutra.md](../../../concepts/vaiseshika-sutra.md)*

The six categories (*padārtha* — literally "the referent of a word"):

| # | padārtha | English | what it covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **dravya** | substance | the nine substrates everything else inheres in (§4) |
| 2 | **guṇa** | quality | colour, taste, smell, touch, number, magnitude… — real, but only ever *in* a substance |
| 3 | **karma** | motion/action | physical movement — likewise real only in a substance |
| 4 | **sāmānya** | generality/universal | what makes all cows *cow* — an objectively real universal, not a mental grouping |
| 5 | **viśeṣa** | ultimate particular | what makes two otherwise-identical atoms *distinct* |
| 6 | **samavāya** | inherence | the relation binding quality to substance, whole to parts |

Later authors add a seventh, **abhāva** (absence) — the real non-existence of the pot on the table is itself something knowable.

**The fifth category names the school.** *Vaiśeṣika* is the school of *viśeṣa* — the "ultimate differentiator." The problem it solves is generated by the school's own atomism: two earth-atoms have the same qualities, the same size, the same everything; what makes them *two*? Answer: each eternal atom (and each soul, each mind) carries its own unrepeatable viśeṣa. Individuality goes all the way down — it is a category of reality, not a fact about our counting.

**Everything on this list is objectively real.** That is the school's signature. Universals are not concepts (against the Buddhist apoha view, Ch. 05); qualities are not bundles that *are* the thing (against the Buddhist reduction); absence is not merely a way of talking. This maximal realism is what Praśastapāda later defends against Dignāga by name (§8).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — padārtha ≠ Aristotle's categories, though this is the deepest cross-cultural parallel in formal ontology.** The concept file types both edges: `structurally-parallel-to` (two exhaustive category-schemes of being — Kaṇāda's six vs Aristotle's ten, Ch. 14) *and* `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent`. The non-equivalence is specific: Aristotle has nothing corresponding to viśeṣa (a real ultimate particular inhering in eternal atoms), and his substance/accident asymmetry differs from the Vaiśeṣika treatment of dravya and guṇa as co-real categories. Parallel form, different commitments.

> ⚠️ **A pure name-collision, flagged because the map must teach it:** Vaiśeṣika *dharma* (VS 1.1.2 — moral-causal merit, that which yields abhyudaya and niḥśreyasa) has nothing to do with Jain *dharma-dravya* (Ch. 09 — the passive medium of motion). Same word, no shared referent at all.

## 4. The nine dravyas {#nine-dravyas}

*→ concept file: [dravya-vaisheshika.md](../../../concepts/dravya-vaisheshika.md)*

Kaṇāda's definition of substance (as reported in the aggregated sources): dravya is the entity that **possesses qualities and actions and is the inherent cause** (*samavāyi-kāraṇa*) of composite things. The system counts exactly nine:

| # | Sanskrit | English | atomic? | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | pṛthivī | earth | yes | four qualities: touch + taste + colour + smell |
| 2 | āp | water | yes | three: touch + taste + colour |
| 3 | tejas | fire | yes | two: touch + colour |
| 4 | vāyu | air | yes | one: touch |
| 5 | ākāśa | ether/space | no | omnipresent; medium of sound (*śabda*) |
| 6 | kāla | time | no | omnipresent; enables sequence |
| 7 | dik | direction | no | omnipresent; enables location |
| 8 | ātman | soul/self | no | infinite in extent, and *multiple* — one per person, not monist |
| 9 | manas | mind | yes (atomic) | internal sense organ; one per soul |

Four structural observations the corpus can now make, with the Jain six-dravya scheme (Ch. 01/09) on the table beside it:

1. **What Vaiśeṣika has that Jainism lacks:** *manas* as a separate atomic substance, and *dik* — direction promoted to a substance in its own right, distinct from space. (The argument that manas must be atomic — attention is serial; an extended mind touching several sense-organs at once would yield simultaneous multi-sense awareness, which does not occur — is tabled in Ch. 15 §9. It remains the only atomic mind among the traditions this corpus covers.)
2. **What Jainism has that Vaiśeṣika lacks:** *dharma* and *adharma*, the passive media of motion and rest — the Jain system's most distinctive metaphysical invention, with no Vaiśeṣika counterpart.
3. **Plural, infinite ātmans.** The self is a substance among substances — not Brahman (the concept file types the `shares-vocabulary-with` edge against *ātman-vedānta*: same word, entirely different theology — Vaiśeṣika's many souls sit in a theistic system where God combines atoms; Advaita's one ātman *is* the absolute).
4. **The definition-of-substance divergence is doing real work.** Kaṇāda: a substance is what *has qualities and actions*. Jain TS 5.30: a substance is what *originates, decays, and persists*, having qualities and modes. Vaiśeṣika hives *karma* (action) off into its own category; Jainism folds change into the substance's own paryāya. One tradition's ontology separates thing from process; the other builds process into the thing.

## 5. Paramāṇu — the atomism {#paramanu}

*→ concept file: [paramanu-vaisheshika.md](../../../concepts/paramanu-vaisheshika.md)*

The four element-substances bottom out in **paramāṇus**: partless, eternal, indivisible ultimate units, reached — exactly as in the Jain case (Ch. 01) — by regress argument: division cannot proceed forever, so there must be a terminal unit. From there the two atomisms diverge on almost everything.

**Four kinds of atom, with tiered quality-sets.** Unlike the Jain single atom-kind, Vaiśeṣika atoms come in four types, one per element, and the qualities *step down* a ladder:

| atom type | qualities possessed |
|---|---|
| earth atom (pṛthivī-paramāṇu) | touch + taste + colour + smell — all four |
| water atom (āp-paramāṇu) | touch + taste + colour |
| fire atom (tejas-paramāṇu) | touch + colour |
| air atom (vāyu-paramāṇu) | touch only |

Atom types are thus *qualitatively distinct*, and aggregation is type-specific: earth atoms build earth-things, water atoms water-things.

**Dyads and triads.** Two same-type atoms form a *dvyaṇuka* (dyad) — still imperceptible. Three dyads form a *tryaṇuka* (triad) — the smallest perceptible thing. Macroscopic matter is built up from there.

**What makes them move.** Atoms are inherently inert. Combination requires an external cause, and the school names two, in historical layers: ***adṛṣṭa*** — the "unseen power," the accumulated moral force of dharma and adharma residing in souls — and, in the later commentators (Praśastapāda, Udayana), explicitly **Īśvara**, God directing atomic combination into an ordered cosmos. This is the pivot on which Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika became the *theistic* wing of Indian atomism, home of the classical Indian arguments for God's existence.

**The three-way atom comparison** (assembled from the concept file's tables; the Greek side is Ch. 14):

| dimension | Vaiśeṣika | Jain | Democritus |
|---|---|---|---|
| kinds of atom | four (per element), tiered qualities | one kind, all four qualities in every atom | indefinitely many *shapes*, qualitatively neutral |
| combination cause | adṛṣṭa + God (later, explicitly) | natural snigdha/rūkṣa (viscous/dry) affinity; no God | mechanical collision in the void; no God |
| aggregate unit | dyad → triad, rigid sequence | skandha of any size | clusters of any size |
| regress argument | "measurement fails at the limit" | "division cannot regress infinitely" | division must stop somewhere (*a-tomos*, "uncuttable") |
| theism | yes (Udayana's synthesis) | no | no |

Three traditions posit partless ultimate particles by a priori argument, then disagree about how many kinds there are, what qualities they carry, and whether anything directs them. That pattern — same argumentative move, divergent metaphysical elaboration — is the strongest evidence that the atomisms are *independent* solutions to a shared problem, not one doctrine diffusing.

> ⚠️ **Prime-directive checkpoint.** None of this is modern atomic theory. The tiered quality-ladder is sensory, not physical; the dyad/triad arithmetic answers a metaphysical worry (how do partless things build extended things?), not an empirical one; and the combination-mechanism is *moral* (adṛṣṭa) or *theological* (Īśvara). The right edges are `structurally-parallel-to` and `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` — never identity.

## 6. Pañcamahābhūta — the five great elements {#mahabhuta}

*→ concept file: [pancha-mahabhuta.md](../../../concepts/pancha-mahabhuta.md)*

The element-scheme the Vaiśeṣika substances plug into is older and wider than Vaiśeṣika: the **pañcamahābhūta**, the five "great beings" that constitute the material world across Hindu and Āyurvedic thought, each paired with a sensory quality:

| element | Sanskrit | sensory quality |
|---|---|---|
| ether/space | ākāśa | sound (śabda) |
| air | vāyu | touch (sparśa) |
| fire | agni / tejas | form (rūpa) |
| water | ap / jala | taste (rasa) |
| earth | pṛthvī | smell (gandha) |

The Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.1–2.5) gives the emergence sequence — from the self came ākāśa; from ākāśa, vāyu; from vāyu, agni; from agni, ap; from ap, pṛthvī — and Sāṃkhya derives the five from the subtle *tanmātras* as the grossest stage of prakṛti's evolution (Ch. 15 §3). Notice the correspondence with §4's quality-ladder: each element down the Upaniṣadic sequence *adds* one sensory quality, and the Vaiśeṣika atoms inherit exactly that cumulative structure (air = touch only … earth = all four, with ākāśa carrying sound but no atoms).

**How many elements? The traditions genuinely disagree:**

| tradition | count | the difference that matters |
|---|---|---|
| Hindu (Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Āyurveda) | 5 | ākāśa is a primary element |
| Buddhist (Abhidharma; MN 140) | 4 | ākāśa demoted to derived matter; the four are *characteristics* (hardness/fluidity/heat/motion), not substances |
| Greek (Empedocles, Aristotle) | 4 | fire/air/water/earth, no space-element, different philosophical function |
| Jain | — | no mahābhūtas at all: earth-bodied, water-bodied, fire-bodied, air-bodied *jīvas* — souls inhabiting elemental bodies, not elements as world-constituents |

The Jain row deserves the emphasis the concept file gives it: what looks like "the Jains also have the four elements" is actually a claim about *living beings* (Ch. 03's ekendriya jīvas), not about kinds of matter. Jain matter is the single category pudgala (typed `shares-vocabulary-with`, not equivalence).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — mahābhūta ≠ paramāṇu.** The elements are the *types* of matter; the atoms are the *units* within each type. Popular science-religion comparisons run the two together ("the ancients knew matter comes in five elements of tiny atoms"); the concept file draws the `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edge explicitly. Category and instance are different levels of the theory.

## 7. Asatkāryavāda — the effect is genuinely new {#asatkaryavada}

*→ concept file: [asatkaryavada.md](../../../concepts/asatkaryavada.md)*

Ch. 15 §5 presented Sāṃkhya's **satkāryavāda**: the effect pre-exists latently in its material cause — the pot was already in the clay. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika's causal axiom is its exact denial. **Asatkāryavāda** (*a-sat* = non-existent; also **ārambhavāda**, "beginning-theory"): before the pot is made there is **no pot in the clay — not even latently**. Production brings something genuinely new into existence.

**The Nyāya argument:** if the pot already existed in the clay, making it would be pointless — you could simply *uncover* it. But the potter manifestly *produces* something not there before. So production involves real novelty.

**The three conditions of causation** (Nyāya's analysis): a cause must show (a) **antecedence** — it precedes the effect; (b) **invariability** — the effect follows whenever it is present; (c) **unconditionality** — nothing else blocks it. The whole analysis presupposes the effect is a *new item* brought into being by the conjunction of its causes.

| feature | asatkāryavāda (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika) | satkāryavāda (Sāṃkhya-Vedānta) |
|---|---|---|
| pre-existence | effect does NOT pre-exist | effect pre-exists in the cause |
| production is | genuine new creation (ārambha) | manifestation of the latent |
| the pot | new; assembled | was already in the clay |
| causal model | efficient cause brings novelty | material cause reveals content |

**Why the axiom fits the rest of the system.** An atomist-realist ontology practically demands asatkāryavāda: the world consists of plural eternal atoms, and every composite thing is a *fresh assembly* — a new substance in which the parts inhere by samavāya. Conversely, Sāṃkhya's one prakṛti evolving all things practically demands satkāryavāda. Each school's causal axiom and its ontology are two faces of one commitment — which is why Ch. 15 called the two families photographic negatives of each other.

**And the two traditions that refuse the question**, recorded in the concept file so the dichotomy is not mistaken for exhaustive: Buddhist *pratītyasamutpāda* rejects both horns — dependent-arising is designed precisely to avoid asserting either that the effect pre-exists or that a self-standing new entity arises (Ch. 04/12). And Jainism's definition of *sat* as origination-*and*-cessation-*and*-persistence (Ch. 01) straddles the dichotomy rather than picking a side.

## 8. Praśastapāda — the commentary that ate its text {#prashastapada}

*→ concept file: [prashastapada.md](../../../concepts/prashastapada.md)*

**Praśastapāda** (tentatively 6th c. CE) wrote the **Padārthadharmasaṃgraha** ("Compendium of the Properties of the Categories"), formally styled a *bhāṣya* on Kaṇāda's sūtra. In fact it is an independent systematic treatise that reorganises the doctrine — and it **overshadowed the mūla-sūtra**: the later Vaiśeṣika commentarial tradition (Vyomaśiva, Śrīdhara, Udayana) wrote its commentaries on *Praśastapāda*, not on Kaṇāda.

This matters beyond Vaiśeṣika housekeeping, because it inverts the usual shape of §4-method commentary-anchoring. Normally the mūla is primary and the bhāṣya its lens (the Tattvārtha Sūtra tradition of the Jain chapters works this way). Here the "commentary" *became* the doctrinal anchor and the sūtra receded — a live reminder that "read through the commentarial tradition" means following where a tradition's actual authority sits, not assuming the oldest layer always governs.

Doctrinally, Praśastapāda expanded the guṇa-list (adding gurutva/weight, dravatva/fluidity, sneha/viscosity, dharma, adharma, śabda, saṃskāra), systematised the atomism (§5's God-directed combination enters with him), and defended the school's signature realism about universals — **objective constituents of reality, not mental constructs** — against Dignāga, whose apoha theory (Ch. 05) makes universals precisely mental exclusions. One debate, shared vocabulary, opposite answers: the concept file types it `shares-vocabulary-with: dignaga-pramana`.

## 9. Cross-tradition: where Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika sits {#cross-tradition}

Extending the diagnostic table run in Ch. 04/11/15:

| question | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | Sāṃkhya-Yoga (Ch. 15) | Advaita Vedānta (Ch. 11) | Jain (Ch. 01–03/09) | Buddhist (Ch. 12) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| How many kinds of real? | 6 (later 7) padārthas; 9 dravyas | 2: puruṣa + prakṛti | 1: Brahman | 6 dravyas | no substances — dharmas/processes |
| Atoms? | yes — 4 kinds, tiered qualities | no — continuous prakṛti | no — nothing but Brahman is finally real | yes — 1 kind, all qualities | no (mainstream) — momentary events |
| Causation | asatkāryavāda — new effects assembled | satkāryavāda — effects pre-exist | vivartavāda — effects merely apparent | sat = origination + cessation + persistence | pratītyasamutpāda — refuses the dichotomy |
| God? | yes — Īśvara combines the atoms (later school explicit) | Sāṃkhya no; Yoga yes (Īśvara) | Saguna Brahman at the empirical level | no | no |
| Self | plural, infinite ātman-substances | plural puruṣas, never really bound | one ātman = Brahman | plural jīvas, really bound by karma-matter | anattā — no self |
| Universals | objectively real (sāmānya) | — | — | — | mental constructs (apoha) |
| Route to liberation | correct knowledge of the categories (both sūtras' opening claim) | viveka — discriminating puruṣa from prakṛti | jñāna — recognising ātman = Brahman | shedding literal karma-matter | extinguishing craving |

The sharpest single contrast to hold onto: **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika is the maximal realism of this corpus.** Where Buddhism reduces (no self, no substances, universals are constructions) and Advaita sublates (only Brahman is finally real), Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *multiplies*: nine substances, real universals, real particulars, real inherence, real absences — and a God to keep the atoms in order. It is the system you get when the response to every ontological puzzle is to grant the puzzling item its own category of reality — and then to insist, with the opening sūtras of both root texts, that *cataloguing it correctly is itself the path to liberation*.

4. Contrast the three atomisms (Vaiśeṣika, Jain, Democritus) on: kinds of atom, cause of combination, and theism. Why does the pattern of agreement-and-divergence suggest independent invention rather than diffusion?
5. State the Nyāya argument *against* satkāryavāda. Why does an ontology of plural eternal atoms practically require asatkāryavāda, and Sāṃkhya's one-prakṛti ontology the reverse?
6. Name the two traditions in this corpus that refuse the satkāryavāda/asatkāryavāda dichotomy outright, and say how each escapes it.
