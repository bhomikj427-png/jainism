## How to use this chapter

Ch 11 gave the Advaita doctrine. Ch 25 gave the people who transmitted it and the identity problems they came with. This chapter is about the **one question that divided them**, and it is worth a chapter for a reason that is not obvious until you see the shape of it:

**Śaṅkara appears not to have answered it, and possibly to have refused it. His successors answered it in two incompatible ways, and the two answers became two schools that persisted for a thousand years.**

That is a specific and unusual thing for a tradition to do, and watching it happen tells you more about how Indian philosophy actually developed than any amount of doctrine-summary. It also puts the corpus in an unusually good position: as of Batch 42 the split is documented **from both sides by both camps' own literature**, which is rare enough that §6 makes a point of it.

## 1. The question {#question}

### 1.1 Where it comes from

Advaita holds that **Brahman alone is real**, that the self is Brahman, and that the appearance of a world of many things is due to ***avidyā*** — ignorance, or *māyā* under one description. Liberation is the removal of that ignorance.

Now ask a question that any careful student asks in week two: **whose ignorance?**

The options are not comfortable.

- If **the jīva** (the individual self) is ignorant, then jīvas must be real enough to have properties — and Advaita has just conceded plurality at the level that matters.
- If **Brahman** is ignorant, then pure consciousness is the seat of its own occlusion, and you must explain how what is by definition self-luminous fails to illuminate itself.

Neither horn is optional, because *something* has to be ignorant if ignorance is doing any work. This is not a scholastic puzzle invented to fill commentaries; it is the point at which the system's central claim has to be cashed out.

### 1.2 The vocabulary

The dispute is conducted in two technical terms, and keeping them apart is most of the battle:

| term | means | on this question |
|---|---|---|
| ***viṣaya*** | the **object** of avidyā — what is concealed | **not disputed.** All parties agree it is Brahman: what ignorance hides is reality |
| ***āśraya*** | the **locus** of avidyā — where it resides | **this is the whole dispute** |

Anyone who reports the dispute as "one school says avidyā is about Brahman and the other says it is about the jīva" has collapsed the distinction and reported nothing.

## 2. The two answers {#answers}

| | **Vivaraṇa** (Padmapāda → **Prakāśātman**) | **Bhāmatī** (**Vācaspati Miśra**) |
|---|---|---|
| **locus (*āśraya*)** | **Brahman**. "Avidyā rests on Brahman but acts on the jīvas" | **the jīva**. "Avidyā **cannot** abide in Brahman. It abides in the jīvas" |
| **how many avidyās?** | **one** — a single universal *mūlāvidyā* | **plural** — "plural since the jīvas are plural" |
| Brahman's other role | avidyā itself is the material cause of the world, a *jaḍātmikā śakti* | **Brahman** is the material cause; avidyā is its **object**, not its locus; māyā only an *accessory* cause. Brahman beyond māyā is **Īśvara**, its controller |
| jīva ← Brahman | ***pratibimbavāda*** — the jīva is a **reflection** of Brahman in the *antaḥkaraṇa* | ***avacchedavāda*** — avidyā **limits** Brahman and makes it appear as a jīva, "**as a pot limits sky**" |
| what liberates | **textual study** is primary; reflection and meditation are auxiliary. "Karma is responsible for the rise of knowledge of the Self" | ***prasaṅkhyāna-vāda*** — **meditation** is primary. "The *mahāvākyas* do not produce *anubhava*… it is the mind seasoned by meditation that gives such experience." Karma contributes nothing and must be renounced first |
| named for | Prakāśātman's ***Pañcapādikā-vivaraṇa*** | Vācaspati's ***Bhāmatī*** |
| later line | — | Amalānanda (13th c., *Kalpataru*); Appayya Dīkṣita (16th c., *Parimalā*) |

### 2.1 The commentarial stack

Both schools are named after a **commentary**, not after a doctrine — which is itself worth noticing. The Vivaraṇa line is four storeys deep and every storey is a gloss on the one below:

> Bādarāyaṇa's *Brahma Sūtra* → Śaṅkara's *bhāṣya* → Padmapāda's *Pañcapādikā* (on the **first four aphorisms only**) → Prakāśātman's *Vivaraṇa*

**The school is named for the fourth storey.** Bhāmatī is shorter — Vācaspati's *ṭīkā* sits directly on Śaṅkara's *bhāṣya* — and generated six sub-commentaries of its own.

That is how a doctrine acquires a name in this tradition: not by being stated, but by being **glossed by someone whose gloss became the standard one**.

### 2.2 Note the third prasthāna

There are **three** post-Śaṅkara lines, not two. **Sureśvara**'s *Vārttika-prasthāna* (Ch 25) is the third, and on the locus question it stands **with Vivaraṇa**: "the locus of avidyā is Brahman and not the jīvas."

So the founding generation divides **two against one**, with Bhāmatī alone on the jīva side. That matters for how the dispute should be pictured: not a symmetric fork, but a majority position and a minority one that produced the more durable commentarial apparatus.

## 3. The underrated half: how many ignorances? {#howmany}

Most summaries give the locus and stop. The **count** is the more interesting half, and it is where the two schools' costs become visible.

**Bhāmatī pluralises ignorance.** One avidyā per jīva, "plural since the jīvas are plural." That keeps Brahman clean — nothing occludes pure consciousness — at the price of **conceding real multiplicity exactly where bondage happens**. A non-dualism whose ignorance comes in a countable plurality has an awkward question waiting for it.

**Vivaraṇa keeps one ignorance.** A single universal *mūlāvidyā*. That preserves the monism at every level — but must then explain how pure consciousness hosts its own occlusion, and the sources state the objection without softening it:

> if Brahman is the locus of avidyā, then ignorance is generated by *pure consciousness*, and **knowledge and ignorance are predicated of one substrate**.

That objection is *why* the Bhāmatī line moves the locus into the jīva. The two positions are not arbitrary preferences; **each is the other's problem, solved.**

### 3.1 What both share

Both run a **two-tier** scheme, so the difference is not that one has root-ignorance and the other does not:

| tier | name | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| causal | ***mūlāvidyā*** (*kāraṇāvidyā*) | primal nescience |
| effect | ***tulāvidyā*** (*kāryāvidyā*) | derivative; responsible for particular errors, and performing **two** functions — ***āvaraṇa*** (veiling) and ***vikṣepa*** (projecting) |

**The difference is where the root sits and how many roots there are.** That is a much more precise statement of the split than "Brahman vs jīva," and it is available only because the corpus now holds both camps' own literature.

## 4. Prakāśātman and the positive nothing {#prakasatman}

### 4.1 The doctrinal first

**Prakāśātman** gave the Vivaraṇa school its name and made one move that everything downstream depends on:

> **he was the first to propound the theory of *mūlāvidyā* / *māyā* as being of "positive beginningless nature"**

Three claims are packed in there:

1. ***bhāvarūpa*** — avidyā is **positive in form**, not a mere absence of knowledge. It is *something*, not a lack.
2. **beginningless** — it has no origin in time, so no "when did ignorance start?" question arises.
3. **material cause of the world** — the world is *made of* it.

Every source consulted agrees on the consequence: this framework "became the leitmotif of all subsequent developments in the evolution of the Advaita tradition."

### 4.2 The comparison the chapter draws, and the disanalogy it attaches

Making avidyā a **single positive beginningless substrate that is the material cause of the world** puts it into precisely the job-slot that ***prakṛti*** occupies in Sāṃkhya (Ch 15). That is a real structural parallel and the corpus draws it.

It also attaches the difference, in one line:

> **prakṛti is real and independent of puruṣa; *mūlāvidyā* is exactly what Brahman-knowledge cancels.**

A substrate that survives liberation and a substrate that is abolished by it are not the same kind of thing, however similar their causal role. The typed edge in the corpus is therefore **`structurally-parallel-to` *and* `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent`** — the charter's one sanctioned two-type pattern (§5), used here exactly as intended: the similarity is real, the identity is false, and the map has to teach both.

But the availability of the comparison is *itself* significant, and it sets up §6. If Advaita's ignorance ends up doing prakṛti's job, one may reasonably ask **whether the apparatus is Advaita's completion or its Sāṃkhyisation.** The corpus does not settle that. It records that the question is live and that it has a named defender on the other side.

## 5. Vācaspati Miśra: for whom all systems are his own {#vacaspati}

### 5.1 The unusual thing about him

Every other figure in this corpus is a partisan of something. **Vācaspati Miśra** wrote the standard sub-commentary in **five of the six darśanas plus grammar**, and was honoured with the epithet ***sarva-tantra-sva-tantra*** — "**for whom all systems are his own**."

| darśana | work | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Advaita** | ***Bhāmatī*** | on Śaṅkara's *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya*; **names the school**; six sub-commentaries of its own |
| Advaita (polemic) | *Brahmatattva-samīkṣā* | on Maṇḍana Miśra's *Brahmasiddhi* — reported **unpublished** |
| **Sāṃkhya** | ***Tattvakaumudī*** | on the *Sāṃkhyakārikā*; **over 90 medieval manuscripts survive** |
| **Yoga** | ***Tattvavaiśāradī*** | a *ṭīkā* on Vyāsa's *bhāṣya* — a commentary on a commentary |
| **Nyāya** | *Nyāyavārttika-tātparyaṭīkā*; *Nyāyasūcīnibandha* | in the chain running down to Gautama's sūtras via Vātsyāyana |
| **Mīmāṃsā** | *Nyāyakaṇikā*; ***Tattvabindu*** | the latter an **independent** treatise on Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā |

**That the same man wrote the standard gloss on the *Sāṃkhyakārikā* — a dualist, ātman-plural, prakṛti-real system — and the founding text of an Advaita sub-school is the datum.** It cuts hard against reading the darśanas as sealed sects, and it yields a working rule for this whole literature:

> **doxographic reliability and doctrinal allegiance must be assessed separately.**

That rule earns its keep two chapters over. Ch 29 §4 reconstructs **Vārṣagaṇya** almost entirely out of Vācaspati's citations of him — a procedure that only works because he quoted positions he did not hold, accurately.

### 5.2 The *Tattvabindu*, or how a sentence means

His Mīmāṃsā treatise surveys the classical Indian semantics debate in five positions and takes a side:

| theory | claim | holder |
|---|---|---|
| *sphoṭavāda* | meaning grasped as an indivisible whole "burst" | **Maṇḍana Miśra** |
| Nyāya | meaning from concatenating memory traces | Naiyāyikas |
| Mīmāṃsā | traces created by the words themselves | Mīmāṃsakas |
| *anvitābhidhāna-vāda* | words denote only as already-connected | **Prābhākaras** |
| *abhihitānvaya-vāda* | words denote separately, then connect | **Bhāṭṭas** |

**He endorses the Bhāṭṭa view**, using *lakṣaṇā* (implication) to carry the connection.

Look at what that means. An **Advaitin** adopts **Kumārila's Mīmāṃsā** semantics — from the school whose entire metaphysics he rejects — because it is the better theory of how sentences mean. And he does it in a treatise that also rejects **Maṇḍana Miśra**'s *sphoṭavāda*, while elsewhere writing commentaries **on** Maṇḍana. Allegiance and assessment come apart everywhere in this man's work.

This also matters for Advaita specifically, and not only as biography: the school's liberating instrument is a **sentence** — *tat tvam asi* — so a theory of how sentences mean is not a side-interest. See Ch 05 on *śabda-pramāṇa*.

## 6. The third position: that the debate misreads its own root text {#third}

The corpus records a dissent that is not a third answer but a rejection of the question. **Satchidānandendra Sarasvatī** argued that **Padmapāda and Prakāśātman had *misconstrued* Śaṅkara's stance** — that Śaṅkara gave no "location" for avidyā at all, giving precedence instead to its *removal*.

The *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* states Śaṅkara's own position in a way that supports the objection without asserting it:

> **Śaṅkara described avidyā as *beginningless*, and held that to search for the origin of avidyā is itself a process founded on avidyā, and hence fruitless.** His disciples then gave the concept much greater attention, and the two sub-schools are the result.

If that is right, the *āśraya* question is a **post-Śaṅkara construction** — a question the master declined, pressed by successors who did not. The corpus does not adjudicate. What it records is that **the debate's own faithfulness to its root text is contested**, which is exactly the kind of finding a commentary-layered tradition tends to generate. Compare Ch 25 on the *Yogabhāṣya*, where the commentary may not be a commentary at all.

Two things follow for a reader.

**First**, "what does Advaita say about the locus of avidyā?" may have no answer at the level of Advaita-as-such. It has two answers at the level of the sub-schools, and possibly a refusal at the level of Śaṅkara.

**Second**, this is why §4.2's Sāṃkhyisation question has force. If Śaṅkara declined to give avidyā a locus or an origin, and his successors gave it a locus, a positive nature, beginninglessness and the role of material cause, then the apparatus has grown a great deal — and it has grown in the direction of the system Advaita defines itself against.

## 7. Three theories of the self, kept distinct {#three}

The post-Śaṅkara tradition offers **three** accounts of how one Brahman appears as many selves. They are routinely blurred; the corpus keeps them apart.

| theory | claim | who |
|---|---|---|
| ***pratibimbavāda*** — reflection | *bimba* (prototype) is Brahman, *pratibimba* (reflection) is the jīva. "The Jiva is a mere reflection…of Brahman, and therefore, **identical with its essence**, Brahman." Consciousness reflected **in nescience** is Īśvara; the same consciousness reflected **in the inner sense** is the jīva | Padmapāda, **Prakāśātman**, Sarvajñātman |
| ***avacchedavāda*** — limitation | the jīva is Brahman **delimited** by an adjunct, "as a pot limits sky" — not reflected in one. Its holders object that reflection theory "**fails to explain**" how a consciousness with no sensible qualities could be reflected at all | **Vācaspati Miśra** |
| ***ābhāsavāda*** — appearance | the jīva as *ābhāsa*, a partial manifestation "which resembles the real but does not have the properties of the real entity" | **Vidyāraṇya** — *or* Sureśvara's Vārttika line; the attribution is contested (§8.3) |

**The trade-off is clean.** *Pratibimbavāda* buys **non-difference (*abheda*)** at the cost of needing a reflection-medium it cannot fully account for. *Avacchedavāda* buys a coherent account of the medium at the cost of leaning on **difference (*bheda*)**. On the reporting source's own assessment, neither fully escapes the dualism that Śaṅkara's *anirvacanīya māyā* was meant to sidestep.

> **This is the shape of the whole post-Śaṅkara literature: the master's un-analysed terms get analysed, and the analyses split.**

### 7.1 A word warning

***Ābhāsa*** is also a key term in Kashmiri Śaivism, where **Utpaladeva** (Ch 29 §6) uses it for the world as Śiva's **real self-expression**. Same word; **opposite verdict on the world's status**. For the Advaitin an *ābhāsa* lacks the properties of the real; for the Śaiva it is the real, appearing. The corpus stores that as a typed non-equivalence.

## 8. What to do with the dates {#dates}

The doctrine is well attested. The biographies are not, and this chapter's `medium` confidences are mostly about them.

### 8.1 Prakāśātman: a three-century spread

| reading | sources |
|---|---|
| **c. 10th century CE** | IEP; wisdomlib; the formula "the Vivaraṇa School is named after Prakāśātman's (tenth century) commentary" |
| **c. 1200 / 1200–1300** | Wikipedia (*Prakasatman*, *Vivarana*); Hindupedia ("lived in A.D. 1200"); Hindupedia's post-Śaṅkara survey ("A.D. 1220") |

The consequence is not small: on the early dating the Vivaraṇa systematisation **precedes Vidyāraṇya (d. 1391) by four centuries** and is available to him as settled doctrine; on the late one it is nearly contemporary with the Vijayanagara consolidation, and "the leitmotif of all subsequent Advaita" becomes a much shorter story.

**No source argues for its date; each simply states one.** That is the diagnostic. A dispute in which nobody is arguing is not a dispute you can adjudicate from outside — it is a report that the field has not settled, and averaging would manufacture a precision that does not exist.

His **teacher** splits the same way: "disciple of **Ananyānubhava**" (Hindupedia) against "student of **Padmapāda**" (wisdomlib) — the latter **chronologically impossible on either dating**, since Padmapāda is mid-8th century. The corpus reads it as *lineage* language ("of Padmapāda's line") flattened into discipleship, and records it because that is exactly how a commentarial school's genealogy compresses into a guru-śiṣya chain. Compare Ch 29 §5 throughout.

Reference catalogues also list **several distinct persons named Prakāśātman**, sometimes distinguished by *yati* or *svāmin* suffixes — the same homonym hazard recorded for Gauḍapāda in Ch 25.

### 8.2 Vācaspati: one number, two calendars

His only usable anchor is a colophon date of **898** in the *Nyāyasūcīnibandha*, with **the era unstated**.

| era | yields |
|---|---|
| **Vikrama** | c. **840 CE** |
| **Śaka** | c. **976 CE** |

Wikipedia gives both and settles on "9th or 10th century"; Hindupedia's survey states flatly **840**; other reference literature gives **900–980**.

**The era-reading, not the number, is what is unknown.** That is a textbook case of a hard datum underdetermining the fact, and it is worth carrying as a template: a precise-looking figure can be exactly as uncertain as a vague one, and the uncertainty can live somewhere other than in the measurement.

### 8.3 Two loose ends, recorded rather than tidied

**A visible reference-work error.** Hindupedia's own article on Vācaspati carries a note that "He lived in A. D. 788–820" — **Śaṅkara's traditional dates**, evidently copied from an adjacent entry, while the same platform elsewhere dates him to 840. Recorded as a live demonstration of charter §4 signal 1: *copies are not confirmation*.

**The *ābhāsavāda* attribution.** Wikipedia's *Pratibimbavāda* article assigns it to **Vidyāraṇya**; Hindupedia's post-Śaṅkara survey assigns it to **Sureśvara's Vārttika-prasthāna** ("jīvas are *ābhāsas* or appearances of Brahman in the individual minds"). These are compatible if Vidyāraṇya inherits the Vārttika position — likely, but **stated by neither source**, so it stays open.

**The wife legend.** Tradition explains the title *Bhāmatī* as Vācaspati's wife's name, he having been so absorbed in the commentary that he noticed her only when it was done. **No source fetched for this corpus states it.** It is recorded in the node as reported tradition of unverified provenance, flagged rather than asserted — and it is mentioned here because it is the single most-repeated thing anyone says about him, which makes it a good test of whether a corpus is reporting or repeating.

## 9. What this chapter establishes {#establishes}

1. **The dispute is real, precise, and about *āśraya*** — not about what avidyā conceals, which nobody disputes.
2. **It has two answers and three lines**, with Sureśvara's Vārttika line joining Vivaraṇa, leaving Bhāmatī in the minority.
3. **The count matters as much as the locus**: one universal *mūlāvidyā* against one avidyā per jīva, each position being the other's problem solved.
4. **Prakāśātman's *bhāvarūpa* move is the hinge** — positive, beginningless, material cause — and it invites a Sāṃkhya comparison the corpus draws with the disanalogy attached.
5. **Vācaspati is the counterexample to the sealed-school picture**, and the reason the corpus can trust him as a doxographer while disagreeing with him as a philosopher.
6. **A named third position holds that the whole debate misreads Śaṅkara**, who called avidyā beginningless and the search for its origin fruitless.
7. **The doctrine is better attested than the men.** Four independently-published sources agree on the split; the dates disagree by three centuries and by a calendar.

And one methodological result the corpus is entitled to state plainly: **it now holds this dispute stated from both sides by both camps' own literature**, reached in different batches from unrelated source-sets. Batches 40–41 recorded Bhāmatī only through IEP's Vivaraṇa-side vantage. Batch 42 reached it through the Bhāmatī literature — and everything IEP had said about Bhāmatī came back unchanged. **That is what independent corroboration looks like when you get it**, and it is why the doctrinal content of this chapter carries more weight than its single-source biographical content.
