# Chapter 41 — The Word as the World: Bhartṛhari and Grammar's Claim to Be a Darśana

> **Primary concepts:** `bhartṛhari` · `vākyapadīya` · `sphoṭa` · `śabdabrahman`
> **Folder:** `hindu/darsana/` — **and the placement is the chapter's argument.** Its four concepts belong to the same discipline as [Ch 40](../shastra/40-what-grammar-was-for.md)'s eight, which sits in `shastra/`. **The corpus split one tradition across two folders because the tradition itself changed category, and the change was contested.**
> **Reads after:** [Ch 40 (What Grammar Was For)](../shastra/40-what-grammar-was-for.md), necessarily. Also [Ch 38 §7 (The New Logic)](38-the-new-logic.md), which asks how to compare an Indian technical system with a modern one without inflating it — §5 here is the same problem with a much more dangerous claim.

---

## How to use this chapter

Ch 40 left the grammarians as technicians: a preservation problem, a rule-system nobody could count, and a commentarial hierarchy that ran upward. SEP's verdict on them is flat — they were "more interested in language and communication than in **ontology**," and "do not develop a *pramāṇa* theory."

Then one man wrote a book arguing that **reality is language**, that grammar is a path to liberation, and that vyākaraṇa should be counted a *darśana*.

Four things to carry out:

1. **A claim that had to be argued** (§1) — and why the arguing is the evidence.
2. **Where doctrines come from** (§2): a four-way origin dispute that dissolves, and two mechanisms caught manufacturing an antiquity.
3. **The precise metaphysics** (§3) — *pariṇāma*, not *vivarta*, and why the corpus refuses to file this under Advaita.
4. **The most inflatable claim in the corpus** (§5), handled at length because it must be.

⚠ **A warning about the sources, carried from Ch 40 §8.2.** Four reference works give Bhartṛhari four different centuries. This chapter's own best source is one of the outliers.

## §1 A promotion that was claimed, not granted

Vyākaraṇa is **never** one of the six āstika darśanas. It is a *vedāṅga* — an ancillary science, filed with phonetics and metre.

And yet, per IEP, Bhartṛhari "**argues** that grammar can be considered a *darśana*, a 'view,' or an official philosophical school."

> **Notice the verb.** He *argues* for it. **That the promotion had to be argued is the best evidence it was not standardly granted** — nobody argues that Nyāya is a darśana.

So the corpus's sources split three ways, and the split is the finding:

| reading | source | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| a **vedāṅga**, technical not philosophical | Wikipedia — the standard classification; never calls it a darśana | the philosophy is a late overgrowth; Bhartṛhari is an outlier, not the tradition |
| a **darśana**, argued for from inside | IEP — the argument is Bhartṛhari's own | advocacy, not classification |
| **neither** — they were not doing ontology | SEP — no *pramāṇa* theory of their own | the philosophical reading is supplied by the grammarians' *readers* (Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya, Vedānta) |

**The corpus's answer is the third plus the second: a vedāṅga that argued its way toward darśana status, and the argument was Bhartṛhari's, not Pāṇini's.** Hence `vyākaraṇa` files as *śāstra* (Ch 40) and this chapter files as *darśana* — **and neither placement is asserted as settled.**

### §1.1 He wrote the book that earns it

The claim is not merely asserted. Look at what the *Vākyapadīya*'s third *kāṇḍa* is organised on. Its **fourteen sections (*samuddeśa*)** are headed *Jāti* (universal), *Dravya* (substance), *Sambandha* (relation), *Bhūyodravya*, *Guṇa* (quality), *Dik* (direction), *Sādhana*, and others — with *kriyā* (action) and *kāla* (time) among the topics.

> **Those are not grammatical categories. They are the Vaiśeṣika ontological ones** — several of which this corpus already holds as nodes, written years before this batch.

A grammarian's treatise on *words* is structured as a treatise on *what there is*. **Bhartṛhari takes up the categories a philosophical school is expected to handle and treats each as a problem about how language carves the world.**

⚠ Whether that makes vyākaraṇa a darśana, or makes Bhartṛhari a philosopher who happened to be a grammarian, is exactly what the sources split on — and this chapter does not pretend to settle it.

⚠ **And it cuts both ways.** Ch 40 §7.1 recorded that Yāska and Pāṇini use *dravya* for the *nominal as against the verbal* — a grammatical contrast, not Vaiśeṣika's substance. **The shared vocabulary post-dates the divergence.** Reading kāṇḍa III backwards onto the early grammarians would make them ontologists from the start, which they were not.

### §1.2 Four sources, four centuries

| date | source | basis |
|---|---|---|
| c. 400 CE | SEP | unstated — the earliest proposal met |
| c. 450–510 CE | IEP | placement relative to a teacher (Vasurāta) and Dignāga |
| c. 485–540 CE | Wikipedia | "florescence c. 5th to 6th century" |
| **7th century CE** | **Abhyankar** | the older consensus, resting on **Yijing** |

The trouble is one witness. The Chinese pilgrim **Yijing** (635–713) mentions Bhartṛhari in his travel notes; taken at face value his testimony puts him in the 7th century. Wikipedia records the modern verdict: "researchers have found some of the details given by Yi-Jing to be **erroneous**, specifically the **time period**." IEP adds that Yijing also **mistook him for a Buddhist**.

⚠ **A witness demonstrably wrong about a subject's religion is a poor witness for his dates** — and that, rather than any positive evidence, is the ground the 7th-century dating was abandoned on.

> **The current 5th–6th c. consensus rests largely on discrediting the only external witness, not on replacing him.** The corpus leaves the spread open.

**And this is where Ch 40 §8.2's correction bites.** Abhyankar — the batch's best source, read at first hand, repeatedly decisive on technical questions — is here simply behind the field. *First-hand* and *specialist* are strong marks for what a discipline knows internally, and weak for chronology, which depends on outside scholarship that moves.

## §2 Where a doctrine comes from, and how it acquires a past

***Sphoṭa*** — from *√sphuṭ*, "to burst open" — answers a real problem. A word is uttered over time, one sound after another; by the time the last is heard the first has ceased. **No single moment of the utterance contains the word.** Yet meaning arrives whole.

The grammarians' answer: what bears meaning is not the succession of noises (***dhvani***) but a **single indivisible linguistic entity** the noises *manifest* — in Patañjali's phrase, "the sound as it initially **breaks out into the open**."

### §2.1 Four origins, and the question that dissolves them

The batch queued a simple test — did Bhartṛhari invent sphoṭa? — and got four incompatible answers. They resolve once you ask **what exactly is being originated:**

| what | whose | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| the **name** "Sphoṭāyana" | in Pāṇini (A 6.1.123), naming a grammarian | ⚠ proves nothing — see §2.2 |
| the **term** *sphoṭa* and the *sphoṭa*/*dhvani* distinction | **Patañjali**, mid-2nd c. BCE | SEP: "introduced the term… for the first time"; IEP: "explicitly discusses" it |
| the **doctrine** | **Bhartṛhari** | Abhyankar: the grammarians "headed by Bhartṛhari… introduced, by way of deduction from Pāṇini's grammar, an important theory" |
| its **generalisation** | **Bhartṛhari** | Wikipedia: extended "to **each** element of the utterance, *varṇa*, *pada*, and *vākya*," unlike Patañjali's narrower use |

> **He did not invent the term, and he did invent the theory.** The batch's framing — invention versus inheritance — was the wrong question. **No single source states this resolution; the corpus assembles it from four.**

### §2.2 Two mechanisms, caught making the doctrine look older than it is

**(1) A doctrine read out of a proper name.** Pāṇini's rule A 6.1.123 names a grammarian, **Sphoṭāyana**. Abhyankar:

> "It is **doubtful** whether this Sphoṭa theory was advocated before Pāṇini. The word has been put by Pāṇini in the rule **only incidentally** and, in fact, **nothing can be definitely deduced from it**, although **Haradatta** says that [he] was the originator."

And the giveaway is in Abhyankar's other entry, preserving the tradition's own reasoning: Haradatta holds Sphoṭāyana was the first advocate "**and hence he was nicknamed** Sphoṭāyana."

> **The inference runs backwards, from the name to the doctrine.** A folk etymology doing the work of evidence — and IEP repeats it as fact.

⚠ This is precisely Ch 40 §8.1's third row (Jinendrabuddhi deriving Pāṇini's biography from A 4.3.94). **Two commentators, two centuries, one move.**

**(2) A term retrojected by commentators.** This is subtler and more useful:

> "The word [*sphoṭa*] is **not actually found in the Prātiśākhya works**. However, **commentators on the Prātiśākhya works have introduced it in their explanations**" (comm. on Ṛk-Prātiśākhya XIII.4; Taittirīya-Prātiśākhya II.1).

The Prātiśākhyas predate Pāṇini and **do not contain the word. Their commentaries do.** And anyone reading them the way the tradition reads everything — *with* commentary, as one object, which is Ch 40 §2's *munitraya* sense-2 — **finds sphoṭa in the oldest phonetic literature and concludes it is primordial.**

> **The antiquity is an artefact of the reading apparatus.**

⚠ **This is the batch's mechanism in its most consequential form.** Elsewhere it filled a blank about a *person* or supplied a *definition*. Here it manufactures the apparent **antiquity of a doctrine** — which is the thing a doctrine's authority most depends on.

And keep Ch 40 §8.2's asymmetry: **the same mechanism produced a perfectly good account of *pratyāhāra*.** It is neutral. Only checking tells you which case you have.

### §2.3 "The grammarians hold the sphoṭa theory" is true and nearly useless

Abhyankar's *Sphoṭavāda* entry gives the debate's shape in one line — these are treatises "written by the **Vaiyākaraṇas who defend and establish** the theory of Sphoṭa **and by the Naiyāyikas who criticise** it." **Sphoṭa is the doctrine that divides the grammarians from the logicians.**

But SEP records that **Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa and Nāgeśabhaṭṭa "rejected Bhartṛhari's unitary model"** — while Abhyankar lists **both** as authors of works *defending* sphoṭa.

⚠ **Both are right.** They defend *sphoṭa* and reject Bhartṛhari's *akhaṇḍa* (partless) version. The tradition's internal argument is about how many kinds of sphoṭa there are and whether the sentence-sphoṭa is genuinely without parts. **A tradition is not a position.**

*(⭐ A corpus finding in passing: Abhyankar names **Maṇḍana Miśra** as author of a* Sphoṭasiddhi*. `mandana-mishra.md` contains no mention of sphoṭa or grammar at all — the corpus held him purely as Mīmāṃsaka-turned-Advaitin and Śaṅkara's rival. **A node we thought we knew has a second career.** Flagged as a follow-up, not written up on one dictionary line.)*

## §3 *Pariṇāma*, not *vivarta*

***Śabdabrahman*** is the claim that **the ultimate reality is language.** Bhartṛhari's opening verse: "The *Brahman* is without beginning and end, **whose essence is the Word**, who is the cause of the manifested phonemes."

Everything turns on one word in SEP's formulation:

> "language constitutes the ultimate principle of reality (*śabdabrahman*). Both the signifier words and the signified entities in the world are perceived to be a **transformation (*pariṇāma*)** of the ultimate unified principle of language."

| | what is ultimately real | what the world is |
|---|---|---|
| **Śabdādvaita** | *śabda* | its **real transformation** (*pariṇāma*) |
| **Advaita** | *nirguṇa* Brahman | an **appearance** (*vivarta*), ultimately unreal |

> **Two monisms that both say "one reality" and both use the word *brahman* — and they disagree about whether the world happened.**

This is exactly the confusion the map exists to teach, so it is drawn as a typed `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` in both `sabdabrahman.md` and `bhartrhari.md`.

⚠ **And it has a consequence worth registering.** *Pariṇāmavāda* is **Sāṃkhya's** causal doctrine. **Bhartṛhari's monism is structurally closer to Sāṃkhya's causality than to Advaita's** — while being a monism where Sāṃkhya is a dualism. A partial alignment, typed as structure and **not** asserted as influence in either direction: no source consulted claims any.

### §3.1 An antiquity lead, recorded and not cashed

Abhyankar, glossing the *Akṣarasamāmnāya* (the fourteen Māheśvara sūtras behind Ch 40 §4.3's pratyāhāras), says it contains the basic letters of *śabda* "**which is Brahma according to Grammarians**" — citing ***Mahābhāṣya* Āhnika 2 end**.

⚠ **If that holds, the śabda-as-Brahman identification has a Mahābhāṣya anchor** — mid-2nd c. BCE — **and the same shape as §2.1's result recurs**: present in Patañjali, built into a doctrine by Bhartṛhari centuries later.

**Held as suggestive only.** The passage was not read; the scan of that entry is partial; and Abhyankar may be summarising the later tradition rather than Patañjali. **The exact citation is recorded so it can be checked, and nothing further is asserted.**

### §3.2 The doctrine read back onto the discipline

Wikipedia's *Vyākaraṇa* entry frames the whole vedāṅga as existing to keep the Vedic "message of '*Sabda Brahman*'… available to all in a pristine form."

⚠ But Ch 40 §1's **sourced** motive is the far more modest one of preventing corrupt transmission. **A late metaphysics presented as the original point of the enterprise** — the batch's pattern once more, and recorded, not endorsed.

## §4 Against his own tradition

It is tempting to treat "the grammarians" as one voice across eight centuries. **They are not**, and sphoṭa is not where they divide most sharply — sentence-meaning is.

- **Kātyāyana and Patañjali** hold ***abhihitānvaya***: word-meanings are grasped **uncontextualised** and combined afterwards.
- **Bhartṛhari rejects exactly this.** For him the sentence is the real unit; words and phonemes abstracted from it "have **no reality of any kind**. They are ***kalpita*** ('imagined')." The sentence "conveys its entire unitary meaning **in a flash**."
- The apprehension is ***pratibhā***, an "instantaneous flash." IEP's formulation is the sharp one: **"Sentence meaning is produced by word meanings but is not constituted by them."**

> **The *munitraya* and the tradition's greatest philosopher are opposed on the central semantic question** — and later grammarians (Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa, Nāgeśa) went back against Bhartṛhari.

⚠ Note the etymological coherence, which is a genuine constraint rather than decoration: *√sphuṭ* "to burst," Patañjali's "breaks out into the open," Bhartṛhari's *pratibhā* "flash." **The metaphor is stable across eight centuries, and it rules out the common gloss "sphoṭa = the eternal word" as a later metaphysical addition rather than what the word says.**

## §5 ⚠ The most inflatable claim in this corpus

"Reality is made of language" is the single most dangerous sentence the corpus holds. It invites — and constantly receives — translation into information-theoretic physics: *it from bit*, the universe as code, reality as information.

**§0 applies at full strength. The limits, stated rather than gestured at:**

1. ***Śabda* is Sanskrit, not information.** The doctrine concerns *the language of the Veda* — a specific phonology, grammar and liturgical function. It is not about a substrate-neutral notion of information, **a concept it has no means of forming.**
2. **The argument is soteriological, not physical.** The point of śabdabrahman is that **grammatical understanding is a path to liberation**. It predicts nothing, measures nothing, and is not offered as a competitor to a physical account of anything.
3. **The monism is metaphysical, not compositional.** *Pariṇāma* here is the transformation of an absolute into appearances. It is **not** a claim that objects are composed of, or reducible to, discrete symbolic units.
4. **And the comparison is not even to physics.** "It from bit" is a **speculative programme within** physics, not a result. **Comparing an ancient metaphysics to a modern speculation and calling the pair a convergence is doubly unearned** — neither side is a settled claim, so the "agreement" is between two guesses.

> ⚠ **No edge is drawn from `sabdabrahman` to any physics node.** The corpus holds `modern-atom` and `quantum-complementarity` as comparanda and **neither is a defensible target**; the relation would be manufactured. Recorded in prose — the precedent `avacchedaka.md` set, and `karaka.md` followed in this same batch when it declined to link the Pāṇinian Grammar Framework to anything.

**Compare Ch 38 §7.** There, the Navya-Nyāya/modern-logic comparison *earned* its place: Matilal and Ganeri, independently and on different problems, converged on the same structural reading, and Ingalls supplied the limit. **Here there is no such convergence to point at — only a slogan and a resemblance of vocabulary.** The difference between the two chapters' treatment is not squeamishness; **it is the difference between a comparison with evidence behind it and one without.**

## §6 What survives of him

A closing caution about the whole picture.

Bhartṛhari's commentary on the Mahābhāṣya — the ***Mahābhāṣyadīpikā***, called ***Tripādī*** — survives in **a single manuscript**, covering only "**up to Pāṇini 1-1-55**." Kaiyaṭa, in the 11th century, still had a fuller version and cites it in his *Pradīpa*.

And the *Vākyapadīya* itself is reported by two sources as "about **635 verses**" — a figure the corpus **challenges rather than repeats**, because a work whose third *kāṇḍa* alone runs to fourteen separately-edited sections is unlikely to total 635 across all three. **The number looks like a count of kāṇḍas I–II only**, and the identical round figure in both sources suggests they are not independent on the point.

> **The tradition's greatest philosopher-grammarian is known to us through one damaged commentary, a verse-count that does not fit, and a text we mostly read through Helārāja.**

⚠ That last is not incidental. **Helārāja** (10th c.), whose *Prakāśa* commentary on kāṇḍa III is how the *Vākyapadīya* is usually read, is also the route by which this corpus reached the grammarians at all: **Matilal** cites *Vākyapadīya* III **with Helārāja's commentary** for the pervasive/partitive locus distinction — **the grammatical ancestry of Navya-Nyāya's central device** (Ch 38 §4.1).

**Ch 40 §2 said a tradition whose root text lacks definitions has to locate authority downstream. This chapter ends on the same note from the other end:** the corpus reaches Bhartṛhari through his commentator, exactly as it reaches Pāṇini through his — **and `avacchedaka.md` recorded that debt as an open gap for four batches before Batch 47 could pay it.**
