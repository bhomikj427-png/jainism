## How to use this chapter

Sāṃkhya and Yoga are traditionally paired as **sister darśanas** — Yoga takes over Sāṃkhya's entire metaphysical picture and adds two things Sāṃkhya itself does not have: a God, and a method. This chapter follows that pairing. §§1–5 lay out Sāṃkhya on its own terms — the founder-problem, the root text, the dualism, the three guṇas, and the causal axiom that makes the whole system run. §§6–8 show what Yoga adds and keeps. §§9–10 take up *citta* and *manas*, two "mind" words that recur across this corpus's other traditions with quietly incompatible meanings — the chapter uses them as a worked case study in why shared vocabulary is not shared doctrine. §11 closes with cross-tradition contrasts against material already covered in Ch. 04, 11, 12, and 13.

**Frequent conflation alert you should know before starting:**

*Sāṃkhya ≠ Yoga, despite the shared metaphysics.* Because Yoga borrows Sāṃkhya's entire ontology wholesale, it is tempting to treat "Sāṃkhya-Yoga" as one system with two names. It is not: classical Sāṃkhya is *nirīśvara* (atheistic) and proceeds by discriminative knowledge through three formal means of valid knowledge (*pramāṇa*); Yoga is theistic (it admits **Īśvara**, a special never-bound puruṣa) and proceeds by an eight-limbed meditative praxis. Same map, different god-status and a different route across it.

## 1. Kapila — the founder who left no text {#kapila}

*→ concept file: [kapila.md](../../../concepts/kapila.md)*

Sāṃkhya's darśana-literature traces itself to **Kapila**, traditionally placed in the 7th–6th century BCE — old enough that tradition says Kapilavastu, the Buddha's natal city, was named in his honour, and old enough to "pre-date the Buddha." But no text demonstrably Kapila's survives: the *Sāṃkhyasūtra* attributed to him is a late work (~14th century CE), centuries too late to be his. He is, in his own file's phrase, "an anchor by attribution" — the name the whole school projects backward onto — not a verifiable author. What *is* recoverable is the teaching-lineage the tradition claims: **Kapila → Āsuri → Pañcaśikha → … → Īśvarakṛṣṇa**, closing with the author of the text this chapter actually reads (§2).

**A genuine doctrinal split hiding under one name.** The Purāṇas — especially the Bhāgavata Purāṇa — recast Kapila as an **avatāra of Viṣṇu**, son of Kardama and Devahūti, teaching his mother a *theistic*, devotional form of Sāṃkhya. This is not a minor variation: the darśana's Kapila founds an atheistic system that needs no creator God; the Purāṇic Kapila is a personal-God incarnation preaching bhakti. One name, two doctrinally opposed figures — recorded as a genuine `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` split in the concept file, not smoothed into "Kapila had many aspects."

## 2. The Sāṃkhyakārikā — the text that actually exists {#samkhyakarika}

*→ concept file: [samkhya-karika.md](../../../concepts/samkhya-karika.md)*

Because nothing of Kapila's survives, classical Sāṃkhya as we can actually read it is **Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṃkhyakārikā** ("Verses on Sāṃkhya," ~350 CE, Gupta period) — "the earliest extant complete text" of the school, its date fixed on the far end by Paramārtha's Chinese translation, completed by 569 CE. It is short: 72 verses (some manuscripts 73; verse 72 itself claims the original had 70) in the Āryā metre. Later commentaries — Gauḍapāda's bhāṣya, the *Yuktidīpikā*, Vācaspati Miśra's *Tattvakaumudī* — elaborate the kārikā rather than revise it.

**The motivating problem (verse 1).** The inquiry opens from *duḥkha* of three kinds: *ādhyātmika* (self-caused, bodily/mental), *ādhibhautika* (caused by other beings), *ādhidaivika* (caused by nature/the supernatural). Ordinary remedies — physicians, ritual, scripture — give only temporary relief; the kārikā offers discriminative knowledge as the route to *absolute* cessation of all three.

**Epistemology.** Sāṃkhya admits exactly **three** *pramāṇas*, in a fixed hierarchy: perception (*dṛṣṭa*), inference (*anumāna*), and reliable testimony (*āptavacana*) — testimony invoked only where the first two are silent. (Ch. 05 covers Nyāya's four and Mīmāṃsā's five/six for comparison — Sāṃkhya's list is the leanest of the darśanas covered in this corpus.)

**Classical Sāṃkhya is atheistic** (*nirīśvara*): the entire 25-tattva evolution proceeds from prakṛti's own guṇa-dynamics, drawn out by puruṣa's mere presence — no creator God is invoked or required. This atheism is a load-bearing structural fact, not incidental: it is exactly what Yoga adds back (§6).

## 3. The dualism — puruṣa and prakṛti {#dualism}

*→ concept files: [purusha-samkhya.md](../../../concepts/purusha-samkhya.md) · [prakriti-samkhya.md](../../../concepts/prakriti-samkhya.md)*

Sāṃkhya reduces all of reality to exactly two ultimate, irreducible, co-eternal categories — a hard dualism unlike any other system in this corpus:

| | **Puruṣa** | **Prakṛti** |
|---|---|---|
| what it is | pure, passive consciousness — the witness | primal, unconscious, material principle |
| activity | never acts, never transforms; mere proximity disturbs prakṛti's equilibrium | active; evolves the entire manifest world, physical *and* mental |
| number | infinitely many, each a distinct individual consciousness (pluralist) | one, undivided |
| composition | no guṇas | constituted entirely by three guṇas (§4) |
| Sāṃkhyakārikā 19 | "the conscious witness, separate, neutral, seer, and inactive" | — |

**Puruṣa is never really bound.** Bondage is an apparent state — puruṣa mistakenly identifies with the body-mind complex (which is entirely prakṛti's product). This is a *cognitive error*, not a physical adhesion. Liberation, **kaivalya** ("isolation"), arises from *viveka-khyāti* — discriminating clearly that "I am not this body/mind/ego." No karma is removed, because nothing was ever really attached; kaivalya is recognition, not purification.

**Prakṛti evolves everything else — including mind.** From prakṛti's disturbed guṇa-equilibrium unfold the 25 tattvas: prakṛti → *mahat/buddhi* (cosmic intelligence) → *ahaṃkāra* (ego-principle) → [sattva branch] the ten sense/action organs plus *manas* (mind, §9) → [tamas branch] the five subtle *tanmātras* → the five gross *mahābhūtas*. Puruṣa is the 25th principle — witness, not evolute.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Sāṃkhya's dualism is not Cartesian mind/body dualism.** Manas, buddhi, and ahaṃkāra — everything a Western reader would call "mind" — are **all** evolutes of prakṛti, the *matter* side. The Sāṃkhya divide runs between *consciousness* (puruṣa) and *everything else, mental or physical* (prakṛti), not between mind and body. Jainism and Vaiśeṣika disagree with this placement in different ways: Jainism has no manas-dravya at all; Vaiśeṣika makes manas a *ninth, separate* atomic substance. Sāṃkhya's "mind is matter" move is its own, not a default Indian position.

## 4. The three guṇas {#gunas}

*→ concept file: [guna-samkhya.md](../../../concepts/guna-samkhya.md)*

Prakṛti is not an undifferentiated blob — it is **entirely composed** of three constitutive strands (*guṇas*), always present together, everywhere, in every phenomenon, mental or physical:

| guṇa | character | manifests as |
|---|---|---|
| **Sattva** | clarity, harmony, lightness | intellect, cognition, virtue |
| **Rajas** | activity, passion, mobility | movement, desire, dynamism |
| **Tamas** | heaviness, inertia, obscurity | solidity, resistance, ignorance |

It is a thing's *proportion* of the three, not the presence of only one, that characterises it — a stone is tamas-dominant, a philosopher's intellect sattva-dominant, a warrior's anger rajas-dominant, but all three are present in each. When the guṇas sit in perfect equilibrium, prakṛti is *avyakta* (unmanifest, pure potential); puruṣa's proximity disturbs that balance, and the guṇa-imbalance that follows *is* the manifest, created world.

Scholarship is not settled on what guṇas fundamentally *are* — the concept file records three live positions (substances constituting prakṛti; qualities/properties of it; functional capacities/tendencies) without adjudicating between them.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Sāṃkhya guṇa ≠ Jain guṇa.** Both traditions use the word *guṇa*, and both mean "quality," but structurally they diverge: Sāṃkhya's three guṇas are universal — every particle of prakṛti has all three, always, in some proportion. Jain guṇa is dravya-specific — each of the six substances has its *own* defining guṇas (pudgala's rūpa/rasa/gandha/sparśa; jīva's upayoga), and there is no universal fixed triad running through all of them. Sāṃkhya guṇa is *constitutive* (prakṛti simply *is* the three guṇas); Jain guṇa is *descriptive* (an attribute of a substance, not identical with it).

## 5. Satkāryavāda — the effect already exists {#satkaryavada}

*→ concept file: [satkaryavada.md](../../../concepts/satkaryavada.md)*

Sāṃkhya's causal axiom, and the reason prakṛti can evolve into a world without anything genuinely new coming into being: **satkāryavāda** — the effect *pre-exists*, latently, in its material cause before production. The pot was already there in the clay; cloth in the threads. Sāṃkhyakārikā 9 gives five arguments (directly fetched, Sanskrit quoted in the concept file):

1. Non-being cannot be produced — nothing from nothing.
2. Every effect requires a material cause that supplies its substance.
3. Not everything arises from everything — clay becomes a pot, gold does not.
4. A cause produces only what corresponds to its own inherent capacity.
5. The effect shares the nature of its cause.

**This single axiom splits Vedānta in two** (Ch. 11 §3.2/§4.1 covered the result without deriving it): Advaita's *vivartavāda* (the effect is *apparent* only — Brahman does not really change) and Viśiṣṭādvaita's *pariṇāmavāda* (the effect is *genuinely real* — the cause truly transforms) are both satkāryavāda-accepting variants that disagree only on whether the manifested effect is real or merely apparent. Sāṃkhya itself is squarely pariṇāmavāda: prakṛti *really* transforms into the tattvas.

**The direct opposite** is Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika's **asatkāryavāda** (*ārambhavāda*): the effect is entirely new, assembled fresh from plural eternal atoms, and does not pre-exist in its cause at all. The two axioms are mutually exclusive — one darśana-family's whole causal picture is the photographic negative of the other's (Ch. 16 covers the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika side).

## 6. From Sāṃkhya to Yoga — Patañjali and "Sāṃkhya with God" {#patanjali}

*→ concept file: [patanjali.md](../../../concepts/patanjali.md)*

**Patañjali** compiled the **Yoga Sūtra** (dated broadly 2nd century BCE – 5th century CE; most scholarship narrows to 1st–4th century CE, Philipp Maas argues for ~400 CE on commentarial grounds — "the only certainty is that it predates the 5th century CE"). Almost nothing biographical is known about him. The text's own opening signals his role precisely: **YS 1.1**, *atha yogānuśāsanam* — "now, the *continuation* of the teaching of yoga." The prefix *anu-* marks continuation of a prior teaching, not founding one from nothing: Patañjali systematised pre-existing, heterogeneous yogic practice into 196 sūtras so authoritatively that the result became, in the concept file's phrase, "the canon for the mechanics of generic Yoga" and "the most translated ancient Indian text in the medieval era."

**What Yoga takes from Sāṃkhya, and what it adds.** Patañjali, described as "a notable scholar of the Sāṃkhya school," builds directly on the puruṣa/prakṛti dualism, the three guṇas, and kaivalya as the goal — taking over Sāṃkhya's entire metaphysics wholesale. What Yoga adds is exactly two things Sāṃkhya lacks: **Īśvara** (a special, never-bound puruṣa, an optional-but-favoured object of devotion — "by the grace of God") and an **eightfold meditative praxis** (aṣṭāṅga, §7). Hence Yoga is called "*seśvara* Sāṃkhya" — Sāṃkhya-with-a-God.

> ⚠️ **A tempting but false identification, flagged and rejected.** A persistent tradition equates the Yoga Sūtra's Patañjali with the *grammarian* Patañjali who wrote the Mahābhāṣya (the great commentary on Pāṇini). This is almost certainly wrong: Louis Renou documented "significant differences in language, grammar and vocabulary" between the two bodies of work, and the equation is first made only in Bhojadeva's *Rājamārtaṇḍa* (~10th century) — a late conflation of two unrelated authors who happen to share a name, centuries after both wrote.

## 7. Yoga darśana — the eight limbs {#yoga-darshana}

*→ concept file: [yoga-darshana.md](../../../concepts/yoga-darshana.md)*

The Yoga Sūtra is organised into four *pādas* (books): *Samādhi* (states of mind), *Sādhana* (the practical path), *Vibhūti* (powers arising from practice), *Kaivalya* (liberation). Its defining sentence, **YS 1.2**, is taken up on its own in §8. The practical method is the **aṣṭāṅga**, eight limbs proceeding from outer to inner (YS 2.29):

| limb | Sanskrit | content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | yama | restraints: *ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha* |
| 2 | niyama | observances: *śauca, santoṣa, tapas, svādhyāya, īśvara-praṇidhāna* |
| 3 | āsana | posture |
| 4 | prāṇāyāma | breath-control |
| 5 | pratyāhāra | sense-withdrawal |
| 6 | dhāraṇā | concentration |
| 7 | dhyāna | meditation |
| 8 | samādhi | absorption |

The final three (dhāraṇā + dhyāna + samādhi) jointly form **saṃyama**, the integrated meditative discipline; the preparatory **kriyā-yoga** is *tapas + svādhyāya + īśvara-praṇidhāna* (YS 2.1). Bondage is rooted in five **kleśas**, chief among them *avidyā*.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Yoga's yamas are not Jain mahāvratas, despite naming the identical five terms.** *Ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha* are, word for word, the Jain five great vows (Ch. 08). But the weight is different: in Yoga, the yamas are *preparatory ethical restraints* on the road to kaivalya, one limb of eight; in Jainism, the same five are *totalising lifelong vows* grounded in a physicalist karma-ontology where every violation binds literal karma-matter to the jīva. Same five Sanskrit words, different metaphysical stakes entirely.

> ⚠️ **A second, structural conflation alert — aṣṭāṅga is not the guṇasthāna ladder**, though both are graded ascents to liberation (Ch. 03/07). The eight aṅgas are a *practice-sequence* one performs; the fourteen guṇasthānas are *states of karmic purification* one passes through, tracked by karma-shedding rather than a training regimen. A path-of-practice and a ladder-of-states are not the same kind of structure even when both are drawn as ordered stages.

## 8. Citta-vṛtti — Yoga's defining formula {#citta-vritti}

*→ concept file: [citta-vritti.md](../../../concepts/citta-vritti.md)*

**YS 1.2**: *yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ* — "yoga is the cessation of the modifications of the mind." Patañjali defines the entire discipline through this one sentence. **Citta**, in the Sāṃkhya-Yoga system, is the mind-organ — a *material* modification of prakṛti, not a separate immaterial substance. It is illuminated by puruṣa (the witness) but is not itself conscious. Liberation is what happens when citta's *vṛttis* (fluctuations) cease: puruṣa stops misidentifying with the mind's activity and rests in its own nature (*sva-rūpa*).

**The five vṛttis (YS 1.5–1.11):**

| # | vṛtti | nature |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | pramāṇa | valid cognition (perception/inference/testimony) |
| 2 | viparyaya | false cognition/error |
| 3 | vikalpa | conceptual construction without a real referent — e.g. "the son of a barren woman" |
| 4 | nidrā | deep dreamless sleep |
| 5 | smṛti | memory |

*Nirodha* (the goal) is not suppression of consciousness — puruṣa remains throughout; what ceases is only the *misidentification* of puruṣa with citta's fluctuating activity.

**A genuinely independent cross-tradition convergence, worth flagging on its own terms.** *Vikalpa* (vṛtti #3) — conceptual construction lacking a real referent — is close kin to what Dignāga's Buddhist apoha theory (Ch. 05) calls *kalpanā*: the conceptual overlay that pure perception does not itself contain. Yoga's response is to *still* it; the Buddhist epistemological response is to explain *why inference, not perception, is its proper domain*. Two traditions independently identify the same cognitive phenomenon and prescribe opposite treatments for it.

## 9. Citta and manas — one syllable, four unrelated metaphysics {#citta-manas}

*→ concept files: [citta.md](../../../concepts/citta.md) · [manas.md](../../../concepts/manas.md)*

This corpus now has enough tradition-coverage to make citta and manas into a worked case study of §0's warning against reading modern or cross-tradition meaning off a bare Sanskrit/Pali word.

**Citta means opposite things in Buddhism and in Sāṃkhya-Yoga — not merely different things.**

| | Buddhist citta | Sāṃkhya-Yoga citta |
|---|---|---|
| what it is | consciousness *itself* — a processual stream of momentary acts of awareness | a **material** cognitive organ, an evolute of prakṛti |
| is it conscious? | yes — citta *is* the awareness | no — citta is illuminated *by* puruṣa, like a mirror reflecting light; it does not itself cognize |
| permanence | impermanent, moment-to-moment (compatible with anattā — no self behind the stream) | evolves with prakṛti; relatively stable as a faculty |
| liberation | citta ceases grasping; the stream stops (nirvāṇa) | puruṣa recognises it is *not* the citta (*viveka*); citta itself need not stop, only the misidentification does |

This is a direct inversion, not a family resemblance: Buddhist citta *is* the consciousness-process; Sāṃkhya-Yoga citta is the *object* consciousness illuminates from outside. The concept files for both `citta.md` and `citta-vritti.md` record this explicitly as `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` on both sides of the pair.

**Manas fragments further still — five traditions, five different ontological commitments**, tabulated in full in `manas.md`:

| tradition | what manas is | ontological status |
|---|---|---|
| Jain | *mano-yoga*, one of three activity-channels (kāya/vāk/manas) | not a sense-organ (*anindriya*) — a mode of soul-activity |
| Buddhist (early) | the 6th sense-base; alongside citta and vijñāna | conditioned process, not a substance |
| Buddhist (Yogācāra) | *manas-vijñāna*, the 7th of eight consciousnesses | self-referential clinging to ālaya-vijñāna as if it were a permanent self |
| Sāṃkhya | one component of the *antaḥkaraṇa* (inner organ), alongside buddhi and ahaṃkāra | material — part of evolving prakṛti |
| Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | an atomic, partless internal sense organ | a *dravya* (substance) in its own right — the only atomic mind among the traditions covered |

The **Nyāya atomicity argument** is worth isolating on its own: manas must be partless because attention is serial, not parallel — if manas had spatial extension able to contact multiple sense-organs at once, simultaneous multi-sense awareness should follow, and it does not. This is the *only* system in the corpus that argues mind itself is literally an atom.

## 10. Cross-tradition: where Sāṃkhya-Yoga sits among the other systems {#cross-tradition}

Building on the diagnostic table introduced in Ch. 04/11 (ultimate reality / individual self / liberation) and Ch. 13's Neoplatonic comparanda:

| question | Sāṃkhya | Yoga | Advaita Vedānta (Ch. 11) | Jain (Ch. 01–03) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ultimate reality | two irreducible categories: puruṣa (plural) + prakṛti (one) | same as Sāṃkhya, plus Īśvara | one Brahman, without parts | six real substances (plural, eternal) |
| Is the divine required? | no — *nirīśvara*, explicitly atheistic | yes — Īśvara, optional-but-favoured | Saguna Brahman in theistic readings; none in strict Advaita | no — universe beginningless, self-regulating |
| Status of mind | material — an evolute of prakṛti, not itself conscious | same as Sāṃkhya (citta as material organ) | ultimately non-different from Brahman (in the fully non-dual reading) | a mode of the conscious jīva-substance (upayoga) itself, not separate matter |
| Liberation mechanism | *viveka-khyāti* — recognizing puruṣa ≠ prakṛti | same recognition, reached via the eight-limbed praxis rather than pramāṇa-based inquiry alone | *jñāna* — recognising ātman = Brahman | individual effort exhausting all karma-matter |
| Is bondage physical? | no — a cognitive error (false identification) | no — same as Sāṃkhya | no — *avidyā*, ignorance | yes — karma is literal subtle matter adhering to the jīva |

The sharpest single contrast to hold onto: **Sāṃkhya-Yoga's bondage is entirely cognitive** — puruṣa was never actually touched by prakṛti, so liberation is *recognition*, requiring no removal of anything. **Jain bondage is literally physical** — karma-particles adhere to the jīva as matter, so liberation requires the tapas/dhyāna machinery of Ch. 08 to actually shed accumulated substance. Two traditions can each describe a "path to liberation through discipline and insight" while disagreeing completely about whether there is any *stuff* to get rid of.
