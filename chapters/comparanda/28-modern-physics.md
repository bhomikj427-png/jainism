## How to use this chapter

Every chapter in this corpus has been written under one standing instruction. §0 of the charter:

> *Understand what these texts actually argued — the philosophy **and** the proto-science — **on their own terms**. NEVER inflate them into modern physics. "The ancient sages already knew quantum mechanics" is the exact failure mode this project exists to prevent. **Analogy is not identity.***

This is the chapter that instruction is about. It has only two primary concepts, and neither is ancient: the **modern atom** and **quantum complementarity** are here purely as comparanda — nodes that exist so the graph can draw a line *and mark it as a line that must not be crossed*.

Which raises an obvious question, and it is worth answering before starting. **Why write the chapter at all?** If the corpus's business is understanding ancient texts on their own terms, why give two pieces of 20th-century physics a chapter?

Because the conflations are already out there, and refusing to address them is not neutrality — it leaves the field to them. "Jain *paramāṇu* = atom" and "*anekāntavāda* = quantum complementarity" are the two claims a curious reader is most likely to have met before opening anything in this corpus. A project that describes Jain atomism carefully and never says what it is *not* has handed that reader a more sophisticated version of what they already believed. So:

| § | what it does |
|---|---|
| **§2** | the atom case, worked in full — including the one break that most accounts miss |
| **§3** | the complementarity case, and the honest analogy that was later inflated |
| **§4** | the **anatomy** of the slide: five moves that turn an analogy into an identity |
| **§5** | the corpus's complete inventory of modern-science non-equivalence edges |
| **§6** | four tests that break the inflation, generalised from §§2–3 |
| **§7** | the **opposite** error, which this corpus has also had to guard against |

Read after Ch 01 (paramāṇu), Ch 02 (anekāntavāda, syādvāda), and Ch 06 (which does the *logic* comparanda properly and is not repeated here). Leave `> stuck` on its own line where you get lost; paste the teaching prompt and it will re-explain in place.

## 1. What a comparandum node is for {#comparandum}

### 1.1 Two jobs, and this chapter's is the second

The corpus's typed links (charter §5) divide cross-tradition relations into two kinds, and modern-science nodes sit almost entirely in the second:

- ***structurally-parallel-to*** — a real similarity of shape, honestly drawn, asserting nothing about identity;
- ***often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent*** — an explicit warning: people *do* equate these, and here is why they are wrong.

The charter is blunt about which applies here: **"A physics link is almost always `structurally-parallel-to` or `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` — essentially never `is-a` or equivalence."** And the instruction that matters most is the one about *not* omitting: false-equivalence edges must be **drawn and flagged, never silently left out. The map must teach the distinction.**

That is the design decision behind this chapter's existence. A graph that simply omitted modern physics would be cleaner and would teach nothing about the most common error its readers arrive with.

### 1.2 An unusual thing about confidence in these two nodes

`modern-atom` carries **confidence: high** — the only high-confidence node in this chapter, and one of relatively few in the corpus. Read the reason carefully, because it is not what a `high` normally means here:

> The physics facts — subatomic structure, empirical discovery, divisibility — are settled textbook science. **The high confidence attaches to the physics, *not* to any claim that the comparison is the "correct" reading of the ancient atomists.**

That is a real distinction and it generalises. A comparandum file contains two very different kinds of claim: **what the modern thing is** (settled, high) and **how it relates to the ancient thing** (interpretive, and only as good as the ancient-side chapter that established it). Collapsing the two would let textbook physics lend its authority to a comparative judgment it cannot underwrite.

So the discipline of this chapter is: **every ancient-side claim is imported from the chapter that established it, with its own confidence intact.** The Jain paramāṇu material comes from Ch 01 at Ch 01's confidence. The anekāntavāda material comes from Ch 02 at Ch 02's. Nothing new about any ancient text is asserted here. This chapter's own contribution is entirely in the *comparisons* and in §§4–7.

## 2. The atom that isn't an atom {#atom}

*→ concept file: [modern-atom.md](../../concepts/modern-atom.md) — `converged`, confidence **high** (on the physics)*

### 2.1 The modern atom, stated plainly

The modern atom is **the smallest unit of a chemical element** — and it is neither the smallest unit of matter nor indivisible.

- **Nucleus:** protons (charge +1; two up quarks and one down quark) and neutrons (neutral; one up and two down), bound by the strong force.
- **Electron cloud:** electrons (charge −1) in quantum-mechanical probability distributions — **orbitals, not orbits**.
- Protons and neutrons are themselves **composite**.

Four properties will do all the work below:

| property | the modern atom |
|---|---|
| **has parts** | protons, neutrons, electrons, quarks |
| **empirically discovered** | Dalton's law of definite proportions (1803) → Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911) → the Bohr model (1913) → the quantum-mechanical model |
| **divisible and destructible** | fission splits nuclei; radioactive decay transforms atoms; accelerators probe substructure |
| **no intrinsic sensory qualities** | it has mass, charge, spin, energy levels — **not** colour, taste, or smell; those are macroscopic and emergent |

### 2.2 The word is a fossil

Here is the fact that generates the entire confusion, and it is a historical accident rather than a discovery.

Greek *átomos* means **"uncuttable."** **Dalton, in 1803, explicitly revived Democritus's term** for his chemical unit. Within a century that unit was found to have parts — and the name stayed. So the modern atom is called "the indivisible" and is *divisible*, and the discipline that named it knows this and does not mind, because in physics a name is a label, not a definition.

**The borrowing is nominal only.** Dalton took a word, not a doctrine. But the word carries an argument inside it, and a reader who does not know the naming history will hear the ancient claim inside the modern term every time — which is why "the Greeks/Jains discovered the atom" is such a stable error. It is not really an inference; it is a **pun** that people have stopped hearing as a pun.

### 2.3 Three ancient atomisms against one modern one

The corpus stores `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent: modern-atom` on three separate ancient nodes — Jain **paramāṇu** (Ch 01), Vaiśeṣika **paramāṇu** (Ch 16), and Greek **democritus-atom** / **epicurus-atom** (Ch 14). Laid out together:

| dimension | **Jain paramāṇu** | **Vaiśeṣika paramāṇu** | **Greek átomos** | **modern atom** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| indivisible? | **yes** — partless by definition | **yes** — partless by definition | **yes** — "uncuttable" by definition | **no** |
| argued or found? | **argued a priori** — infinite regress of division is impossible | **argued a priori** — a limit of measurement/divisibility | **argued a priori** — to halt infinite regress | **found empirically** |
| sensory qualities intrinsic? | **yes** — one each of colour, taste, smell, and two of touch | **tiered by element type** | no | **no** — emergent and macroscopic |
| eternal? | **yes** | **yes** | **yes** | **no** — fission, decay |
| smallest unit of matter? | **yes**, definitionally | **yes**, definitionally | **yes**, definitionally | **no** — quarks are smaller; leptons have no measured size |

Read the second row across. **Four systems, and the modern one is alone in its column.** Whatever else divides Jain, Vaiśeṣika and Greek atomism from each other — and they divide substantially — all three are conclusions of *arguments*, and the modern atom is the residue of *experiments*. That is not a difference of detail. It is a difference in what kind of thing the claim is.

And the corpus draws the honest positive edge too, which is the other half of doing this properly: **`paramanu structurally-parallel-to paramanu-vaisheshika`** — Jain and Vaiśeṣika atomism *are* genuinely comparable, both positing partless ultimate units by an infinite-regress argument, differing in that the Jain has one kind of atom bearing all four qualities and the Vaiśeṣika has four kinds binding into dyads and triads. **Comparison within the period is often solid. It is the jump across the modern break that fails.**

### 2.4 The break that most accounts miss

Of the four rows, the third is the one that decides the case, and it is the one usually skipped because it sounds like a curiosity.

> **The Jain paramāṇu has colour, taste, smell and touch as *intrinsic* properties.** The modern atom does not — those are emergent, macroscopic, and in the modern account are *produced by* atomic-scale structure rather than possessed at it.

Why this is decisive rather than incidental: **the Jain system needs that property to work.** Ch 26 §5 set out *leśyā*, the karmic colouring of the soul — the soul takes on the colour of the karma-matter bonded to it, the way a crystal takes the colour of its surroundings. That doctrine requires matter to carry colour intrinsically **at the finest grade**, since *kārmaṇa-vargaṇā* is the subtlest matter there is (Ch 26 §3). So this is not an ornamental feature of Jain atomism that could be quietly dropped in the light of modern chemistry. It is load-bearing for the soteriology.

Which yields a clean statement of why the identification cannot be repaired: **the property that makes the Jain system work is precisely the property modern matter lacks.** You cannot hold "paramāṇu = atom" *and* keep leśyā. Anyone who equates them has, without noticing, deleted a doctrine.

### 2.5 The Epicurean sub-case: the swerve and quantum randomness

One more conflation, because it is common and instructive in a different way. **Epicurus's *clinamen*** — the unpredictable minimal swerve of an atom, introduced to make room for free will and to get atom-collisions started in a downward rain — is "frequently but wrongly read as anticipating quantum randomness" (the corpus's own words on the `epicurus-atom` node).

Note what makes this one seductive: unlike "atom," here the *structural* resemblance is real. Both posit genuine indeterminacy at the smallest scale, in a system otherwise deterministic. That is a better starting point than a shared name.

And it still fails, for the §2.3 reason plus one more. The clinamen is **posited to secure an outcome** — it exists because Epicurus needs free will and needs the atoms to collide. Quantum indeterminacy is **found**, and it is not there to secure anything; it falls out of the formalism whether or not anyone likes its consequences. A posit introduced *because a system requires it* and a result obtained *despite what anyone wanted* are different kinds of claim even when they describe similar-sounding behaviour.

### 2.6 The icon in the collider: Naṭarāja

The corpus stores one further `modern-atom` non-equivalence, and it is not about atomism at all. On the **naṭarāja** node (Ch 18 §4.2):

> The **Capra/CERN** reading — "the cosmic dance ≈ the ceaseless creation and destruction of subatomic matter" — treats Naṭarāja as **a picture of modern particle physics**. It is in fact a **Śaiva Siddhānta soteriological icon**: the *pañcakṛtya*, the five acts, whose point is the freeing of souls from *māyā*. The analogy is **imposed from outside**, not found inside.

In **2004** India presented CERN with a **two-metre Naṭarāja**, installed with a plaque quoting **Fritjof Capra**: the cosmic dance "unifies ancient mythology, religious art and modern physics" (Ch 18 §4.2). It is the most visible instance of this failure mode in the world — an analogy imposed from outside and then **physically installed at a particle-physics laboratory**, which lends it an institutional authority the textual evidence does not support. And it differs from §§2.3–2.5 in an important way: **there is no shared vocabulary here at all.** No pun, no borrowed word, no technical term in common. The bridge is built entirely from a *visual* resemblance — a dance of creation and destruction, a shower of particle tracks — and from the appeal of the pairing.

Which tells you the failure mode does not need a linguistic hook. It just needs something that *feels* like a match and a reason to want one.

## 3. The duality that isn't a duality {#complementarity}

*→ concept file: [quantum-complementarity.md](../../concepts/quantum-complementarity.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium***

### 3.1 What complementarity actually says

**Quantum complementarity** (Bohr, 1927) is the principle that quantum systems have **pairs of properties — wave-like and particle-like behaviour; position and momentum; energy and time — that are mutually exclusive in any given experimental arrangement.** In a single experiment, only one of a complementary pair can be fully manifest; the other is correspondingly undefined or blurred.

Three features to fix, because each of them independently breaks the comparison in §3.3:

**It is not about measurement clumsiness.** The mutual exclusivity is a **fundamental feature of quantum mechanics**, not a limitation of technique that better instruments would remove.

**It is quantitative and derived.** Robertson (1929) derives it from commutation relations — ΔAΔB ≥ ½|⟨[Â,B̂]⟩| — giving, for position and momentum, **Δx·Δp ≥ ℏ/2**. It is a **theorem of quantum mechanics**, not a philosophical stance adopted alongside it. Wootters and Zurek (1979) formalised the wave–particle trade-off as an inequality of the same kind.

**It is apparatus-dependent, not standpoint-dependent.** Which behaviour manifests depends on the **experimental arrangement** — a double slit with or without which-path detection. It has, in the concept file's flat phrasing, **"nothing to do with subjective perspective, standpoint epistemology, or many-valued logic."**

### 3.2 The analogy, and who actually made it

The comparison to Jain *anekāntavāda* was not invented by careless popularisers. **D. S. Kothari** — a physicist, in a 1961 lecture, "Complementarity Principle and Eastern Philosophy" — drew it explicitly, and drew it **as an analogy offered for cross-cultural dialogue, not as a claim of equivalence.**

That provenance matters for how this chapter treats it. The original move was legitimate: two developed frameworks, both insisting that a single fixed description is inadequate to their subject-matter, are worth setting side by side. **The conflation happened downstream, when later popular writers upgraded the analogy to an identity** — "the ancient Jains already knew quantum mechanics."

So the corpus's handling is the sanctioned two-edge pattern (charter §5): `structurally-parallel-to` **and** `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` on the same pair. The parallel is real; the identity is false; the map has to carry both or it teaches half the truth.

### 3.3 Where it breaks — and it breaks in the opposite direction

| feature | **quantum complementarity** | **anekāntavāda** (Ch 02) |
|---|---|---|
| **domain** | physical measurement of quantum systems | metaphysical attributes of any substance |
| **exclusivity** | **hard** — position and momentum *cannot* both have sharp values | **soft** — attributes **coexist in the object**; *naya* (standpoint) brings one out for description |
| **ontology** | the wave function is **not** "both wave and particle at once"; it is a state that *responds differently* to different setups | **all valid predications are simultaneously true** of the object |
| **mechanism** | commutation relations; measurement; collapse | multiple-aspect realism; *syāt* hedging marks an **epistemic** limit |
| **quantitative?** | **yes** — Δx·Δp ≥ ℏ/2 | **no** — not a mathematical inequality |
| **predictions?** | **yes** — specific probabilistic predictions about outcomes | **none** — it makes no physical predictions |
| **role of the observer** | the **apparatus** determines which property manifests | the *naya* selects an aspect for **linguistic description**; all aspects remain in the substance |

Now the crux, and it deserves to be stated as sharply as the sources allow:

> **Quantum complementarity says: you *cannot* have sharp values of both observables at once.**
> **Anekāntavāda says: the object *does have* both attributes — permanence and change, unity and plurality — simultaneously.**

These are **structurally opposite ontological claims.** One is a doctrine of *exclusion*: the properties cannot cohabit. The other is a doctrine of *coexistence*: the properties do cohabit, and our descriptions are what have to take turns.

This is the most important sentence in the chapter, because it shows how a conflation can be not merely imprecise but **inverted**. The wave–particle duality *resolves* into the quantum state. The *sat*/*asat* duality of a *dravya* **does not resolve** — both are equally real at all times, which is the entire content of the doctrine.

### 3.4 The one valid bridge

What *is* shared: **both frameworks recognise that a single, fixed, classical description is inadequate to their objects**, and both therefore call for perspectival or contextual description.

That is a genuine structural parallel and it is the **only** valid bridge. It earns `structurally-parallel-to`. It does not earn more, and notice how thin it is once stated precisely: "a single description won't do" is a constraint satisfied by an enormous number of unrelated theories. Shared *inadequacy of the simple case* is weak evidence of anything, because it is where most sophisticated frameworks end up.

### 3.5 A note on the logic side, which belongs to Chapter 06

Complementarity also carries a NOT-equivalent edge against **many-valued-logic**: *"quantum probabilities are physical amplitudes, not Łukasiewicz truth-values; the non-bivalence of quantum measurement is about physical exclusivity of outcomes, not semantics."*

The whole logic cluster — *catuṣkoṭi*, *saptabhaṅgī*, many-valued, paraconsistent and fuzzy logic — is **Chapter 06's** business and is not redone here. What is worth carrying across is that Ch 06 reached the same conclusion by a different route: shared *non-bivalence* is not shared *doctrine*. Saptabhaṅgī parameterises assertions by standpoint to **avoid** contradiction; dialetheists **accept** contradiction; fuzzy logic grades truth **by degree** while each *bhaṅga* is asserted as fully true (*eva*) from its standpoint. Three ways of not being classical, and no two of them the same way.

## 4. The anatomy of the slide {#anatomy}

Six worked cases are enough to generalise. Here is how an analogy becomes an identity, in five moves. They can occur separately; usually several run together.

**Move 1 — the fossil word.** A modern discipline borrows an ancient term (*átomos* → "atom"), the referent changes completely, and the borrowing is forgotten. Afterwards the ancient claim seems to be *asserted by* the modern term. §2.2.

**Move 2 — the shared negation.** Two frameworks are compared on what they both **deny** — "not simply one description," "beyond ordinary logic," "beyond words." Shared negations are cheap: an enormous range of unrelated positions share them. This is Ch 13 §8's finding (the One, śūnyatā and nirvāṇa are all "ineffable" and no two are the same), Ch 06's finding about non-bivalence, and §3.4's finding about complementarity. **A negative predicate is the weakest possible ground for identification, and the most common one used.**

**Move 3 — dropping the mechanism.** The two claims are compared at the level of their *conclusions* and never at the level of *what produces them*. Compare "reality resists a single description" in Bohr and in Umāsvāti and they match. Compare *commutation relations and wave-function collapse* with *multiple-aspect realism and standpoint-indexed predication*, and nothing matches at all. §3.3.

**Move 4 — the flattering upgrade.** An honest comparison offered by a competent person (Kothari, 1961) is repeated by others without its hedges, and each retelling drops another qualification, because "an interesting analogy" is a less repeatable sentence than "they already knew." The corpus has now watched this happen twice — Kothari on complementarity (§3.2), and Capra on Naṭarāja (§2.6).

**Move 5 — borrowed authority.** The modern side's confidence is transferred to the comparison. Quantum mechanics is extremely well confirmed; therefore a claim *containing* quantum mechanics feels well confirmed. §1.2 is the guard against this, and it is why the `modern-atom` node states in its own text that its `high` attaches to the physics only.

> **The diagnostic question that catches all five:** *if the ancient text is right about this, what would the modern theory have to be wrong about — and vice versa?* If the answer is "nothing, they are compatible," the two are not making the same claim, and the identity was never available. Genuine agreement between theories is **risky**; theories that agree because they never meet are not agreeing.

## 6. Four tests {#tests}

Generalising §§2–3 into something portable. Given a proposed ancient/modern identity, ask:

**Test 1 — What does each one *do* in its own system?** Ch 13 §8 introduced this and it has not failed since. The Jain paramāṇu explains **how a soul gets entangled in matter**; the modern atom explains **spectra and stoichiometry**. Nothing that answers those two questions could be one thing. Ch 24 §8.3 added the companion question — *did the thinker assert anything, or refuse to?* — which separated Damascius from Nāgārjuna.

**Test 2 — Argued or found?** Every ancient atomism in §2.3 is the conclusion of an argument (infinite regress cannot be completed). The modern atom is the residue of experiments. These are different *kinds of claim*, and a claim of the first kind cannot be confirmed or refuted by evidence of the second kind — which is why "but modern physics vindicated the Jains" is not even the right shape of sentence.

**Test 3 — Is it quantitative, and does it predict?** Complementarity gives Δx·Δp ≥ ℏ/2 and makes specific probabilistic predictions about measurement outcomes. Anekāntavāda gives **no inequality and makes no physical predictions**. This is the fastest of the four tests in practice, and Ch 26 §9 applied it to Jain karma with the same result: "karma is matter" predicts nothing and no observation bears on it, so it is an **ontological commitment**, not a physical hypothesis — and classifying it correctly makes it a *better* idea, since as physics it is empty and as ontology it is coherent and unusual.

**Test 4 — Is the resemblance in a *negation*?** If the two frameworks are being matched on what they both deny — not-simply-one-description, not-bivalent, not-expressible — treat the match as **near-worthless** until a positive commonality is produced. §4's Move 2.

Apply all four to the two headline cases and every test fails in the same direction:

| | **paramāṇu ≟ atom** | **anekāntavāda ≟ complementarity** |
|---|---|---|
| Test 1 (what it does) | soul-entanglement vs spectra | describes any substance vs predicts measurement outcomes |
| Test 2 (argued/found) | a priori vs empirical | a priori vs derived-and-tested |
| Test 3 (quantitative) | no inequality vs no inequality either — but the modern side is embedded in a predictive theory | **no** vs **Δx·Δp ≥ ℏ/2** |
| Test 4 (negation-based?) | no — this one runs on a **fossil word** | **yes** — "not one description" |

## 7. The opposite error {#deflation}

A chapter this insistent about inflation owes a word about the mirror failure, because this corpus has hit it too and the charter names it: *"The goal is neither to praise these traditions nor to debunk them — only to know them accurately."*

**Deflation** is the error of letting a modern verdict answer a question it was not asked. Chapter 23 met it directly. A peer-reviewed medical article gave an **uncaveated 600 BCE date** for Āyurvedic texts that the philological literature does not support — a *peer-reviewed* source, wrong on a claim outside its domain of scrutiny. And more generally, Ch 23 had to record a **negative modern clinical assessment** of Āyurveda while still describing the tradition, which forced the distinction into the open:

> A modern verdict answers ***"is it true?"***. This corpus asks ***"what does it claim?"***. **The debunking no more settles the doctrine's content than a validation would.**

That formulation is the deflationary counterpart of §4's Move 5. Inflation borrows the modern side's authority to elevate the ancient claim; deflation borrows it to dismiss the ancient claim. **Both mistake the modern theory for the arbiter of what the ancient text meant**, and in both cases the ancient text's content is settled by something that was never about it.

Two further symmetries worth noting.

**Both errors make the material less interesting.** "The Jains discovered atoms" makes Jain physics a rough draft of chemistry. "Jain physics is prescientific nonsense" makes it not worth reading. The accurate description — a graded matter-ontology in which the finest grade bonds to souls and carries intrinsic colour — is stranger and more informative than either.

**And §0 cuts both ways.** The prime directive is usually read as a guard against enthusiasts. It is equally a guard against the reader who arrives already certain there is nothing here. The instruction *when in doubt, assert less* restrains both.

## 8. Why this matters {#why}

**Because refusing the comparison is not the same as making it carefully.** A corpus that never mentioned modern physics would be silent on the one thing most of its readers arrive believing. Chapters 01 and 02 could describe *paramāṇu* and *anekāntavāda* perfectly and still leave a reader more confident in the conflation than before, because a careful ancient description with no boundary marked reads as *confirmation*. The non-equivalence edges are not decoration on the graph. They are the part that does the correcting.

**Because the inverted case (§3.3) shows how bad the failure can get.** "Anekāntavāda is quantum complementarity" is not a loose approximation of a true statement. Complementarity says the properties **cannot** cohabit; anekāntavāda says they **do**. Someone who accepts the identification has adopted the exact negation of the Jain doctrine while believing they have confirmed it — which is the strongest possible argument for the charter's rule that analogy must never be quietly upgraded.

**Because the honest version is more interesting, every time.** This has been the finding in every chapter that ran the test. Ch 13 gave up "East meets West at the summit" and got two precisely-located metaphysics that genuinely disagree about the top of reality. Ch 24 gave up "the Greek Mīmāṃsā" and got three different locations for ritual causation. Here, giving up "the Jains discovered the atom" gets you a system in which matter carries intrinsic colour because the soul must be able to take on its complexion — a doctrine with no modern counterpart at all, which is precisely why it is worth knowing.

**And because §1.2's distinction is the chapter's most portable idea.** Confidence attaches to *claims*, not to *files*. A node can be highly confident about the modern physics it describes and carry no authority whatever for the comparison in the next paragraph. Any project that mixes settled and interpretive material needs that firewall, and this chapter is where the corpus states it.

## 9. Flags {#flags}

| flag | why the resemblance is tempting | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **paramāṇu ≠ modern atom** | both are "the smallest unit of matter"; both combine into larger structures | **partless vs structured**, **a priori vs empirical**, **eternal vs destructible**, and the modern atom is **not** the smallest unit of matter at all |
| **and specifically: intrinsic qualities** | it sounds like an incidental ancient quirk | the Jain paramāṇu's **intrinsic colour/taste/smell is load-bearing for *leśyā*** (Ch 26 §5); equating the two deletes a doctrine |
| **"atom" ≠ *atomos*** | the modern discipline uses the ancient word | **Dalton borrowed a name in 1803**, not a doctrine; the referent changed and the name stayed. The inference is a **pun nobody hears as a pun** |
| **the *clinamen* ≠ quantum indeterminacy** | both are genuine smallest-scale indeterminacy inside an otherwise deterministic system — a real structural echo | the swerve is **posited because the system requires it** (free will; collisions); quantum indeterminacy **falls out of the formalism** whether or not anyone wants it |
| **Naṭarāja ≠ particle physics** | the "cosmic dance" of creation and destruction; a statue at CERN with a *Tao of Physics* plaque | a **Śaiva Siddhānta soteriological icon** — the *pañcakṛtya*, the freeing of souls from māyā. And note: **no shared vocabulary at all** — the bridge is pure imagery |
| **anekāntavāda ≠ quantum complementarity** | both deny that one fixed description is adequate | **inverted**: complementarity says the properties **cannot** cohabit; anekāntavāda says they **do**, simultaneously, and only our *descriptions* take turns |
| **complementarity ≠ measurement clumsiness** | "you disturb what you measure" is the usual gloss | it is a **theorem** — Robertson 1929 from commutation relations — not a technical limitation better instruments would remove |
| **complementarity ≠ standpoint epistemology** | both are "perspective-dependent" | it is **apparatus**-dependent: which-path detection or not. It has "nothing to do with subjective perspective" |
| **the Kothari analogy ≠ the popular claim** | the popular claim cites the analogy's author | **Kothari (1961) offered it explicitly as analogy** for cross-cultural dialogue; the identity is a downstream upgrade with the hedges stripped |
| **quantum probabilities ≠ many-valued truth-values** | both are non-bivalent | **physical amplitudes**, not Łukasiewicz truth-values; physical exclusivity of outcomes, not semantics |
| **a shared *negation* ≠ a shared doctrine** | "neither is simply true-or-false / one-description / expressible" | negative predicates are cheap and are satisfied by unrelated frameworks — see also Ch 06 and Ch 13 §8 |
| **high confidence on physics ≠ high confidence on the comparison** | the node is marked `high` and the comparison is in the same file | the `high` **attaches to the physics only**, by the node's own statement. Borrowed authority is §4's Move 5 |
| **"modern physics vindicated them" ≠ a well-formed sentence** | it sounds like the strongest available praise | an **a priori** claim cannot be confirmed by **empirical** evidence; the two are not the same kind of claim (Test 2) |
| **debunking ≠ description** | a negative modern verdict is evidence about something | it answers *"is it true?"*; this corpus asks *"what does it claim?"* — **the debunking no more settles the content than a validation would** (Ch 23) |
| **a comparandum node ≠ an endorsement** | why include modern physics at all in a corpus about ancient texts? | the edges exist **to be drawn and flagged** — the charter forbids silently omitting a false equivalence; the map must **teach** the distinction |
