## 1. The problem Parmenides sets: what can truly *be*? {#parmenides}

*→ concept file: [parmenides-being.md](../../concepts/parmenides-being.md)*

**Parmenides of Elea** (c. 515–450 BCE) wrote a single hexameter poem, *On Nature*, cast as a goddess's revelation and divided into the **Way of Truth** and the **Way of Opinion**. From one premise — **"it is and cannot not be"** — and the claim that not-being cannot even be thought, he deduces the "signs" of **what-is (*to eon*)**: ungenerated and imperishable, whole and uniform, unchanging and motionless, complete — a perfect sphere. The consequence is severe: **change, motion, and plurality are illusory**, mere deceptive content of mortal opinion.

What Parmenides *meant* by this is itself contested — strict numerical monism, a logical exposure of incoherence in earlier cosmology, a meta-principle about what any fundamental entity must satisfy, or a modal claim about necessary vs. contingent being. What is not contested is the effect: every major thinker who follows him in this chapter is answering him. Democritus's atoms are built to satisfy the Parmenidean requirements on "what is" (ungenerated, imperishable, internally changeless) while still permitting plurality and motion. Plato's eternal, changeless Forms descend in part from the same contrast between true unchanging being and deceptive appearance. Aristotle will spend an entire treatise (the *Physics*) rehabilitating change as intelligible rather than illusory.

**A first, deliberately sharp cross-tradition marker:** Parmenidean Being is often set beside **brahman** — both are a single, ungenerated, changeless reality behind a world of apparent flux, the closest Greek analogue to the Advaita One. But Brahman is *consciousness* (*sat-cit-ānanda*) and identical with the self (*ātman*), known by spiritual realisation; Parmenides' *to eon* is the terminus of a *logical* argument (not-being is unthinkable), with no consciousness-claim and no soteriology attached — a deduced object, not a realised Self. A second marker cuts the other way: Parmenidean Being *excludes* change as not-being, whereas Jain **sat** is defined by *utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya* — origination, cessation, *and* persistence together. One tradition makes permanence total; the other makes permanence-in-change the very nature of the real. Two "theories of being," opposite verdicts.

## 2. Democritus: atoms and void {#democritus}

*→ concept file: [democritus-atom.md](../../concepts/democritus-atom.md)*

**Leucippus and Democritus** (5th c. BCE) answer Parmenides by admitting exactly the two things his logic seemed to forbid: plurality and "what is not." The universe, for them, is **atoms** (*atomos*, "uncuttable") and **void** (*kenon*) — nothing else. Atoms are solid, indivisible, eternal, uncreated; they are also **qualitatively neutral**: no intrinsic colour, taste, smell, or heat, only **shape**, **arrangement**, and **position**. Every sensory quality we experience is the *product* of atomic combination, not a property of the atoms themselves. The argument for indivisibility is a-priori: division cannot regress infinitely, so there must be an ultimate, uncuttable unit — the same infinite-regress argument Jain and Vaiśeṣika atomists independently use for *paramāṇu*.

That shared argument-structure makes the three-way comparison worth setting out in full, reused here from the concept file's own table:

| dimension | Greek (Democritus) | Jain paramāṇu | Vaiśeṣika paramāṇu |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intrinsic sensory qualities? | **No** — qualitatively neutral, shape/size only | **Yes** — touch+taste+smell+colour, one each | **Yes** — tiered by element (earth=4, water=3, fire=2, air=1) |
| How many kinds? | one kind | one kind | four kinds |
| Combination mechanism | mechanical collision in the void | *snigdha/rūkṣa* affinity | *adṛṣṭa* (unseen force) + later Nyāya's Īśvara |
| Void/space? | **Yes** — essential | **No** — *ākāśa* gives location, no void; the cosmos is full | **Yes** — *ākāśa* as medium |
| Soteriological role? | none — pure materialist physics | yes — karma is itself the finest matter | limited — tied to ritual/liberation via *adṛṣṭa* |
| A-priori infinite-regress argument? | yes | yes — same structure | yes — same structure |

**The qualitative-neutrality of Greek atoms is the decisive difference from both Indian theories** — Democritus explains apparent qualities by combination of neutral units, a move closer in spirit to modern physics; Jain and Vaiśeṣika atoms carry their qualities intrinsically, a realist rather than a reductive position. The scholarly consensus, per the concept file, is that Greek and Indian atomism arose **independently** — the popular story that Democritus learned atomism from Indian sages on his travels is not well-attested in reliable ancient sources, and the structural similarity likely reflects parallel reasoning (the same infinite-regress problem, tackled twice) rather than transmission. A further, frequently-drawn but wrong equivalence: Democritus's "atoms and void" is not a Greek anticipation of Nāgārjuna's **śūnyatā** ("emptiness") — Democritean void is a positive spatial container in which atoms move; śūnyatā is the *absence of svabhāva* (own-nature), not a place. Opposite ontological directions dressed in superficially similar language.

## 3. Plato I: the Theory of Forms {#plato-forms}

*→ concept file: [plato-forms.md](../../concepts/plato-forms.md)*

Where Democritus answers Parmenides by admitting a plural, changing physical world alongside eternal atoms, **Plato** answers him by relocating "true being" to a wholly non-physical realm. The **Forms** (*eidos* / *idea*) — Beauty itself, Justice itself, above all the **Form of the Good** — are eternal, unchanging, aspatial, atemporal archetypes; physical particulars are imperfect, temporary copies that merely **participate in** (*methexis*) or **imitate** (*mimesis*) them. Sense-perception yields only opinion (*doxa*) about the changing particulars; reason (*nous*) alone grasps the eternal Forms, which the soul is said to have known before birth and now merely **recollects** (*anamnēsis*) — the epistemology of the *Phaedo*, the *Republic*'s Cave, and the *Timaeus*'s craftsman-God who fashions the world *using* the Forms as a pattern.

This is the most extreme "realism about universals" in the Western tradition: universals exist **separately from and prior to** every particular that shares in them. Aristotle's most famous objection — the **Third Man Argument** — is that if the Form of Man explains what many men share, and the Form is itself man-like, a further Form is needed to explain what the Form and the particulars share, and so on without end. His fix, worked out in §6 below, is to make form **immanent** — *in* things, not separate from them.

The cross-tradition table, carried over from the concept file, is worth holding onto for the rest of the chapter, since it recurs whenever "universals" come up again (Aristotle's Categories in §7, and the Vaiśeṣika comparanda that Ch 16 will cover in full):

| tradition | position on universals |
|---|---|
| **Platonic** | transcendent — Forms exist separately, independently, eternally |
| **Aristotelian** | immanent — form exists only *in* particulars; no separate Form of Man |
| **Advaita Vedānta** | Brahman alone is real; particulars are *māyā* — closest Indian parallel (Forms ≈ Brahman as true reality behind appearance), but Brahman is **one**, Forms are **many** |
| **Jain** | *sāmānya* (universal) is real but immanent, dependent on individual *dravyas* — closer to Aristotle than to Plato |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | no universals at all — only momentary particulars; *apoha* explains general terms without positing any universal — the strongest rejection of all |
| **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** | *sāmānya* is real and immanent via *samavāya* (inseparable inherence); *viśeṣa* (ultimate particular) is its own distinct category |

**The Plato–Brahman parallel deserves the same caution given to Parmenides in §1, and for the same reason.** Brahman is one; Forms are many. Brahman is the world's very *substance*; Forms are archetypes standing outside the world they pattern. And for Advaita, the individual *ātman* ultimately *is* Brahman — there is no Platonic equivalent of a particular soul merging with the Form of Soul. The structural analogy (true reality behind mere appearance) is genuine; the identity claim is not — the same verdict Ch 13 reaches, at much greater length, for Plotinus's One.

## 4. Plato II: the tripartite soul {#plato-soul}

*→ concept file: [plato-soul.md](../../concepts/plato-soul.md)*

Plato's *Republic* (Book IV, 435c–441c) gives the soul (*psychē*) three parts, argued for from the **principle of opposites**: the same part of a thing cannot, at the same time, desire and refuse the same object; since we sometimes want a drink while some part of us refuses it, there must be separate parts in conflict.

| part | Greek | function | city-class analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reason | *logistikon* | thinks, loves truth, governs | rulers (*philosophoi*) |
| Spirit | *thymoeidēs* | anger, pride, honour; sides with reason against appetite | guardians (*phylakes*) |
| Appetite | *epithymētikon* | food, sex, material desire | producers |

The *Phaedrus*'s **chariot allegory** (246a–249c) recasts the same structure as a charioteer (reason) driving two winged horses, one noble (spirit) and one unruly (appetite); the soul's capacity to ascend toward the Forms depends on the charioteer governing both well. **Justice**, in the *Republic*, is simply this internal hierarchy working properly — reason ruling, spirit supporting, appetite complying — mirrored exactly by the city's three classes. The soul is also held to be **immortal**, undergoing post-mortem judgment and reincarnation (*Timaeus*, *Laws*) as an educational, purifying process.

The cross-tradition comparison is instructive precisely because the closest Indian analogue turns out *not* to be the one that looks most similar on the surface:

| tradition | soul structure | agent/passive | liberation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plato | tripartite (reason/spirit/appetite); fundamentally reason | reason is genuinely *agent*; body a hindrance | return to the Forms via philosophy |
| Jain *jīva* | single conscious entity; *upayoga* its mark | active agent (*kartā*/*bhoktā*) | karma-free existence at the *loka*'s summit |
| Buddhist *khandhas* | five aggregates; no permanent soul | no permanent agent | *nirvāṇa* (cessation) |
| Sāṃkhya *puruṣa* | pure witness-consciousness, no parts | passive witness, **not** an agent | discrimination from *prakṛti* |

Plato's soul is *structurally* closest to the **Jain jīva** — both are individual, immortal, body-entangled agents responsible for their own liberation or ascent. But the Platonic soul has genuine **internal** division (reason can be dominated by its own appetite); the Jain jīva is uniform in nature, and its corruption is *external* (karma-matter adhering to it), never an internal split between rival parts of the self. There is no Jain equivalent of the tripartite soul as such.

## 5. Aristotle I: substance and hylomorphism {#aristotle-substance}

*→ concept file: [aristotle-substance.md](../../concepts/aristotle-substance.md)*

**Aristotle**'s account of *ousia* ("substance," "being," "essence") is the central category of his metaphysics — what exists independently and is the primary bearer of all other properties. The account shifts across two texts. In the *Categories*, individual particulars (this horse, this Socrates) are the **primary substances**; species and genera ("horse," "animal") are **secondary substances**, real but dependent, "said of" the particulars. In the *Metaphysics*, the deepest sense of *ousia* becomes the **substantial form** — the organising principle that makes a thing the kind of thing it is — with matter demoted to bare potentiality. Whether this is a real revision or a deepening of one insight is, per the concept file, a genuinely contested question in Aristotle scholarship; §7 returns to it directly.

Underlying both accounts is **hylomorphism**: every physical thing is a compound (*synolon*) of **matter** (*hylē* — indeterminate stuff, pure potentiality) and **form** (*morphē*/*eidos* — the principle that gives matter its determinate structure and function). Neither is ever encountered alone; formless matter and matterless form are conceptual limits, not real things. This is Aristotle's explicit, considered rejection of Plato's separately existing Forms (§3): for Aristotle, "horse-ness" exists only *in* particular horses, never floating free of them.

| feature | Aristotle (*ousia*) | Jain (*dravya*) | Vaiśeṣika (*dravya*) |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary substances | individual particulars | all six *dravyas* equally real; no primary/secondary split | nine substance-types (earth, water, fire, air, ether, time, space, soul, mind) |
| matter/form structure | hylomorphic compound | no form/matter duality — *guṇas* and *paryāyas*, not composed of matter+form | qualities (*guṇa*) inhere via *samavāya*; no hylomorphism |
| persistence through change | substance persists through *accidental* change; *substantial* change requires form-replacement | *dravya* persists as permanent substrate; modes (*paryāya*) change — the closer parallel | composite *dravyas* do **not** persist through substantial change, unlike Aristotle |
| non-physical substances | soul debated as form-of-body, not a separate substance | *jīva*, *dharma*, *adharma*, *ākāśa*, *kāla* — several non-physical *dravyas*, no Aristotelian parallel | soul and mind are substances; more overlap with Aristotle here |
| distinctive category | substantial form | none — intrinsic *guṇa-paryāya* unity, no "inherence" | *samavāya* (inseparable inherence) — Aristotle has no equivalent |

The closest Greek-Jain structural parallel in the whole chapter sits here: **ousia** and **dravya** are both the independent substrate-category, both contrasted against quality and change, and Jain *sat* (TS 5.30, *utpādavyayadhrauvyayuktaṃ sat*, "being is that which is endowed with origination, cessation, and persistence") resembles Aristotle's substance-persisting-through-change far more than it resembles Parmenides' changeless Being (§1) or Advaita's changeless Brahman. But one caution travels the other direction: Aristotle's *prime matter* is **not** atomic — he explicitly denies infinite downward divisibility stops at a fixed unit the way Democritus's atoms do (§2). The Greek parallel to Jain/Vaiśeṣika *paramāṇu* is Democritus, not Aristotle's *hylē*.

## 6. Aristotle II: the ten categories {#aristotle-categories}

*→ concept file: [aristotle-categories.md](../../concepts/aristotle-categories.md)*

The *Categories* sets out **the ten highest genera of being** — not categories of thought, but of what there *is*:

| # | Greek | Latin | classifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *ousia* | *substantia* | substance |
| 2 | *poson* | *quantitas* | quantity |
| 3 | *poion* | *qualitas* | quality |
| 4 | *pros ti* | *relatio* | relation |
| 5 | *pou* | *locus* | place |
| 6 | *pote* | *tempus* | time |
| 7 | *keisthai* | *positio* | position/posture |
| 8 | *echein* | *habitus* | having/state |
| 9 | *poiein* | *actio* | acting |
| 10 | *paschein* | *passio* | being acted upon |

Categories 2–10 are **accidents**: they cannot exist independently, only by inhering in a substance. The whole scheme is substance-anchored, and it yields a four-fold classification by "said-of" and "present-in" relations: secondary substances (universals, said-of but not present-in), particular accidents (present-in but not said-of), universal accidents (both), and primary substances (neither) — the ontological bedrock.

The tension flagged in §5 belongs here in its sharpest form: the *Categories* makes bare **particulars** the primary substances; *Metaphysics* Z argues **form** is substance in the most fundamental sense. This is "one of the most debated questions in Aristotle scholarship" (SEP), and this chapter — like the concept file — states it as open rather than resolving it.

The comparanda repeat and sharpen §3's universals table: Jain *guṇa*/*paryāya* is structurally close to Aristotelian quality-accidents and relational-accidents (permanent attributes vs. transient modes), but Jain *dravya* has **no** primary/secondary substance split — every *dravya* is equally substantial. The Vaiśeṣika *padārtha* scheme is the more exact structural match: also roughly ten categories, also anchored by substance first — a case of **independent convergence** on substance-centred categorial ontology (full treatment of the Vaiśeṣika side awaits Ch 16). And the *Categories*' own secondary substances (universals *immanent in* particulars) is Aristotle's direct, considered rejection of Plato's *transcendent* Forms (§3) — the same Greek-internal quarrel restated in the vocabulary of predication rather than participation.

## 7. Aristotle III: the four causes {#four-causes}

*→ concept file: [four-causes.md](../../concepts/four-causes.md)*

**Aitia** — Aristotle's four kinds of explanation (*Physics* II.3; *Metaphysics* V.2) — is broader than the modern word "cause"; the better gloss is "explanatory factor," whatever answers the question "why?" about a natural thing or change:

| cause | Greek | question | bronze-statue example |
|---|---|---|---|
| material | *hylē* | what is it made of? | the bronze |
| formal | *morphē*/*eidos* | what is its form/essence? | the statue's shape |
| efficient | — | what initiated the change? | the sculptor's art |
| final | *telos* | for what end? | the finished statue; honouring the subject |

A complete explanation invokes all four; an account that mentions only material causes — Democritus's atomism (§2) is Aristotle's implicit target — says only *of what* something is made, never *what it is, by what, or for what*. The **final cause** is the distinctive and most controversial commitment: Aristotle grounds biological explanation teleologically (sharp teeth exist *for* biting) without requiring a conscious intender — the function is simply part of what the thing *is*. This marks the sharpest line in the whole chapter between Aristotle and every Indian causal theory surveyed elsewhere in this corpus: no Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Buddhist, or Jain account of causation carries an Aristotelian *telos*. Buddhist **pratītyasamutpāda** (dependent origination) is the closest formal parallel — both explicitly reject single-cause reduction, analysing causation as genuinely multi-factor — but *pratītyasamutpāda* is purposeless: the twelve *nidānas* are conditions, not explanatory *kinds*, and there is no cosmic goal built into the chain the way *telos* is built into Aristotle's dental example.

## 8. Aristotle IV: syllogistic logic {#aristotle-logic}

*→ concept file: [aristotle-logic.md](../../concepts/aristotle-logic.md)*

The *Prior* and *Posterior Analytics* give the West its first systematic formal logic. A *sullogismos* (*Prior Analytics* I.2, 24b18–20) is "speech in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so" — three terms (major, minor, middle), two premises, one necessary conclusion, arranged in three **figures** by the middle term's position. Aristotle separates **apodeictic** (demonstrative, premises "true and primary," yielding scientific knowledge) from **dialectical** syllogisms (premises merely *endoxa*, accepted opinion, used for argument) — a distinction Indian logic marks too, as *svārthānumāna* (inference for oneself) vs. *parārthānumāna* (inference for another), though the concept file treats that parallel only in passing since Nyāya epistemology has its own full chapter (Ch 05).

| feature | Aristotle | Nyāya *anumāna* | Buddhist *trairūpya* (Dignāga) |
|---|---|---|---|
| inferential core | 3 terms (major/middle/minor) | 3 terms (*sādhya*/*hetu*/*pakṣa*) | 3 conditions on the *hetu* |
| universals | assumed, stipulated in the major premise | *vyāpti*, empirically grounded in positive **and** negative instances | rejected outright — only particulars; *apoha* replaces universals |
| character | purely formal/deductive | empirico-deductive | formal + causal |

**The deepest structural difference**, per the concept file: Aristotle's major premise ("All men are mortal") is simply *asserted* as already universal — the formal system is silent on how it was established. Nyāya's *vyāpti* must be *earned*, confirmed across positive instances (*sapakṣa*) and checked against negative ones (*vipakṣa*) before it licenses an inference. Aristotle's logic is syntactic-formal; Nyāya's is epistemically grounded in a certified pervasion-relation. Two systems that both organise inference around a three-term middle-mediated structure, arriving at universality by stipulation in one case and by verification in the other.

## 9. Aristotle V: ethics and the good life {#aristotle-ethics}

*→ concept file: [aristotle-ethics.md](../../concepts/aristotle-ethics.md)*

The *Nicomachean Ethics* names **eudaimonia** (flourishing) the highest good — desirable in itself, never as a means (EN I.1, 1094a14-16). The **function argument** (EN I.7, 1097b24–1098a4) reasons from Aristotle's own metaphysics (§5–§8): every kind of thing has a characteristic function; the human function is rational activity; the good human life is therefore activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, over a complete life. **Virtue** (*aretē*) comes in two kinds — intellectual virtues, perfected by teaching, and character virtues (courage, justice, temperance), acquired by habituation — governed by the **doctrine of the mean** (*mesotēs*, EN II.6, 1106a26-b28): each character virtue is the perception-guided intermediate between excess and deficiency, not an arithmetic average.

Eudaimonia is emphatically **this-worldly and political**: achieved through virtuous activity within the *polis*, never through withdrawal or release from embodied existence. Book X's *theōria* (philosophical contemplation) is the highest activity, but it presupposes a virtuous community as its foundation, not an escape from one. This gives the chapter its sharpest cross-tradition diagnostic: **Indian liberation-ethics aims *out of* the world** (Jain *tri-ratna*, the Buddhist Noble Path, both moving *saṃsāra → mokṣa/nirvāṇa*); **Aristotelian ethics aims *into* the world** (*bios → eudaimonia*). The direction is reversed, not merely the vocabulary. Virtue is genuinely comparable — Jain *cāritra* (conduct) is likewise constitutive of, not merely instrumental to, the highest goal, and Jain *tapas* (austerity) shares with Aristotelian habituation the idea that disciplined practice reshapes character — but the destination each virtue serves points opposite ways.

## 10. From metaphysics to therapy: the Hellenistic turn {#hellenistic-turn}

After Aristotle, and after Alexander dissolved the *polis* that Aristotelian ethics presupposed, Greek philosophy's centre of gravity shifts. The great systems that follow — Epicureanism, Stoicism, Cynicism, and the two Skepticisms — keep doing metaphysics, but metaphysics is now explicitly in service of a **therapeutic** goal: securing a settled, undisturbed state of mind (however each school defines it) against a world that no longer offers the political stability Aristotle assumed. Every school below names its target state explicitly — *ataraxia*, *apatheia*, *autarkeia* — and each proposes a different route to it. Read together, §11–§14 tell one connected story of four rival answers to a single question: *given that turmoil is real, how does a person become tranquil?*

## 11. Epicureanism: atoms, the swerve, and tranquility {#epicureanism}

*→ concept files: [epicurus-atom.md](../../concepts/epicurus-atom.md) · [epicurus-ethics.md](../../concepts/epicurus-ethics.md)*

**Epicurus** (341–270 BCE) inherits Democritus's atoms-and-void wholesale (§2), then adds two things Democritus lacked: **weight** (atoms have a natural "downward" motion through the bottomless void, answering Aristotle's objection that pure inertia-plus-collision never explains why atoms move *at all*) and the **swerve** (*clinamen*/*parenklisis*) — an uncaused, minute, random veering at no determinate time or place. The swerve does double duty: cosmologically, it reintroduces the collisions that parallel-falling atoms would never produce; ethically, it "breaks the bonds of fate," making room for human freedom against strict determinism. Notably, the swerve is **not attested in Epicurus's own surviving writings** — it survives only via Lucretius and Cicero, which is why the concept file (and this chapter) keeps this doctrine at medium rather than higher confidence.

The physics grounds the ethics directly. Epicurean ethics makes **pleasure** (*hēdonē*) the highest good — but redefines it away from indulgence: the highest pleasure is **katastematic** (the settled *state* of a want already met — **ataraxia**, freedom from mental disturbance, and **aponia**, absence of bodily pain), not **kinetic** pleasure (the process of satisfying a want). This yields a surprising asceticism-of-desire: a three-way classification of desires (natural-and-necessary, to satisfy; natural-but-unnecessary, luxuries that breed dependency; vain/empty — wealth, power, fame — to eliminate outright) counsels *reducing* wants rather than multiplying goods. The **Tetrapharmakos** ("four-part cure") states the therapy plainly: don't fear the gods (they exist but are indifferent, dwelling in the *intermundia*); don't fear death ("death is nothing to us, since while we exist our death is not, and when death occurs we do not exist" — the soul, being atomic, simply disperses); the good is easy to get; the bad is easy to endure.

Two comparative pairs are worth holding side by side. Against **Cārvāka**, the Indian materialist school (Ch 17): both are this-worldly, pleasure-oriented, afterlife-denying materialisms, but Epicurus *reasons inferentially* to imperceptible atoms and void exactly as Democritus does, while Cārvāka rejects inference itself and admits only direct perception — the two split precisely on whether the unperceived can be known at all. Against **Buddhist nirvāṇa** (Ch 12): both diagnose suffering as rooted in craving and false belief, and both prescribe a structured therapy (the Tetrapharmakos loosely parallels the four-part diagnose-and-cure shape of the Four Noble Truths) ending in an undisturbed state — but Epicurean *ataraxia* is tranquility *within one finite mortal life* (no rebirth, because there is no persisting soul to be reborn), while *nirvāṇa* is release *from* the cycle of rebirth itself, grounded in *anattā* and dependent origination. No-afterlife-to-fear is not the same claim as no-more-wheel-to-turn.

## 12. Stoicism: logos, fate, and virtue {#stoicism}

*→ concept files: [stoicism.md](../../concepts/stoicism.md) · [stoic-logos.md](../../concepts/stoic-logos.md)*

**Zeno of Citium** (c. 334–262 BCE) founded Stoicism around 300 BCE at the Stoa Poikile in Athens, after studying under the Cynic Crates (§13); **Chrysippus** systematised it so thoroughly that "but for Chrysippus, there had been no Porch." The school divides into logic, physics, and ethics. Its physics is a materialist pantheism: an **active principle** — *logos*/God/Zeus/***pneuma*** (a "designing fire," corporeal and rational) — pervades a **passive principle**, matter, making the cosmos one rational living being in which everything unfolds by **fate** (*heimarmenē*), an unbroken, providentially-ordered chain of causes. The world periodically dissolves into fire and is reborn (*ekpyrōsis*, eternal recurrence).

The *logos* is worth dwelling on because it is the single term in this chapter most tempting to collapse into an Indian absolute — and the concept file resists the collapse point by point:

| feature | *logos* (Stoic) | brahman (Advaita) | *dharma-dravya* (Jain) | *pratītyasamutpāda* (Buddhist) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| material or immaterial | **material** — corporeal fiery *pneuma* | immaterial — pure consciousness | material — one of six *dravyas* | neither — a process, not a substance |
| active or passive | **active** — moves, organises, creates | *nirguṇa* passive / *saguṇa* active | **passive** medium (enables motion, does not cause it) | neither — conditioned arising, no agent |
| rational? | explicitly rational | beyond conceptualisation | non-rational, a physical medium | impersonal causal law, not "rational" |
| personal? | personal (Zeus/God) | impersonal (*nirguṇa*) / personal (*saguṇa*) | impersonal | impersonal |

The most common surface conflation, *logos* ≈ brahman, breaks exactly where the Parmenides–Brahman and Forms–Brahman parallels broke in §1 and §3: *logos* is **material** and **rational**; *nirguṇa* Brahman is **immaterial** and *beyond* rationality. The analogy holds only at "single cosmic principle pervading everything" — it fails at materiality, at rationality, and at the personal/impersonal question.

Stoic ethics makes **virtue the only good** and **sufficient**, by itself, for *eudaimonia* — the *telos* is "living in agreement with nature" (*homologoumenōs tēi physei*), harmonising one's own reason with the cosmic *logos*. Everything else — health, wealth, reputation, even survival under torture — is an **indifferent** (*adiaphora*), though some are "preferred." The **passions** (*pathē*) are literally false value-judgments, to be extirpated into **apatheia** (freedom from destructive emotion), while healthy rational affects (caution, wishing, joy) remain. Because all humans share in reason, Stoic ethics is explicitly **cosmopolitan**. Against Aristotle (§9): both are eudaimonist virtue-ethics organised around *aretē* and a single *telos*, but for Aristotle external goods are also *needed* for full flourishing, while for the Stoics virtue alone suffices and everything external is, strictly, indifferent — virtue-sufficiency against virtue-plus-externals. Against Epicurus (§11): both aim at a tranquil state, but by opposite routes — Stoic *apatheia* through virtue and **engagement** with the world and one's social duty; Epicurean *ataraxia* through **withdrawal** (*lathe biōsas*, "live unnoticed") — a contrast the Stoics themselves (via Epictetus) drew explicitly.

## 13. Cynicism: the short path {#cynicism}

*→ concept file: [cynicism.md](../../concepts/cynicism.md)*

**Cynicism** descends from Socrates through **Antisthenes** (c. 445–365 BCE), is made famous by **Diogenes of Sinope** (c. 412–323 BCE), and passes through **Crates of Thebes** — who directly taught Zeno of Citium (§12), making Cynicism Stoicism's literal ancestor; the Stoics later called it "the short path to virtue." No founding treatise survives; the school lives entirely in anecdote (transmitted by Diogenes Laërtius).

The Cynic demand is stark: **virtue (*aretē*) is the only good**, achieved by living "according to nature" (*kata physin*) in deliberate, public defiance of **convention** (*nomos*) — wealth, status, comfort, all rejected as worthless and enslaving. The practical vocabulary: **autarkeia** (radical self-sufficiency — Diogenes' single cloak and barrel, so nothing external can be taken from you), **askēsis** (rigorous training in hardship), **anaidea** ("shamelessness" — doing publicly what convention deems private, to expose convention's arbitrariness), **parrhēsia** (fearless truth-telling, even to the powerful), and **kosmopolitēs** — Diogenes called himself "a citizen of the world."

Stoicism is Cynicism's direct heir but a **softened** one: the Stoics retain virtue-as-the-only-good but keep "preferred indifferents" and civic engagement, where Cynicism rejects convention and possession outright. India offers two comparanda, both marked the same way: Diogenes' propertyless self-sufficiency parallels Jain **aparigraha** (non-possession) — both minimise external dependence to secure inner freedom — but Cynic renunciation targets *autarkeia* within one mortal life, secular self-mastery, while Jain *aparigraha* targets the stopping of karmic influx (*āsrava*) toward *mokṣa* across rebirths. Likewise Cynic *askēsis* and Jain **tapas** are both radical hardship-programmes aimed at liberation from a perceived bondage, but one is secular freedom-now, the other karma-shedding across lifetimes.

## 14. The Skeptics: Pyrrhonism and the Academy {#skeptics}

*→ concept files: [pyrrhonism.md](../../concepts/pyrrhonism.md) · [academic-skepticism.md](../../concepts/academic-skepticism.md)*

Two rival Hellenistic skepticisms, historical contemporaries, both use *epochē* (suspension of judgment) but for different scope and reasons.

**Pyrrhonism**, named for **Pyrrho of Elis** (c. 365–275 BCE, who wrote nothing and is known through his student Timon), is preserved chiefly in **Sextus Empiricus**'s *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* (2nd–3rd c. CE). Its mechanism is **isostheneia**: for any question, the sceptic sets out opposed arguments of *equal strength*; finding them balanced, one cannot assent to either, so judgment is **suspended**. The suspension is engineered by the **Ten Modes of Aenesidemus** (perceptual and cultural relativity) and the **Five Modes of Agrippa** (dispute, infinite regress, relativity, hypothesis, circularity), which attack the *structure* of justification itself. **Ataraxia** follows, but only as an *unsought* sequel — Sextus's own image is the painter Apelles, who, frustrated at failing to paint a horse's lather, threw his sponge at the panel, and the random smear produced exactly the effect he wanted: tranquility arrives once you stop trying to pin reality down. Crucially, the Pyrrhonist asserts **no thesis**, not even the thesis that nothing can be known — he lives *adoxastōs* ("without belief"), by mere appearances, refusing theoretical commitment about their hidden nature.

**Academic skepticism** is the Platonic Academy's own skeptical period (c. 266–90 BCE), running through **Arcesilaus** (radical *epochē*, attacking the Stoic claim to an infallible "grasping impression," on the ground that felt conviction is not a mark of truth) and **Carneades** (*to pithanon*, "the probable" — truth *exists* but is inaccessible, so graded plausibility, not certainty, guides practice). The decisive contrast the concept files draw explicitly: **Academics affirm that truth exists** and permit probabilistic judgment; **Pyrrhonists suspend judgment on everything, including whether truth exists at all** — targeted probabilism against universal suspension, same form (*epochē*), different scope and different outcome.

Two further comparisons recur across both skepticisms. Pyrrho's four-branched formula — a thing "no more is than is not, or both is and is not, or neither is nor is not" — has the same four-cornered shape as the Buddhist **catuṣkoṭi** (Ch 06/12), and the Pyrrhonist's refusal to assert any thesis (*aphasia*) structurally echoes Nāgārjuna's own "I have no thesis" (*Vigrahavyāvartanī* 29); but Madhyamaka's no-thesis serves a determinate soteriology (śūnyatā, the two truths, liberation from grasping), while Pyrrhonism is a content-free therapy aimed only at *ataraxia* — no emptiness-doctrine, no *nirvāṇa*, suspension is the whole of it. And against **Cārvāka** (again): Cārvāka is *dogmatic* — it positively asserts materialism and flatly denies inference, soul, and karma — while Pyrrhonism asserts nothing and suspends judgment even about materialism; denial is itself a dogma the Pyrrhonist refuses to make.

## 15. Cross-tradition harvest {#harvest}

Collected in one place, the pattern repeating across §1–§14 is the same pattern Ch 11 names for Vedānta and Ch 13 names for Neoplatonism: a real, structural parallel gets discovered, and then a single load-bearing difference is what keeps the two sides from collapsing into "the same idea in two languages."

| Greek concept | genuine structural parallel | the one fact that breaks the identity |
|---|---|---|
| Parmenidean Being (§1) | brahman | *to eon* is a logical deduction; Brahman is realised consciousness (*cit*), with no not-being-is-unthinkable argument behind it |
| Parmenidean Being (§1) | Jain *sat* | opposite verdicts on change: Parmenides excludes it as not-being, Jain *sat* is defined *by* origination-cessation-persistence together |
| Democritean/Epicurean atoms (§2, §11) | Jain/Vaiśeṣika *paramāṇu* | Greek atoms are qualitatively neutral (shape/size only); Indian atoms carry intrinsic sensory qualities |
| Democritean void (§2) | śūnyatā | void is a positive spatial container; śūnyatā is the absence of *svabhāva* — opposite ontological direction |
| Platonic Forms (§3) | brahman / māyā | Forms are many, transcendent, non-substantial archetypes; Brahman is one, is the world's substance, and *is* the self |
| Aristotelian *ousia* (§5) | Jain/Vaiśeṣika *dravya* | the closest parallel in the chapter — but hylomorphism (matter+form) has no Jain equivalent, and *samavāya* has no Aristotelian one |
| Aristotelian four causes (§7) | *pratītyasamutpāda* | both reject mono-causal reduction; only Aristotle's scheme carries a *telos* (purposive end) |
| Aristotelian syllogistic (§8) | Nyāya *anumāna* | Aristotle stipulates the universal premise; Nyāya's *vyāpti* must be empirically earned |
| Aristotelian eudaimonia (§9) | Jain *cāritra* / Buddhist *nirvāṇa* | Aristotle's good life is *lived inside* the world (*polis*); Indian liberation-ethics moves *out of* it |
| Epicurean *ataraxia* (§11) | Buddhist *nirvāṇa* | tranquility within one finite life vs. release from the rebirth-cycle itself |
| Stoic *logos* (§12) | brahman | material, corporeal, rational vs. immaterial and beyond rationality |
| Cynic *autarkeia*/*askēsis* (§13) | Jain *aparigraha*/*tapas* | secular self-mastery in one life vs. karma-shedding across rebirths toward *mokṣa* |
| Pyrrhonist *aphasia* (§14) | Madhyamaka *prasaṅga* | both refuse a thesis; only Madhyamaka's no-thesis serves a soteriology (emptiness, liberation) |

The lesson is not that the parallels are fake — several (Parmenides/Jain-sat as an *opposite*, Aristotle/dravya as a *genuine convergence*) are among the sharpest comparisons in the whole corpus. The lesson is that naming the parallel and naming the break are the same act of care, and skipping the second half is exactly the failure mode this project exists to prevent (Charter §0).

## 16. Why this matters, and where it leads {#why}

This chapter is the foundation the rest of the Greek-world comparanda stand on. Chapter 13 opens with Plotinus reading himself as a faithful expositor of Plato — and everything that makes Ch 13 work depends on vocabulary built here: the Forms (§3) become the contents of Plotinus's *Nous*; the immanent/transcendent universals quarrel between Aristotle (§6) and Plato (§3) recurs, transposed, in Porphyry's *Isagoge* and its unanswered "problem of universals"; *ousia* (§5) and *logos* (§12) both feed directly into how later Neoplatonists and Stoics-in-dialogue-with-Neoplatonism describe the active ordering principle of the cosmos. Read in sequence — Parmenides sets the problem, Democritus and Plato give two rival answers, Aristotle systematises against both, the Hellenistic schools turn the whole apparatus toward *how to live* — Ch 13's "third-century revival of Plato" is the next chapter in a story, not a stand-alone curiosity.

The comparative payoff mirrors Ch 11 and Ch 13 exactly: holding a Greek idea and an Indian idea side by side sharpens *both*. Aristotle's *ousia* is clearer once you see exactly where it converges with Jain *dravya* (matter-independent substance persisting through change) and exactly where it diverges (hylomorphism has no Jain counterpart). Stoic *logos* is clearer once "material and rational" is held firmly against Brahman's "immaterial and beyond reason." The discipline throughout has been the same one this whole project runs on: analogy is not identity, and the value is in knowing exactly where each analogy stops.
