## How to use this chapter

Chapters 01–02 were inside Jainism: *what exists* and *how Jains say we know it*. This chapter steps outside. Four classical Indian schools — Jain, Nyāya, Buddhist, Mīmāṃsā — all asked the same question: **what are the legitimate ways a belief can count as knowledge?** They gave four different lists. Mapping those lists against each other is the single most productive comparison in Indian philosophy, because the schools were not talking past each other — they were arguing, in writing, for centuries, over the *same* examples (smoke and fire, the fat man who does not eat by day).

You do not need Chapter 02 to start here, but §2 will make more sense if you have met the naya/pramāṇa pair (Ch 02 §2.2).

Leave `> stuck` on its own line where you get lost; paste the teaching prompt; it will re-explain in place from a different angle.

## 1. The shared question and the four answers {#question}

*→ concept files: [pramana.md](../concepts/pramana.md) · [pramana-nyaya.md](../concepts/pramana-nyaya.md) · [dignaga-pramana.md](../concepts/dignaga-pramana.md) · [mimamsa-pramana.md](../concepts/mimamsa-pramana.md)*

A **pramāṇa** is a *means of valid knowing* — a pathway by which a cognition earns the status of knowledge rather than mere opinion. Every school agrees there is such a thing as valid cognition; they disagree on how many irreducible pathways there are. "Irreducible" is the key word: nobody denies that we learn from testimony, but a school that thinks testimony is *really just a kind of inference* will not list it separately.

Here are the four answers side by side. This one table is the spine of the whole chapter — every later section is a column of it expanded.

| pramāṇa | Nyāya | Mīmāṃsā (Bhāṭṭa) | Buddhist (Dignāga) | Jain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perception — *pratyakṣa* | ✓ (sensory + conceptual) | ✓ | ✓ (non-conceptual only) | ✓ — but **direct/super-sensory** (parokṣa covers the senses) |
| Inference — *anumāna* | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | subsumed under *parokṣa* |
| Comparison — *upamāna* | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ (reduces to inference) | ✗ |
| Testimony — *śabda* | ✓ | ✓ (Vedic, authorless) | ✗ (reduces to inference) | subsumed under *śruta* (parokṣa) |
| Postulation — *arthāpatti* | ✗ (→ inference) | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| Non-apprehension — *anupalabdhi* | ✗ | ✓ (Kumārila only) | ✗ | ✗ |
| **Total** | **4** | **5–6** | **2** | **2** |

Two schools land on "2" (Buddhist and Jain) but for opposite reasons — and they draw the line between the two in opposite places. That collision is §2 and §4. The "more is better" wing (Mīmāṃsā, 6) and the "less is better" wing (Dignāga, 2) frame the whole debate; Nyāya sits in the middle at 4 and is the tradition everyone else argues against.

## 2. The Jain answer and its quiet inversion {#jain}

*→ concept file: [pramana.md](../concepts/pramana.md)*

The Tattvārtha Sūtra gives the Jain epistemic toolkit in one verse:

> **TS 1.6** (directly fetched, Vijay K. Jain 2018): *pramāṇanayairadhigamaḥ* — "Knowledge [of the seven categories] is attained by means of **pramāṇa** and **naya**."

Pramāṇa and naya are a pair (naya is Chapter 02's subject). Pūjyapāda's gloss draws the sharpest line in the corpus:

| | pramāṇa (*sakalādeśa*) | naya (*vikalādeśa*) |
|---|---|---|
| literally | "complete statement" | "partial statement" |
| grasps | the whole substance, all attributes at once | one attribute or mode |
| who can use it fully | only an omniscient being (kevala-jñāna) | every finite knower |

So far this looks like Nyāya's "valid cognition." But Jainism then does something none of the other three do — it **inverts the meaning of pratyakṣa.** TS 1.9 lists five kinds of knowledge (sensory *mati*, scriptural *śruta*, clairvoyant *avadhi*, telepathic *manaḥparyāya*, omniscient *kevala*) and sorts them like this:

| Jain division | Sanskrit | which knowledge-types | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | pratyakṣa | avadhi, manaḥparyāya, kevala | unmediated — the soul knows *without* the senses |
| **Indirect** | parokṣa | mati (sensory) + śruta (scriptural) | mediated by sense-organs and language |

> ⚠️ **The inversion you must not miss.** In Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and ordinary speech, *pratyakṣa* = sense perception — the most everyday kind of knowing. In Jainism, **sense perception is parokṣa (indirect)**, because the soul reaches the object only *through* an organ. *Pratyakṣa* is reserved for the soul knowing directly, of which the limit case is omniscience. This is not a small terminological quirk: it encodes the Jain claim that the soul (jīva) is itself the knower and the senses are a *limitation*, not the source, of knowledge. A reader who imports the Nyāya meaning of pratyakṣa into a Jain text will get the system exactly backwards.

(The Jain divergence on pratyakṣa is flagged in the concept file as not-yet-independently-sourced from a comparative-epistemology text — treat the *fact* of the inversion as solid, the precise scholarly framing as medium.)

## 3. Nyāya: four pathways, perception first {#nyaya}

*→ concept files: [pramana-nyaya.md](../concepts/pramana-nyaya.md) · [anumana-nyaya.md](../concepts/anumana-nyaya.md)*

Nyāya (the "logic" school, founded on Gautama's *Nyāyasūtra*, ~200 CE) is the mainstream against which the others define themselves. It admits four pramāṇas and treats perception as foundational — Vātsyāyana's argument is that inference, comparison, and testimony all *presuppose* perception, so perception is first not just in the list but in dependence-order.

| pramāṇa | Sanskrit | what it is | worked example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perception | pratyakṣa | direct sense–object contact; non-verbal, non-deviating | seeing smoke on the hill |
| Inference | anumāna | concluding from an invariable sign (*vyāpti*) | inferring fire from the smoke |
| Comparison | upamāna | learning an unfamiliar thing by stated similarity | "a wild ox is like a cow" → recognising one |
| Testimony | śabda | the assertion of a trustworthy expert (*āpta*) | being told there is fire |

Inference is where Nyāya built its machinery, so it gets its own section (§5). One structural point to carry forward: Nyāya is a **realist** school — it holds that the universals (*jāti*, §6) and the self (*ātman*) that its inferences talk about are real features of the world. Every disagreement that follows traces back to whether the opponent shares that realism.

## 4. Buddhist (Dignāga): only two, and perception says nothing {#dignaga}

*→ concept files: [dignaga-pramana.md](../concepts/dignaga-pramana.md) · [hetu-vidya.md](../concepts/hetu-vidya.md)*

Dignāga (~480–540 CE) founded the Buddhist epistemological tradition (the *pramāṇavāda*) and cut the list to **two**. His argument is elegant: there are only two kinds of *object* — the unique particular (*svalakṣaṇa*) and the general character (*sāmānya-lakṣaṇa*) — so there can be only two means of knowing them.

| pramāṇa | reaches | nature |
|---|---|---|
| **pratyakṣa** (perception) | *svalakṣaṇa* — the bare, momentary particular | pre-verbal, pre-conceptual raw sensation |
| **anumāna** (inference) | *sāmānya-lakṣaṇa* — general characters | conceptual, linguistic; absorbs comparison and testimony |

The radical move is the definition of perception as **devoid of conceptual construction** (*kalpanā-apoḍha*). The instant the mind adds a word or a category — "*this is fire*" — the act is no longer perception; it has become inference. Pure perception is real but mute. This directly denies Nyāya's "determinate perception" (*savikalpaka-pratyakṣa*), the everyday seeing-*that*-it-is-fire that Nyāya counts as perception.

Because everything conceptual is funnelled into inference, Dignāga can dissolve comparison and testimony into it — and he does (§7). Underneath sits his theory of meaning, *apoha* (§6), and his whole tradition's name, *hetu-vidyā*, "the science of reasons" (§8).

## 5. Mīmāṃsā: more pathways, and the authorless word {#mimamsa}

*→ concept files: [mimamsa-pramana.md](../concepts/mimamsa-pramana.md) · [arthapatti.md](../concepts/arthapatti.md) · [sabda-pramana.md](../concepts/sabda-pramana.md)*

Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā is at the opposite pole from Dignāga: it *adds* pathways. It is primarily a school of Vedic interpretation, and its whole epistemology is engineered to protect the authority of the Veda. It keeps Nyāya's four and adds one or two more:

- **Arthāpatti (postulation)** — positing an unseen fact to make two known facts hang together. *"Devadatta is fat; Devadatta does not eat by day"* forces the conclusion *he eats at night.* No prior rule "fat non-day-eaters eat at night" was ever learned, so (Mīmāṃsā argues) this is not inference — there is no pre-established *vyāpti* to recall. The resolution is generated on the spot by the pressure of the contradiction. (See the ⚠️ in §6.3.)
- **Anupalabdhi (non-apprehension)** — knowing an *absence*. "There is no pot here" is, for Kumārila, a direct apprehension of absence, not a negative inference. Prabhākara's sub-school rejects this, which is why Mīmāṃsā's total is **5 or 6** depending on which sub-school you read.

Two doctrines power the school, both built to answer the Buddhist charge that the Vedas, being human-made, could err:

| doctrine | claim | what it buys |
|---|---|---|
| **apauruṣeyatva** | the Vedas have *no author* — they are eternal, beginningless | an authorless text cannot have a biased or mistaken author, so its authority can't be impeached |
| **svataḥ-prāmāṇya** | cognitions are valid *by default*; invalidity must be positively shown | inverts the burden of proof — Nyāya must *certify* knowledge, Mīmāṃsā presumes it |

This is the most extreme position on testimony in the table: Vedic *śabda* is valid not because a reliable person said it, but because *no one* said it. Hold that thought for §7.

## 6. The engine under every inference: *vyāpti* {#vyapti}

*→ concept files: [vyapti.md](../concepts/vyapti.md) · [jati.md](../concepts/jati.md) · [apoha.md](../concepts/apoha.md)*

### 6.1 What vyāpti is {#vyapti-def}

Every school that accepts inference at all rests it on one relation: **vyāpti**, *invariable concomitance* — the "wherever-X-there-Y" pervasion that lets a sign carry you to a conclusion.

> *yatra dhūmaḥ tatra vahniḥ* — "wherever there is smoke, there is fire."

It comes in two directions, and a complete inference uses both:

| form | Sanskrit | statement |
|---|---|---|
| positive | anvaya-vyāpti | wherever there is smoke, there is fire (co-presence) |
| negative | vyatireka-vyāpti | wherever there is no fire, there is no smoke (co-absence) |

Everyone uses vyāpti. The fight is over **what makes it true** — and that fight is really a fight about whether universals are real.

### 6.2 The grounding dispute {#vyapti-grounding}

| tradition | what grounds the pervasion |
|---|---|
| **Nyāya** | **real universals.** "Smokiness" and "fieriness" are genuine repeatable properties (*jāti*) inhering in every instance; perceiving the particulars also grasps the universals, which bind the concomitance across all of time |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | **trairūpya** — a purely formal three-condition test (the sign is in the subject, present in similar cases, absent in *all* dissimilar cases); no universals invoked |
| **Buddhist (Dharmakīrti)** | **real necessity** of two kinds: causation (smoke is *caused by* fire) or identity (an oak *is* a tree); Nyāya's abstract universals rejected as fictions |
| **Jain** | induction; both anvaya and vyatireka recognised — but any inferential claim is *naya*-relative, valid within a standpoint, never absolutely (Ch 02) |
| **Cārvāka (materialist)** | **nothing** — universal concomitance is unknowable; you can never check all cases, so inference is never certain |

### 6.3 Universals vs exclusion: jāti against apoha {#jati-apoha}

This is the metaphysical bedrock of the whole quarrel. When you call two animals "cows," what makes that one word correct for both?

- **Nyāya — jāti (real universals).** A single, eternal *cowhood* (*gotva*) really inheres in every cow through a real relation (*samavāya*). It is "one thing existing in many" (Annambhaṭṭa). Without it, Udayana argues, causal regularity — and therefore vyāpti — would have nothing to be true *of*.
- **Buddhist — apoha (meaning by exclusion).** There is *no* real cowhood. The word "cow" works by **double negation**: it means "not-non-cow." Concepts are delimiters that carve the world by excluding what falls outside; they are mental impositions (*kalpanā*) on a stream of unrepeatable particulars. Dharmakīrti grounds this causally — similar particulars produce indistinguishable mental images, and we *judge* them "the same" without there being any shared essence.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — jāti and apoha are rivals, not two words for "concept."**
>
> Both explain the same datum: we successfully use general words. The surface similarity tempts a reader to treat them as interchangeable accounts of "universals." They are **opposite ontologies.** Jāti says the universal is *out there*, a real feature the word latches onto. Apoha says there is *nothing out there* but particulars, and the word's whole content is the negative act of excluding non-cows. Collapsing them erases the deepest fault line in Indian philosophy of language — realism vs nominalism — and makes every downstream dispute (about vyāpti, testimony, perception) unintelligible.
>
> Note also: apoha is about **semantics** (how words mean), while Buddhist *śūnyatā* is about **ontology** (particulars lack svabhāva too). They are compatible but distinct — don't fuse those either.

## 7. The Nyāya syllogism, its fallacies, and its doubt-remover {#anumana}

*→ concept files: [anumana-nyaya.md](../concepts/anumana-nyaya.md) · [hetvabhasa.md](../concepts/hetvabhasa.md) · [tarka.md](../concepts/tarka.md)*

### 7.1 The five-member proof {#five-member}

Nyāya's *public* inference (*parārthānumāna* — inference *for another*, used in debate) has five explicit steps. The redundancy is deliberate: each step is separately checkable by an opponent.

| member | Sanskrit | function | the hill example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | pratijñā | the claim | "there is fire on the hill" |
| Reason | hetu | the observed sign | "because there is smoke" |
| Example | udāharaṇa | the vyāpti-carrying case | "wherever smoke, there fire — as in a kitchen" |
| Application | upanaya | apply the sign to the subject | "the hill has such smoke" |
| Conclusion | nigamana | restate the thesis as proved | "therefore the hill has fire" |

The **example** step is what makes Nyāya inference *empirical* — the universal is shown via real cases (a kitchen), not stipulated. (This is the contrast with Aristotle noted in §9.)

### 7.2 Where inference fails: hetvābhāsa {#hetvabhasa}

A *hetvābhāsa* is a reason that **looks** valid but is defective — the boundary line of good inference. The Nyāyasūtra (NS I.2.4, quoted in the WisdomLib essay) names five:

| Sanskrit | the defect | example |
|---|---|---|
| savyabhicāra | inconclusive — the sign occurs with *and* without the target (vyāpti fails) | "sound is permanent because imperceptible" — imperceptibility attaches to both permanent and impermanent things |
| viruddha | contradictory — the sign actually proves the *opposite* | "sound is permanent because produced" — being produced proves *im*permanence |
| prakaraṇasama | counterbalanced — an equally good reason supports the opposite | two sides each with a hetu of equal force |
| sādhyasama | unproven — the reason needs the very proof the conclusion does | "sound is eternal because it has eternal qualities" — circular |
| kālātīta / bādhita | overridden — a stronger pramāṇa (perception, scripture) contradicts the conclusion | inference defeated by what you can plainly see |

Each fallacy is a *mode of vyāpti-failure*: savyabhicāra breaks the pervasion, viruddha reverses it, bādhita overrides it. (Dignāga's tradition independently arrives at a shorter list — *asiddha / viruddha / anaikāntika* — from his trairūpya; the two traditions converge on the same failure-types by different routes.)

### 7.3 Tarka: removing doubt, not making knowledge {#tarka}

What if you simply *doubt* a vyāpti — maybe smoke sometimes comes without fire? **Tarka** (the 8th of Nyāya's sixteen categories) is the repair tool. It is *reductio ad absurdum* run in service of a positive claim:

1. the vyāpti is doubted →
2. *suppose* smoke without fire →
3. then ordinary causal perception would be systematically deceptive — absurd →
4. reject the supposition →
5. the vyāpti stands.

Tarka is **not a pramāṇa** — it generates no new knowledge, it only clears away doubt so that an existing inference can do its work. Gaṅgeśa calls it the last resort when repeated observation has not settled the matter.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — tarka is not Nāgārjuna's *prasaṅga*.**
>
> Both use the same logical move: assume a position, derive absurdity, reject it. But the *aim* is opposite. **Tarka** endorses the positive claim that survived the reductio (the vyāpti *does* hold). Madhyamaka **prasaṅga** (Ch 04 territory) refutes *all* positions and endorses *none* — it shows every view collapses, and rests in none of them. Same form, antithetical intent: confirm-one vs refute-all. Reading prasaṅga as "Buddhist tarka" smuggles a constructive goal into a method whose whole point is to be non-constructive.
>
> Mirror-image pairing: **tarka vs hetvābhāsa.** Tarka confirms a valid inference by exposing the absurdity of denying its vyāpti; hetvābhāsa catalogues the invalid inferences. They are the positive and negative boundaries of the same thing — *anumāna*.

## 8. Testimony, and the Buddhist who dissolved it {#sabda}

*→ concept files: [sabda-pramana.md](../concepts/sabda-pramana.md) · [hetu-vidya.md](../concepts/hetu-vidya.md) · [dharmottara.md](../concepts/dharmottara.md)*

### 8.1 Four positions on whether words can be a separate pramāṇa {#sabda-four}

| tradition | position | commitment |
|---|---|---|
| **Nyāya** | independent 4th pramāṇa | *āptopadeśaḥ śabdaḥ* (NS 1.1.7): testimony is the pronouncement of a trustworthy expert (*āpta*); understanding a sentence is one direct cognitive event, not a chain of inferences — but its validity rides on the speaker's reliability |
| **Mīmāṃsā** | independent — and *strongest* in its Vedic form | Vedic *śabda* is valid because authorless (*apauruṣeya*) and self-certifying; it owes nothing to any speaker's reliability |
| **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | **not** independent — reduces to inference | words mean by *apoha* (exclusion); understanding "fire" is an inferential act about exclusion-classes, so testimony is a species of *anumāna* |
| **Jain** | *parokṣa* (indirect), via *śruta* | scriptural knowledge (*śruta-jñāna*) is mediated by language, hence indirect; valid only if traceable to an omniscient's (kevalin's) perception |

### 8.2 The Kumārila–Dignāga collision {#kumarila-dignaga}

This single disagreement organised centuries of Indian philosophy. Dignāga (~5th–6th c.) argued testimony collapses into inference. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (~7th c.) wrote the *Ślokavārttika* specifically to defend Vedic śabda's irreducibility and eternal authority. The Mīmāṃsaka needs śabda to be its *own* pathway, because the entire authority of the Veda hangs on it; the Buddhist needs it *not* to be, because an authorless eternal Veda is exactly the thing his momentary, dependently-arisen world has no room for. The epistemology was never only epistemology — it was a proxy war over whether the Veda speaks with unimpeachable authority.

## 9. The Buddhist logic tradition and its transmission {#hetu-vidya}

*→ concept files: [hetu-vidya.md](../concepts/hetu-vidya.md) · [dharmottara.md](../concepts/dharmottara.md)*

Dignāga's two-pramāṇa system was not a one-off; it founded a school — *hetu-vidyā*, "the science of reasons," one of the Five Major Sciences in later Tibetan learning. Its through-line:

| figure | date | contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Asaṅga | ~4th c. | earliest Buddhist logic chapter (in the *Yogācārabhūmi*); pre-systematic |
| **Dignāga** | ~480–540 | founded the tradition; *Pramāṇasamuccaya*; trairūpya; 2 pramāṇas; apoha; cut Nyāya's 5-member syllogism to 3 (*pakṣa / hetu / dṛṣṭānta*) |
| **Dharmakīrti** | ~625 | *Pramāṇavārttika* (7 treatises); grounded vyāpti in causal necessity or identity; tightened the formalism |
| **Dharmottara** | ~740–800 | Kashmiri commentator; defined pramāṇa as "capable of verification"; introduced *niścaya-pratyaya* (the ascertaining cognition between raw perception and judgement); his *Nyāyabinduṭīkā* is the only one of his works surviving in Sanskrit and became a backbone of Tibetan monastic study |

Dharmottara is the hinge: without his commentary, Dharmakīrti's terse verses would have been far harder to read, and the tradition might not have transmitted so cleanly into Tibet (where pramāṇa, Tib. *tshad ma*, remains a core curriculum). His school read the whole tradition through a Yogācāra "mind-only" lens — a live interpretive split from the more realist Sautrāntika reading.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — "Indian logic" is not one tradition.** Surveys often teach Nyāya and Buddhist *hetu-vidyā* together as "Indian logic." They were **historically adversarial** and differ on three load-bearing points: the *number* of pramāṇas (4 vs 2), the *members* of a syllogism (5 vs 3), and the *ground* of vyāpti (real universals vs causal-necessity-plus-apoha). They share vocabulary (pratyakṣa, anumāna, hetu, vyāpti) precisely because they were arguing with each other — shared terms are evidence of a *dispute*, not of agreement.

## 10. A caution on the Western comparison {#west}

Several concept files carry a `structurally-parallel-to: aristotle-logic` link, and it is worth stating the rule the corpus follows. Nyāya's five-member inference and Dignāga's trairūpya are *formally comparable* to the Aristotelian syllogism — all three locate the force of an argument in a necessary universal connection between a middle and a major term. That is a real structural parallel and the corpus marks it as such.

But the parallel is **structural, not identity**, and the difference is sharp: the Nyāya *example* step (*udāharaṇa*) makes the universal **empirical** — shown through real cases like a kitchen hearth — whereas Aristotle's major premise is **stipulated** as a formal starting point. Indian inference earns its universal by observation (and, for Dignāga, by apoha); Aristotle assumes it. "Nyāya invented the syllogism" and "the syllogism is just Aristotle in Sanskrit" are both wrong for the same reason: they read a shared *aim* as a shared *method*.
