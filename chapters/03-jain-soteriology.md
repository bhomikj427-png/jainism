## 1. The map: seven categories of reality — *tattva-jain* {#tattvas}

*→ concept file: [tattva-jain.md](../concepts/tattva-jain.md)*

### 1.1 TS 1.4 — the foundational verse {#tattvas-verse}

The entire Tattvārtha Sūtra is named after its opening taxonomy. TS 1.4, directly fetched from Vijay K. Jain 2018 (Motilal Banarsidass):

> **jīvājīvāsravabandhasaṃvaranirjarāmokṣāstattvaṃ**
> "Soul, non-soul, influx, bondage, stoppage, gradual dissociation, and liberation constitute the reality (*tattva*)."
> — TS 1.4, Vijay K. Jain (tr.), Motilal Banarsidass 2018

Seven Sanskrit words, one per tattva. The Tattvārthasūtra (*tattva* + *artha* + *sūtra* = "aphorisms on the meaning of reality") is structured to explain all seven in order. This is both an ontological map and a problem-solution arc.

### 1.2 The arc encoded in the seven tattvas {#tattvas-arc}

Pūjyapāda (*Sarvārthasiddhi*, via Vijay K. Jain 2018) explains the ordering:

| # | tattva | Sanskrit | TS chapters | role in the arc |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Soul | jīva | 1–2 | *what exists and gets affected* |
| 2 | Non-soul | ajīva | 5 | *the matter that does the affecting* |
| 3 | Influx | āsrava | 6 | **problem begins** — karma flows toward soul |
| 4 | Bondage | bandha | 8 | **problem deepens** — karma adheres to soul |
| 5 | Stoppage | saṃvara | 9 | **solution begins** — new influx blocked |
| 6 | Dissociation | nirjarā | 8–9 | **solution deepens** — bonded karma shed |
| 7 | Liberation | mokṣa | 10 | **problem solved** — soul karma-free |

Tattvas 1–2 establish the players. Tattvas 3–4 are the diagnosis. Tattvas 5–6 are the treatment. Tattva 7 is the cure. Read this way, the TS is a complete philosophical medicine — identifying the disease (karma bondage), its mechanism (āsrava + bandha), its treatment protocol (saṃvara + nirjarā), and its definition of health (mokṣa).

**Nine-tattva variant:** Some popular Jain teaching adds *puṇya* (merit) and *pāpa* (demerit) for nine tattvas. Pūjyapāda's commentary holds these are "implied in influx and bondage" — the seven-tattva count is TS-canonical. Both counts are standard in Jain teaching; neither is heretical.

## 2. The soul that needs liberating — *jīva* {#jiva}

*→ concept file: [jiva.md](../concepts/jiva.md)*

### 2.1 Defining characteristic: consciousness (*upayoga*) {#jiva-upayoga}

TS 2.8, confirmed across multiple sources:

> **upayogo lakṣaṇam**
> "Consciousness (*upayoga*) is the distinctive characteristic of the soul."

*Upayoga* is **active cognitive engagement** — not mere passive awareness, but the soul's ongoing act of knowing and perceiving. It divides into two modes (TS 2.9):

| mode | Sanskrit | character |
|---|---|---|
| Undifferentiated apprehension | darśana | bare presence of an object; pre-conceptual |
| Differentiated cognition | jñāna | conceptual, structured knowing |

At the lowest level (stage 1 of the 14 guṇasthānas), upayoga is heavily obscured by karma. At the highest level (stage 13–14), it expands into *kevala-jñāna* — complete, omniscient cognition. The entire soteriology is the story of upayoga being progressively un-obscured.

### 2.2 Two states of jīva {#jiva-states}

TS 2.10: souls divide into two classes:

| class | Sanskrit | condition | location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transmigrating | saṃsārin | bound by karma; cycling through embodiment | anywhere in loka |
| Liberated | mukta (siddha) | karma-free; permanent omniscient upayoga | siddha-śilā at lokāgra |

**Key structural point:** Jainism is a **pluralism** — there are infinitely many distinct jīvas; no jīva is ever absorbed into another or into a universal Being. Every saṃsārin is a distinct soul on its own path. Every mukta is a distinct siddha at lokāgra. This is not a detail — it is the entire difference from Advaita Vedānta's account (see §10 conflation alert).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *jīva* ≠ Advaita *ātman***
>
> Both terms are translated "soul." The surface similarity is strong: both claim a real conscious self that survives physical death.
>
> The difference is structural: Advaita holds that individual ātman is ultimately identical to universal Brahman — liberation (*mokṣa*) is the recognition of that identity, dissolving individuation. Jainism holds there is no universal Ātman; each jīva remains permanently distinct even in liberation. The siddha at lokāgra is not merged into anything — it is a distinct point of omniscient consciousness. Mapping Jain jīva onto Advaita ātman silently imports the monism that Jainism explicitly rejects.

## 3. The binding agent — karma as physical matter {#karma}

*→ concept file: [karma.md](../concepts/karma.md)*

### 3.1 The uniquely Jain claim {#karma-physical}

Jainism holds that karma is **fine physical matter** (*sūkṣma pudgala*) — a specific class of the infinitesimally small skandha particles described in Ch 01. This is not a metaphor. Karma particles flow through space, adhere to souls, and are physically shed. No other major Indian philosophical tradition makes this claim.

### 3.2 Eight karma types {#karma-types}

The standard Jain eight-fold karma taxonomy (from the Bhagavatī Sūtra tradition, not the TS itself — different primary text, same tradition):

**Ghātīya (destructive)** — directly obstruct the soul's natural capacities:

| karma | Sanskrit | what it obscures |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge-obscuring | jñānāvaraṇīya | infinite knowledge |
| Perception-obscuring | darśanāvaraṇīya | right insight / undifferentiated apprehension |
| Deluding | mohanīya | right belief and right conduct |
| Obstructive | antarāya | virtuous capacities (energy, charity, etc.) |

**Aghātīya (non-destructive)** — condition embodied existence without directly obstructing capacity:

| karma | Sanskrit | what it determines |
|---|---|---|
| Feeling-producing | vedanīya | quality of pleasure/pain experience |
| Lifespan-determining | āyuh | type and duration of next embodiment |
| Body-making | nāma | physical form and individuality |
| Status-determining | gotra | social position / family of birth |

**The dissolution sequence:** At stage 13 (sayoga-kevalī), the four *ghātīya* karmas are destroyed → kevala-jñāna (omniscience) arises. At stage 14, the four *aghātīya* karmas exhaust at death → mokṣa. The distinction between ghātīya and aghātīya is what makes kevala-jñāna possible *before* death — the soul can be omniscient while still embodied.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain karma ≠ Hindu/Vedic karma ≠ Buddhist karma**
>
> The same Sanskrit word *karma* (action/deed and its effects) operates in all three traditions, but the underlying ontology is incompatible:
>
> | tradition | what karma *is* | mechanism |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Jain** | fine physical matter; explicit particles | flows physically into soul; physically shed |
> | **Hindu/Vedic** | law of intentional action; residual impression (*saṃskāra*) | moral law governs rebirth; not material particles |
> | **Buddhist** | *cetanā* (intentional action) and its mental traces | intention determines karmic fruit; no soul to bind |
>
> The Jain position is the most radical: karma literally accumulates as matter in the soul-space and must be physically purged through austerity. The Hindu and Buddhist accounts are intentional/moral-legal frameworks. Calling all three "the same doctrine of karma" erases this structural difference.

## 4. The adhesive — *kaṣāya* (passions) {#kashaya}

*→ concept file: [kashaya.md](../concepts/kashaya.md)*

### 4.1 Four passions, sixteen intensities {#kashaya-matrix}

TS 6.5 (directly fetched, Vijay K. Jain 2018) identifies four passions (*kaṣāya*):

| passion | Sanskrit | orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Anger | krodha | aversion |
| Pride | māna | aversion |
| Deceit | māyā | attachment |
| Greed | lobha | attachment |

Each passion manifests at four intensities, giving 16 sub-types of *cāritra-mohanīya* (conduct-deluding) karma:

| intensity | Sanskrit | what it blocks | eliminated at guṇasthāna |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infinite-chain | anantānubandhi | right faith itself | 4 (right faith arises) |
| Non-renounceable | apratyākhyāna | partial vows | 5 (layperson's *aṇuvratas*) |
| Renounceable | pratyākhyāna | full renunciation | 6 (monk's full *mahāvratas*) |
| Smoldering | saṃjvalana | perfect conduct | 10–12 |

### 4.2 Why passions are central {#kashaya-mechanism}

Without passions, karma particles touch the soul but do not bind — like water on a lotus leaf (*īryāpatha* āsrava: non-binding influx). With passions, karma adheres with duration and intensity (*sāmparāyika* āsrava: binding influx). The passions are the adhesive; yoga (mental/bodily activity) is merely the channel that karma flows through. Removing activity (saṃvara's *gupti* practices) closes the channel. But the deeper work is removing the passions that make karma stick.

## 5. Influx — *āsrava* {#asrava}

*→ concept file: [asrava.md](../concepts/asrava.md)*

### 5.1 TS 6.2 — the water-gate analogy {#asrava-def}

TS 6.2 (directly fetched, Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **sa āsravaḥ**
> "The activity of the body, the speech, and the mind — the *yoga* — is the influx (āsrava)."

Pūjyapāda's commentary (*Sarvārthasiddhi*, via Vijay K. Jain 2018): "Just as the gate from which water flows into a lake is called *āsrava*, similarly the activity or *yoga*, which is the conduit for the flow of karmic matter into the soul, is called the influx."

**Warning on the word *yoga*:** In Jain usage, *yoga* = "vibration/activity of mind, speech, and body." This has nothing to do with the Pātañjali yoga of meditation postures. It means any exertion of the soul's capacities — any thought, word, or bodily movement — that opens the soul-space to incoming karma.

### 5.2 Binding vs. non-binding influx {#asrava-types}

| kind | Sanskrit | condition | result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binding | sāmparāyika | yoga **with** kaṣāya (passions present) | karma adheres; genuine bondage (bandha) |
| Non-binding | īryāpatha | yoga **without** kaṣāya (highly restrained practitioner) | karma touches momentarily, does not stick; no lasting bondage |

**Position in the arc:** Āsrava is what saṃvara closes. Once saṃvara is fully operative, no new āsrava enters — not because the soul stops all activity, but because passions are gone and residual activity produces only non-binding īryāpatha influx.

## 6. Bondage — *bandha* {#bandha}

*→ concept file: [bandha.md](../concepts/bandha.md)*

### 6.1 Five causes of bondage — TS 8.1 {#bandha-causes}

TS 8.1 (directly fetched, Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> "Wrong-belief (*mithyādarśana*), non-abstinence (*avirati*), negligence (*pramāda*), passions (*kaṣāya*), and activities (*yoga*) are the causes of bondage."

| # | Sanskrit | English | what it represents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | mithyādarśana | wrong belief | false view of reality; 5 varieties |
| 2 | avirati | non-abstinence | failure to observe ethical restraints toward the 6 classes of beings |
| 3 | pramāda | negligence | indifference / carelessness toward practice |
| 4 | kaṣāya | passions | the adhesive (see §4) |
| 5 | yoga | activities | the channel (mind / speech / body vibration) |

**Note:** The popular summary "four causes of bondage" (omitting *yoga*) comes from an inaccurate Wikipedia summary of TS 8.1. The directly fetched critical-edition text lists **five** causes. This discrepancy is flagged in bandha.md.

### 6.2 Four kinds of bondage — TS 8.3 {#bandha-kinds}

The *type* and *amount* of karma is determined by yoga; the *duration* and *intensity* is determined by kaṣāya:

| what determines it | Sanskrit | what it specifies |
|---|---|---|
| **Yoga** | prakṛti-bandha | *which* of the 8 karma types is being bonded |
| **Yoga** | pradeśa-bandha | *how many* karma particles are bonded (quantity) |
| **Kaṣāya** | sthiti-bandha | *how long* the karma persists before ripening |
| **Kaṣāya** | anubhāga-bandha | *how intensely* the karma will be experienced when it ripens |

This table explains why the passions (kaṣāya) matter more than the activities (yoga): yoga determines what kind and how much karma enters, but kaṣāya determines how severely and for how long it will condition the soul. A person performing many actions but with zero passion would generate only weak, brief, non-binding karma. A person performing few actions but with intense passion generates severe, long-lasting bondage.

## 7. Stopping new karma — *saṃvara* {#samvara}

*→ concept file: [samvara.md](../concepts/samvara.md)*

### 7.1 Definition — TS 9.1 {#samvara-def}

TS 9.1 (directly fetched, Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **āstravanirodhaḥ saṃvaraḥ**
> "The obstruction of influx is stoppage."

Saṃvara is not the removal of existing bondage (that is nirjarā's job). It is the *closing of the gate* — the cessation of new karma influx. Together saṃvara and nirjarā are the two active purification processes: one stops the inflow, the other drains what has already accumulated.

### 7.2 Two forms of saṃvara {#samvara-forms}

Pūjyapāda (*Sarvārthasiddhi*, via Vijay K. Jain 2018) identifies:

| form | Sanskrit | level | what it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychic stoppage | bhāva-saṃvara | internal | cessation of the dispositions and intentions that generate karma: uprooting kaṣāya and yoga at the level of awareness |
| Material stoppage | dravya-saṃvara | external | the actual interruption of physical karma-matter influx — the consequence of bhāva-saṃvara |

The inner (bhāva) precedes and causes the outer (dravya). You cannot achieve material karma-stoppage by external regulation alone; the internal dispositions must change first.

### 7.3 The practice arsenal {#samvara-practices}

TS 9.2–9.5 (confirmed via JainSquare Chapter 9 and search-result aggregation) enumerate six categories of practice that constitute saṃvara:

| category | Sanskrit | count | what it counters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restraints | gupti | 3 (mind / speech / body) | yoga — closes the āsrava channel directly |
| Carefulnesses | samiti | 5 | mindful walking, speech, eating, handling, waste |
| Virtues | dharma | 10 | forbearance, modesty, purity, truthfulness, restraint, austerity, renunciation, non-attachment, humility, continence |
| Contemplations | bhāvanā | 12 | reflective meditations (impermanence, saṃsāra, helplessness, etc.) — targets kaṣāya at the level of attention |
| Hardship endurance | parīṣahajaya | 22 | equanimity under hunger, cold, insects, pain, etc. |
| Conduct | cāritra | — | the full ethical code (*samyak-cāritra*) |

**Structural note:** The three *guptis* (restraints of mind/speech/body) are the exact inverse of the three *yogas* that constitute āsrava. Closing those three channels is the most direct form of saṃvara. The deeper practices (bhāvanā, parīṣahajaya) address kaṣāya — the adhesive — so that even residual yoga produces only non-binding *īryāpatha* influx.

## 8. Shedding accumulated karma — *nirjarā* {#nirjara}

*→ concept file: [nirjara.md](../concepts/nirjara.md)*

### 8.1 TS 8.23 — the fruition-and-fall mechanism {#nirjara-def}

TS 8.23 (directly fetched, Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **tataś ca nirjarā**
> "After fruition (*anubhava*), the shedding — nirjarā — of karmas takes place."

Pūjyapāda's analogy: karma falls away after delivering its effect, like food that decays after serving its purpose. The natural lifecycle of bonded karma is: bond → mature → deliver effect → dissolve.

### 8.2 Natural vs. accelerated shedding {#nirjara-types}

| kind | Sanskrit | mechanism | soteriological value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural ripening | sāvipāka (vipākajā) | karma runs its assigned duration and falls away on its own | passive; happens to everyone; does NOT accelerate liberation |
| Accelerated shedding | avipāka (avipākajā) | tapas (austerity) forces early ripening before natural maturity | active; the practitioner's instrument; shortens the path |

Every living being — even those with no spiritual practice — undergoes sāvipāka nirjarā. Karma bonded in past lives naturally matures and dissipates. But this is matched by ongoing āsrava and bandha from new actions. The net effect is no progress toward liberation; the karma balance stays roughly constant.

Avipāka nirjarā through tapas is what actually moves the balance — it burns through accumulated karma faster than new karma accumulates (especially once saṃvara has reduced new influx). TS 9.3 (*tapasā nirjarā ca* — "and nirjarā through austerity") makes tapas the instrument.

## 9. The fourteen stages — *guṇasthāna* {#gunasthana}

*→ concept file: [gunasthana.md](../concepts/gunasthana.md)*

### 9.1 The full progression {#gunasthana-table}

The *guṇasthāna* scheme maps the entire soteriological arc onto fourteen stations, showing exactly which of the five bandha-causes (§6.1) operate or cease at each stage. Primary enumeration from Nemicandra's *Gommaṭasāra* (~10th c.); the guṇasthāna framework is confirmed via TS Chapter 9 and Pūjyapāda's commentary (Wikipedia, citing Padmanabh Jaini 1991 and Nathmal Tatia 1994):

| # | Sanskrit name | English gloss | key event |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | mithyātva | false world-view; complete delusion | all five bondage-causes fully operative |
| 2 | sāsvādana | lingering taste of right faith (brief, transitional) | momentary glimpse, then fall back |
| 3 | samyak-mithyātva | mixed right/wrong view | unstable intermediate state |
| 4 | samyag-dṛṣṭi | right faith without restraint | *darśana-mohinīya* karma destroyed; **anantānubandhi** kaṣāya eliminated |
| 5 | deśa-virata | partial self-restraint | layperson's *aṇuvratas* commence; **apratyākhyāna** kaṣāya eliminated |
| 6 | sarva-virata | complete self-restraint | monk's full *mahāvratas*; **pratyākhyāna** kaṣāya eliminated |
| 7 | apramatta-virata | perfect vows; no negligence | *pramāda* eliminated; deep meditation possible |
| 8 | apūrva-karaṇa | novel karmic purification | gross passions still present |
| 9 | anivṛtti-karaṇa | progressively purer experience | passions persist but waning |
| 10 | sūkṣma-sāmparāya | subtlest smoldering greed only | near-complete passion cessation |
| 11 | upaśānta-kaṣāya | passions **suppressed** | peak without destruction; regression possible |
| 12 | kṣīṇa-moha | passions **destroyed** | *cāritra-mohinīya* karma eliminated; no regression possible |
| 13 | sayoga-kevalī | omniscience with bodily activity | three remaining ghātīya karmas destroyed; **kevala-jñāna arises** |
| 14 | ayoga-kevalī | omniscience without any activity | four aghātīya karmas exhaust at death; **mokṣa** |

### 9.2 The critical distinction: stage 11 vs. stage 12 {#gunasthana-11-12}

Stage 11 (*upaśānta-kaṣāya*): passions are **suppressed** (*upaśama*) — held down, not destroyed. The soul at stage 11 is in a state of great purity, but the passion-karma is merely dormant. It can re-emerge; the soul can fall all the way back to stage 1. This is *upaśama-samyaktva* (suppressional right faith).

Stage 12 (*kṣīṇa-moha*): passions are **destroyed** (*kṣaya*) — the mohanīya karma is annihilated, not suppressed. No regression is possible from stage 12 or beyond. This is *kṣāyika-samyaktva* (destructional right faith).

The difference matters: a practitioner can appear fully enlightened (stage 11) and still fall. Stage 12 is the irreversible threshold.

### 9.3 Stages 13–14: the endgame {#gunasthana-endgame}

At stage 13 (*sayoga-kevalī*): the three remaining destructive karmas — jñānāvaraṇīya, darśanāvaraṇīya, antarāya — are annihilated. The result is *kevala-jñāna*: omniscience. The soul is fully omniscient but still embodied; the four aghātīya karmas (vedanīya, āyuh, nāma, gotra) continue to run out their bonded duration.

At stage 14 (*ayoga-kevalī*): even subtle yoga ceases. The four aghātīya karmas exhaust. Physical death follows: the body drops, the soul is untethered, and mokṣa occurs.

## 10. Liberation — *mokṣa* {#moksha}

*→ concept files: [moksha.md](../concepts/moksha.md) · [loka-jain.md](../concepts/loka-jain.md)*

### 10.1 TS 10.2 — the definition {#moksha-def}

TS 10.2 (Wikipedia relay, citing TS; critical-edition text not independently fetched this run):

> "Owing to the absence of the cause of bondage and with the functioning of the dissociation of karmas, the annihilation of all karmas is liberation."

The TS itself is sometimes called the *Mokṣaśāstra* (Scripture on Liberation) — mokṣa is the telos of the entire text.

### 10.2 The liberated soul — siddha, and four infinites {#moksha-siddha}

At mokṣa the soul's natural qualities emerge fully — the four *ananta-catuṣṭaya* (four infinites), from *Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya* (a Jain text distinct from the TS; via Wikipedia "Mokṣa (Jainism)"):

| quality | Sanskrit | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| Infinite knowledge | ananta-jñāna | complete, unobstructed omniscience |
| Infinite perception | ananta-darśana | unlimited undifferentiated apprehension |
| Infinite bliss | ananta-sukha | unobstructed bliss (no vedanīya karma remains) |
| Infinite energy | ananta-vīrya | unlimited capacity (no antarāya karma remains) |

These were always the jīva's intrinsic nature — the karmas were covering them, not constituting them. Mokṣa is not the acquisition of something new; it is the removal of all obstruction to what the soul already is.

### 10.3 The cosmic destination — *siddha-śilā* / *lokāgra* {#moksha-location}

The liberated soul rises automatically to *Siddha-śilā* ("the siddha's rock/ledge") at the very summit of the Jain cosmos (*lokāgra* — the apex of *loka*). The motion is automatic: freed of karma's downward-binding weight, the intrinsically weightless soul rises like a flame. Once at lokāgra, it rests there eternally — no re-entry into saṃsāra is possible. (moksha.md; loka-jain.md.)

The siddha at lokāgra is: omniscient (kevala-jñāna in its permanent form), distinct from all other siddhas, not worshipped by, not involved with, and not causally connected to any ongoing world affairs. The Jain God-question is answered here: the Tīrthaṅkaras (*tirthankara.md*) were humans who achieved this liberation; they are revered as exemplars, not prayed to as intervening agents.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *mokṣa* ≠ Advaita *mokṣa* ≠ Buddhist *nirvāṇa***
>
> The same Sanskrit word *mokṣa* is used in Jainism and Advaita Vedānta; *nirvāṇa* is the Buddhist term for the analogous goal. All three are "liberation" from the cycle of rebirth. All three accounts are structurally incompatible on the most fundamental question: **does any individual self survive liberation?**
>
> | tradition | what liberation *is* | what survives |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Jain** | annihilation of all karma; soul rises to lokāgra | the **distinct individual jīva** — fully conscious, omniscient, permanently separate |
> | **Advaita Vedānta** | recognition that individual ātman is identical to universal Brahman | **no individual remains** — ātman merges into Brahman; individuation was *māyā* |
> | **Buddhist** (*nirvāṇa*) | cessation of craving; Abhidharma: cessation of the dharma-stream | **no self to survive** — anattā means there was no permanent individual to begin with |
>
> These three are mutually inconsistent on metaphysical grounds: Jain mokṣa requires a real, persistent individual soul; Advaita mokṣa requires realising the individual was never real as separate; Buddhist nirvāṇa denies there was ever a substantial self. Using "liberation" as if it names the same thing across traditions is not neutral comparison — it is a substantive metaphysical claim that all three traditions would dispute.

## 11. The complete arc — a summary visual {#arc}

The seven tattvas as a single diagram:

```
SOUL (jīva) — conscious substance, upayoga its mark
  │
  │  ← āsrava (karma influx, via yoga)
  │  ← bandha (karma sticks via kaṣāya)
  │
  ▼
BOUND jīva (saṃsārin) — karma obscures upayoga
  │
  │  guṇasthāna 1–6: all 5 bandha-causes operative
  │  guṇasthāna 7: pramāda removed
  │  guṇasthāna 12: kaṣāya destroyed (irreversible)
  │
  ├── saṃvara (blocking new āsrava) ─────────────────→ no new karma enters
  │
  └── nirjarā (shedding bonded karma via tapas) ──────→ old karma burns off
           │
           ▼ (all ghātīya karmas gone at guṇasthāna 13)
       KEVALA-JÑĀNA (omniscience while still embodied)
```
