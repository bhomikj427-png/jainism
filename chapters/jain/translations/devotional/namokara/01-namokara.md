# Ṇamokāra Mantra (Pañca-Namaskāra) — the Five Homages

**Text:** *Ṇamokāra-mantra* / *Pañca-namaskāra* / *Navkār mantra*
**Recensions used:** Both Digambara and Śvetāmbara recite a modern common form; genuine historical divergences are documented below, not smoothed over.
**Editions/sources used:** Padmanabh S. Jaini, *The Jaina Path of Purification* (1979); Paul Dundas, *The Jains* (2nd ed., 2002); Wikipedia "Namokar Mantra" (for epigraphic dating only); existing concept node `namokara.md`.
**Status:** in-progress (unit #16 in the translation reading-room queue — a devotional unit taken ahead of the numerically-lower-pending TS adhyāyas, per the user's explicit "next batch in devotional" request).

This is the shortest text in the reading room (seven lines total) but, per Dundas and Jaini, carries **more genuine recensional complexity per line than any TS adhyāya drafted so far** — not in verse-numbering (there is only one "verse"), but in **wording**, in **which line five even says**, and in **whether the closing two lines carry scriptural authority at all**. All three are recorded below as real forks, not softened into "both traditions agree."

---

## The mantra — Both recensions (modern common form)

**Recension status:** Both recensions recite this form today; see the DIVERGENT findings below for what is genuinely contested underneath this surface agreement.

**Text — IAST (Digambara-orthography, ṇ-forms, most common printed form today):**

> Ṇamō Arihantāṇaṁ
> Ṇamō Siddhāṇaṁ
> Ṇamō Āyariyāṇaṁ
> Ṇamō Uvajjhāyāṇaṁ
> Ṇamō Lōē Savva-Sāhūṇaṁ
>
> Ēsō Pañca-Ṇamōkkārō, Savva-Pāvappaṇāsaṇō
> Maṅgalāṇaṁ ca Savvēsiṁ, Paḍhamaṁ Havaī Maṅgalaṁ

**Devanāgarī (Digambara-orthography, sourced):** णमो अरिहंताणं । णमो सिद्धाणं । णमो आयरियाणं । णमो उवज्झायाणं । णमो लोए सव्व-साहूणं ॥ एसो पंच-णमोक्कारो, सव्व-पावप्पणासणो । मंगलाणं च सव्वेसिं, पढमं हवइ मंगलं ॥

**Meter:** Not classical Sanskrit meter — Prakrit *gāthā* (the closing couplet is in the Prakrit Āryā-family *gāhā* metre used throughout early Jain canonical verse; the five salutation-lines themselves are prose formulae, not metrical).

## Recension status: DIVERGENT — three separate findings, kept distinct

Per §A of this reading-room's method, these are recorded as three genuinely *different kinds* of divergence, not flattened into one "Dig. vs Śvet." line.

### Fork 1 — the ṇ/n phonetic-orthographic layer (surface, but real)

| aspect | "Digambara" printed form (Maharashtri Prakrit) | Older / historically-prior form (Ardhamāgadhī) |
|---|---|---|
| initial word | *ṇamō* | *namō* |
| line 1 | *ṇamō arihantāṇaṁ* | *namō arahantāṇaṁ* |
| what it commits you to | The form in nearly all popular Digambara publications today. | The form both Jaini (1979) and Dundas (2002) actually transcribe throughout — *"namo arahamtanam"* (Jaini), *"arahat"* (Dundas) — reflecting the older Ardhamāgadhī layer, not the later Digambara Maharashtri-Prakrit *ṇ*-shift. |

Jaini's own footnote is explicit that *arahanta* and *arihanta* are "both Prakrit forms of the Sanskrit *arhat* or *arham*" (Jaini 1979: 163 n.11) — i.e. this is dialectal variation within Prakrit, not a doctrinal fork. But it is a genuine textual-layer difference, and it is why this reading room's own two academic sources (Jaini, Dundas) print a form that disagrees with the Devanāgarī given above. **Open item:** a citable source giving the Śvetāmbara-recited form in Devanāgarī script directly (rather than reconstructed by substituting *n* for *ṇ*) was not found this session — flagged rather than fabricated.

### Fork 2 — the fifth line itself is not stable (the real finding)

The oldest attestations do not agree on how many beings are saluted, or who the fifth is:

| witness | date (per source) | lines invoked |
|---|---|---|
| Pale Caves inscription | c. 200–100 BCE (Wikipedia, citing epigraphic survey) | first line only — *namo arahatanam* |
| Hāthīgumphā inscription (King Khāravela) | c. 1st–2nd c. CE / c. 150 BCE (sources differ on the inscription's own date; not reconciled here) | two lines — *arahaṃta* + *siddha* only |
| **Bhagavatī Sūtra (i.1)** — oldest Śvetāmbara canonical citation of a *fivefold* form | canonical, pre-commentarial | five lines, **but the fifth is *namo bambhīe livīe* — "homage to the Brāhmī script" — not homage to the sādhus** (Jaini 1979: 163 n.12, citing Bhagavatī i.1) |
| Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama opening (Puṣpadanta) | Digambara tradition dates this c. 2nd c. CE; Vīrasena (8th c.) is the source for the claim | five lines in the now-standard form, ending in homage to the sādhus |
| modern common recitation (both traditions) | — | five lines, ending in homage to the sādhus |

This is a stronger claim than a wording variant: **the earliest Śvetāmbara scriptural witness to a five-part salutation does not have the same fifth object of veneration that the mantra has today.** Dundas independently confirms the shape of this finding from the other direction: "The Five Homages was virtually unknown to the Śvetāmbara scriptures. The fifth aṅga [Bhagavatī] does quote it at the beginning but expands it by expressing homage to the alphabet... and to scripture also" (Dundas 2002: 82), and notes commentators on the sixth *upāṅga* do not gloss it at their text's opening at all, "which suggests they regarded its occurrence there as the result of interpolation" (ibid.). Two independent scholars, working from different citation chains (Jaini quotes the Bhagavatī passage directly; Dundas discusses the aṅga/upāṅga commentarial silence), converge on the same conclusion: **the modern sādhu-ending five-line form is better attested as a Digambara-transmitted text (via the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama) than as an original Śvetāmbara one**, even though both traditions now recite it identically. This is recorded as the file's central finding, not softened into "both recensions agree."

### Fork 3 — the closing couplet's authority is itself contested

Dundas: "there exist some minor differences in versions of the text which caused sectarian disagreement amongst the Śvetāmbaras in the late medieval period, and the Sthānakvāsī sect rejects the authority of the second portion [the closing couplet]" (Dundas 2002: 82). This is a live, named sectarian position (Sthānakvāsī, a Śvetāmbara reform lineage), not a historical curiosity — it means the seven-line text presented above as "both recensions" is not, in fact, universally accepted even within Śvetāmbara Jainism today.

## Disambiguation (from commentary)

The mantra's own commentarial tradition addresses a real doctrinal puzzle that a bare reading of the Prakrit does not raise on its own: *why does the very first homage go to the arhat — a being who, by Jain doctrine, still carries the four *aghātiyā* karmas and has not yet fully shed all karmic matter — ahead of the siddha, who has?* The Dhavalā-ṭīkā (Vīrasena's commentary on the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama) poses this exactly and answers it: without the teaching of an existing (embodied) arhat, "knowledge of the true teachings [*āpta-āgama*] would not arise for people like us" (Dhavalā-ṭīkā, quoted in Jaini 1979: 163 n.13 — Prakrit/Sanskrit text given, English paraphrase here is the reading room's own gloss on Jaini's citation, not an independent from-scratch translation). This is the same resolution already recorded, independently, in the existing `namokara.md` concept node (gratitude for the teacher over rank of the taught) — the commentarial source here (Dhavalā) is a different, earlier citation chain than that node's Wikipedia/Jainworld signals, and **converges** with them: a case of the trust method (CLAUDE.md §4) actually closing the loop across a concept-node pass and a translation-room pass done independently.

A second commentarial point, also from Jaini's citation apparatus: the *om* used in later Jain devotionalism is explained (Dravyasaṅgraha-ṭīkā, quoted Jaini 1979: 165 n.16) as an acrostic built from the mantra's own five salutations — *a* (a-rihanta), *a* (a-śarīra, i.e. the bodiless siddha), *a* (ā-yariya), *u* (u-vajjhāya), *m* (m-uni, i.e. sādhu) — sandhi-combining to *o-ṃ*. This is recorded as a **derivative devotional reading**, not a claim that *oṃ* and the mantra are historically identical in origin; it postdates the mantra itself and is presented here strictly as what the later commentarial tradition itself says, sourced and quoted, not endorsed as etymology.

## Assembled reading

*"Homage to the arhats. Homage to the siddhas. Homage to the ācāryas. Homage to the upādhyāyas. Homage to all the sādhus in the world. This five-fold homage, which destroys all sin/evil, is — of all auspicious things — the foremost auspicious thing."*

This reading is grounded strictly in the padaccheda rows and the two independent scholarly translations below; it introduces no gloss beyond what both Jaini and Dundas converge on line-for-line.

## Published translations (comparison only)

- **Jaini (1979: 162–163):** "I bow before the worthy ones [arhat] — the Jinas... I bow before the Perfected beings... who have attained mokṣa... I bow before the [mendicant] leaders [ācārya]... I bow before the [mendicant] preceptors [upādhyāya]... I bow before all the [Jaina] mendicants [sādhu] in the world... This fivefold salutation, which destroys all sin, is preeminent as the most auspicious of all auspicious things."
- **Dundas (2002: 82):** "Homage to the omniscient ones (*arahat*). Homage to the liberated ones (*siddha*). Homage to the teachers (*āyariya*). Homage to the preceptors (*uvajjhāya*). Homage to all monks (*sāhu*) in the world... This is the Five Homages which destroys all evil. It is the first auspicious statement of all auspicious statements."
- **Divergence noted:** Jaini glosses line 1 through the Jina-identity ("the worthy ones [arhat] — the Jinas") and explicitly names *mokṣa* attainment for line 2; Dundas glosses line 1 through the epistemic attribute ("the omniscient ones") and leaves line 2 unglossed beyond "liberated." Different English wording, different explanatory emphasis, same underlying five referents — the kind of divergence this reading room treats as confirming genuine independence rather than one translator copying the other.
- A third, non-academic comparison point (Jainworld / institutional Jain sources, already used in `namokara.md`) renders the closing couplet as "of all that is auspicious, it is the foremost auspicious thing" — consistent with both academic translations; used only as a cross-check, not counted as an independent scholarly source (same treatment given to the "popular paraphrase" sources flagged in the Bhaktāmara unit).
