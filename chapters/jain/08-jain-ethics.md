## How to use this chapter

Chapters 01–02 covered what Jainism says *is* (ontology) and how it *reasons* (epistemology). Chapter 03 laid out the soteriological engine — how karma binds the soul and how it is shed. Chapter 07 covered the second jewel, right knowledge. **This chapter covers the third jewel: right conduct (*cāritra*) — what the doctrine asks you actually to *do*.**

It is the most practical chapter in the book, and it has a single organising idea: in Jainism, **ethics is physics**. The vows are not merely moral rules; they are the operating instructions for a karma-management system. Chapter 03 established that karma is fine matter (*pudgala*) that sticks to the soul through the glue of passion (*kaṣāya*). Everything in this chapter — the five vows, the lay/monk split, austerity, meditation, the daily rite, even the final fast — is an answer to one engineering question: *how do you stop the influx and shed the residue?* Read §4 first if you want the mechanism before the rituals.

The chapter builds: §1 places conduct as the third jewel; §2 recaps the five vows; §3 is the spine — the **two intensities** (monk's *mahāvrata* vs layperson's *aṇuvrata*) and why the difference is one of *scope*, not of two different ethics; §4 gives the karma mechanism that makes any of it matter; §5–6 add the active tools (austerity, meditation, the daily rite); §7 treats the contested last vow, *sallekhanā*.

## 1. Conduct is the third jewel — *cāritra* {#charitra}

*→ concept file: [charitra.md](../../concepts/charitra.md)*

Recall TS 1.1 from Chapters 03 and 07 — the verse that names the whole path, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **samyagdarśanajñānacāritrāṇi mokṣamārgaḥ**
> "Right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, together, constitute the path to liberation."

Three jewels, one path (Ch 07 §1). Chapter 07 unpacked the second jewel, *samyak-jñāna*. **This chapter is the third: *samyak-cāritra*, right conduct** — "disciplined restraint of all censurable movements of speech, body, and mind." But notice the order of dependence Pūjyapāda fixed: conduct depends on knowledge, knowledge on faith. Conduct is not where you *start*; it is where the other two jewels *cash out*. A vow kept without right understanding of why is mere behaviour; right conduct is restraint *informed by* the knowledge of how karma works. This is why the chapter cannot be a bare list of rules — every rule has the §4 mechanism behind it.

Cāritra comes in two grades, and that distinction *is* §3:

| level | Sanskrit | practitioner | vows | scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| complete conduct | *sakala-cāritra* | monks and nuns | five **mahāvratas** in absolute form | total renunciation |
| partial conduct | *vikala-cāritra* | laypeople (*śrāvaka/śrāvikā*) | five **aṇuvratas** adapted to household life | partial restraint |

## 2. The five vows — *the mahāvratas* {#mahavratas}

*→ concept files: [ahimsa.md](../../concepts/ahimsa.md) · [satya.md](../../concepts/satya.md) · [asteya.md](../../concepts/asteya.md) · [brahmacarya.md](../../concepts/brahmacarya.md) · [aparigraha.md](../../concepts/aparigraha.md)*

The entire ethical edifice rests on five abstentions, named in one sūtra. TS 7.1, Sanskrit directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **hiṃsā'nṛtasteyābrahmaparigrahebhyo virativratam**
> "Desisting from injury, falsehood, stealing, unchastity, and attachment-to-possessions is the (fivefold) vow."

| # | vow | Sanskrit | abstention from |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | non-violence | *ahiṃsā* | *hiṃsā* — harm to any living being |
| 2 | truthfulness | *satya* | *anṛta* — falsehood |
| 3 | non-stealing | *asteya* | *steya* — taking what is not given |
| 4 | chastity | *brahmacarya* | *abrahma* — unchastity |
| 5 | non-possession | *aparigraha* | *parigraha* — possessive attachment |

These were each treated in earlier chapters as standalone concepts; here they are presented as the *single integrated vow* TS 7.1 makes them. Two structural facts:

- **Ahiṃsā is the root; the other four are its fence.** As Chapter 04 noted, Jain non-violence is uniquely *total* (it extends to one-sensed beings — see ahimsa.md's six-category scope). Falsehood, theft, unchastity, and possession are all, in the Jain analysis, *occasions for violence*: you lie to harm, you steal by harming, possessiveness breeds the passions that drive harm. The five are not a flat list but a centre (ahiṃsā) with four supports.
- **Each vow carries five "observances."** TS 7.3 adds that each of the five has five *bhāvanā* (stabilising practices) — e.g. aparigraha's five are the renunciation of attachment/aversion across the five senses (TS 7.8, Ch 03/aparigraha.md). The vows are not single acts but trained dispositions.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *ahiṃsā* ≠ Buddhist / Vedic *ahiṃsā***
>
> All three traditions prize "non-violence" and use the same word, so they are read as one ethic at three addresses. They are not (this was Chapter 04's argument; it governs this chapter too). Jain ahiṃsā is *consequence-sensitive and maximally extended*: harm to a one-sensed being (earth, water, fire, air, plant bodies) counts, and even unintentional harm binds karma (though less). Buddhist non-harm is *intention-centred*: the moral weight is in the volition (*cetanā*), and the unintended is largely karmically inert (see [ahimsa-buddhist.md](../../concepts/ahimsa-buddhist.md)). Vedic ahiṃsā is *bounded by ritual duty*: it coexists with sanctioned sacrificial killing (see [ahimsa-vedic.md](../../concepts/ahimsa-vedic.md)). The vow that opens this chapter is the *Jain* one — the most demanding of the three, because it is built on the jīva-doctrine that there are souls in the soil.

## 3. The spine: two intensities, one ethic — *mahāvrata and aṇuvrata* {#anuvrata}

*→ concept file: [anuvrata.md](../../concepts/anuvrata.md)*

This is the chapter's central move and the most frequently misunderstood point in Jain ethics. The five vows come in **two intensities** — and the difference is *degree of observance*, not *two different moralities*.

### 3.1 TS 7.2 — partial and total {#anuvrata-verse}

TS 7.2, English fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018; the Sanskrit not independently verified this run):

> "The vow is of two kinds, small and great (*aṇu / mahā*), from its being partial (*deśa*) and total (*sarva*)."

The *same five abstentions* become:

| | who takes it | how | name |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mahāvrata** (great vow) | monks and nuns | *total* renunciation (*sarva-virati*) — no exceptions, no gradations | "great" |
| **aṇuvrata** (small vow) | laypeople | *partial* renunciation (*deśa-virati*) — abstain from the *gross* forms while living in the world | "small / atomic" |

The contrast, vow by vow:

| vow | mahāvrata (total) | aṇuvrata (partial) |
|---|---|---|
| ahiṃsā | harm *no* being, including one-sensed | abstain from *gross, intentional* violence (esp. to mobile beings) |
| satya | absolute truth | abstain from *ruinous* falsehood |
| asteya | take nothing not given | abstain from *gross* theft |
| brahmacarya | complete celibacy | *svadāra-santoṣa* — contentment with one's spouse |
| aparigraha | total non-possession | *parimāṇa* — a self-set ceiling on possessions |

> **Read this carefully — it is the part people get wrong.** The aṇuvrata is *not a watered-down second-class ethic for people who can't manage the real thing*. It is the **same vow, taken to the depth a householder's life permits**. The monk and the layperson are on one ladder, not two. The whole genius of the scheme is that it is *graduated*: a layperson is meant to *tighten* the radius of the aṇuvratas over a lifetime, approaching the mahāvrata without pretending to have already reached it. Jainism's refusal to demand the impossible of householders — while never lowering the *standard* the householder is climbing toward — is exactly what let a renunciant religion sustain a lay community for two and a half millennia.

### 3.2 The twelve vows of the layperson {#twelve-vows}

The five aṇuvratas are the core of a *twelve-vow* programme for the committed layperson — the other seven progressively shrink the sphere within which the five operate:

| group | Sanskrit | members | function |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 lesser vows | *aṇuvrata* | the five above | the core abstentions, partial form |
| 3 merit vows | *guṇavrata* | *digvrata* (limit travel by direction) · *bhogopabhoga-parimāṇa* (limit goods consumed) · *anartha-daṇḍa-viramaṇa* (avoid purposeless harm / harmful trades) | shrink the *spatial and material* field of action |
| 4 disciplinary vows | *śikṣāvrata* | *sāmāyika* (periodic equanimity) · *deśāvakāśika* (limit movement to a set area) · *proṣadhopavāsa* (periodic fasting) · *atithi-saṃvibhāga* (feeding ascetics / the needy) | add *temporal* disciplines — recurring practices |

Note that **sāmāyika appears here as a vow** *and* will reappear in §6 as the first of the six daily rites: the practice and the vow are the same act seen from two angles. The twelve vows are not twelve different things to abstain from — they are five abstentions plus seven tighteners of *when, where, and how much*.

## 4. Why vows work: ethics as karma-engineering — *kaṣāya, saṃvara, nirjarā* {#mechanism}

*→ concept files: [kashaya.md](../../concepts/kashaya.md) · [samvara.md](../../concepts/samvara.md) · [nirjara.md](../../concepts/nirjara.md)*

Here is the mechanism that makes the vows more than etiquette. Recall the soteriological chain from Chapter 03: **āsrava** (karma flows in) → **bandha** (it binds) → **saṃvara** (the inflow is stopped) → **nirjarā** (the residue is shed) → **mokṣa**. The vows act at two precise points in that chain.

### 4.1 The glue: *kaṣāya* {#kashaya}

Why does karma stick at all? Because of the passions. TS 6.5, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018), names them in the karmic-influx chapter:

> The passions (*kaṣāya*) — anger (*krodha*), pride (*māna*), deceit (*māyā*), greed (*lobha*) — are the moistening agent that makes karmic matter adhere to the soul.

Without passion, karmic matter would touch the soul and fall away dry. *Kaṣāya is the glue.* And every one of the five vows targets a passion: violence springs from anger/pride, falsehood and theft from greed/deceit, possessiveness *is* greed crystallised. **To keep a vow is to drain a passion** — and to drain a passion is to dry the glue.

### 4.2 The two effects: stopping and shedding {#samvara-nirjara}

So the vows do two things at once:

| effect | Sanskrit | what the vow does |
|---|---|---|
| stopping new influx | *saṃvara* | a kept vow withholds the passion that would have drawn fresh karma in — it closes a channel |
| shedding old residue | *nirjarā* | sustained restraint (and the austerities of §5) actively burns off karma already bound |

This is why ahimsa.md, aparigraha.md, and the rest all carry `part-of: samvara` links. Right conduct *is* saṃvara-in-practice. The vows are not how you become a *good person* in some free-floating sense; they are how you **stop feeding the karma that keeps you embodied**. Ethics, in Jainism, is the applied engineering of liberation.

## 5. The active tools: austerity and meditation — *tapas and dhyāna* {#tapas-dhyana}

*→ concept files: [tapas.md](../../concepts/tapas.md) · [dhyana-jain.md](../../concepts/dhyana-jain.md)*

The vows *stop* the inflow (saṃvara). To actively *shed* the backlog (nirjarā) takes more than abstention — it takes austerity.

### 5.1 Austerity — *tapas* {#tapas}

TS 9.19, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018), lists the six *external* austerities:

> **anaśanāvamaudarya vṛttiparisaṃkhyāna rasaparityāga viviktaśayyāsana kāyakleśā bāhyaṃ tapaḥ**
> "The six external austerities: fasting (*anaśana*), reduced diet (*avamaudarya*), restriction of begging (*vṛtti-parisaṃkhyāna*), giving up delicacies (*rasa-parityāga*), solitary lodging (*vivikta-śayyāsana*), and bodily mortification (*kāya-kleśa*)."

There are also six *internal* austerities (TS 9.20: confession, reverence, service, study, renunciation of ego, meditation). Tapas is the corpus's clearest case of *ethics-as-physics*: the word *tap* means "to burn," and in Jainism it is meant **literally** — austerity *burns off* the fine pudgala already stuck to the soul. This is the engine of *avipāka-nirjarā* (deliberate karma-eradication, as opposed to karma simply expiring on its own schedule).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *tapas* ≠ Hindu *tapas***
>
> The same word, the same root *tap* ("to heat/burn"), and both name ascetic discipline — so they are read as one practice. The *metaphysics is opposite*. In the Hindu/Vedic stream, tapas generates *heat as spiritual power* — it builds up energy, "cooks" the practitioner, can be hoarded and even spent (the sage whose accumulated tapas grants boons or curses). In Jainism, tapas does not *accumulate* anything; it **removes** — it burns *away* the material karma clinging to the soul, subtracting rather than charging up. Hindu tapas fills; Jain tapas empties. Mapping the "spiritual energy" sense onto the Jain vow imports a power-generation model into a system whose entire point is *de*-accretion. See [tapas.md](../../concepts/tapas.md). (The Buddhist Middle Way, by contrast, *rejected* extreme mortification altogether — a third position.)

### 5.2 Meditation — *dhyāna* {#dhyana}

Internal austerity culminates in meditation, and Jain meditation has a sharp moral sorting. TS 9.28, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **ārtaraudradharmyaśuklāni**
> "The four meditations are: sorrowful (*ārta*), cruel (*raudra*), virtuous (*dharmya*), and pure (*śukla*)."

The crucial fact: **not all meditation is good meditation.** The first two *bind* karma; only the latter two shed it.

| type | character | karmic effect |
|---|---|---|
| *ārta* | brooding over loss / craving | generates *pāpa* — binds |
| *raudra* | fixation on harming, cheating | generates *pāpa* — binds deeply |
| *dharmya* | contemplating Jina's teaching, karma, the cosmos | destroys karma — enables nirjarā |
| *śukla* | meditation on the soul's pure nature | total karma-annihilation → mokṣa |

This is a striking contrast with the meditative traditions that treat *any* concentration as progress. For Jainism, an absorbed, single-pointed mind fixed on revenge (*raudra-dhyāna*) is not neutral and not a "misuse of a good tool" — it is one of the four meditations, and it is *actively binding karma*. Concentration is morally loaded by its *object*. The śukla-dhyāna of the final rungs is the same meditative act that, in Chapter 07's terms, accompanies the destruction of the ghātīya karmas at guṇasthāna 13 — meditation and omniscience meeting at the top of the ladder.

## 6. The daily rite — *ṣaḍāvaśyaka* {#shad-avashyaka}

*→ concept file: [shad-avashyaka.md](../../concepts/shad-avashyaka.md)*

Vows are standing commitments; austerity and meditation are intensive efforts. Between them sits the **daily practice layer**: six obligatory observances (*āvaśyaka*, "what must be done") that keep the whole system running on a daily clock.

| # | Sanskrit | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *sāmāyika* | ~48 minutes (one *muhūrta*) of stable-posture equanimity — "I abandon all bad activity … in mind, body and speech" |
| 2 | *caturviṃśati-stava* | praise of the twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras |
| 3 | *vandana* | homage to one's ascetic teachers |
| 4 | *pratikramaṇa* | repentance — confessing and renouncing the day's lapses |
| 5 | *kāyotsarga* | "abandoning the body" — motionless bodily detachment |
| 6 | *pratyākhyāna* | formal resolve of future abstention |

The sequence is a daily audit loop: **settle the mind (1) → orient it to the ideal (2–3) → check the day's conduct against that ideal (4) → detach from the body that recorded the lapses (5) → re-bind the future (6).** It is §4's karma-mechanism in miniature, performed every day: pratikramaṇa sheds (nirjarā) what slipped through; pratyākhyāna closes the channel again (saṃvara). The grandest form of pratikramaṇa is the annual *Saṃvatsarī*, the climax of the Paryuṣaṇa festival, when Jains seek pardon from every being — *Micchāmi Dukkaḍaṃ*, "may my faults be without effect."

> Two of these six double as things you've already met: **sāmāyika** is also one of the twelve lay vows (§3.2), and its stable-posture equanimity overlaps the *dharma-dhyāna* of §5.2. The six āvaśyaka are not a separate sixth system bolted on — they are the vows, austerity, and meditation *scheduled into a day*. (Note: the Digambara tradition also calls a *different* list — worship, teacher-reverence, study, restraint, austerity, charity — "the six essentials" for laypeople; shad-avashyaka.md maps both. Don't conflate the two "sixes.")

## 7. The last vow — *sallekhanā* {#sallekhana}

*→ concept file: [sallekhana.md](../../concepts/sallekhana.md)*

The chapter on the layperson's vows ends, in the Tattvārtha Sūtra itself, with a vow taken *once*, at the very end of life. TS 7.22, Sanskrit + English directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **māraṇāntikī sallekhanāṃ joṣitā**
> "The householder adopts, with a sense of contentment (*joṣitā*), the practice of dispassionately abandoning his body — *sallekhanā* — at the end of his life (*māraṇāntikī*)."

Sallekhanā (*sat* "well" + *lekhanā* "scouring-out") is the **voluntary, gradual emaciation of body and passions as death approaches** — a fully-conscious withdrawal from food and drink, undertaken only when death is already certain and near, so that one departs the body in equanimity rather than in attachment or fear. It is the logical terminus of everything in this chapter: the final, total act of tapas (§5), the last pratikramaṇa, the ultimate aparigraha — non-possession extended even to the body itself. The qualifier *joṣitā* ("with contentment") is load-bearing: the rite is valid *only* in calm detachment, never in despair or the wish to die.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — *sallekhanā* ≠ suicide (*ātma-ghāta*)**
>
> This is the most consequential conflation in the chapter, and it has been litigated in court. The surface fact — a person deliberately ceasing to eat until they die — reads as self-killing. The Jain tradition holds the two are categorically *different acts*, distinguished on every axis that matters:
>
> | | suicide (*ātma-ghāta*) | sallekhanā |
> |---|---|---|
> | driving state | passion — grief, fear, anger (*kaṣāya*) | passionless equanimity (*joṣitā*) |
> | means | violent, sudden (poison, weapon) | gradual, no instrument |
> | timing | flees a *bearable* life | only when death is *already* imminent |
> | reversibility | irreversible by design | may be set aside if the condition lifts |
> | oversight | private, concealed | public vow, ascetic counsel, witnessed |
> | aim | to *escape* | to *not flee* — meet death without attachment |
>
> The dispute is genuinely live, not merely doctrinal. In *Nikhil Soni v. Union of India* (2015) the Rajasthan High Court ruled sallekhanā punishable as abetment of suicide; within months the Supreme Court of India *stayed* the ban, treating it as protected religious practice. **The disagreement is the finding** (sallekhana.md presents it as a divergence table, not a settled gloss): the whole question is whether calm, gradual, witnessed self-emaciation in the face of certain death is a *different kind of act* from suicide, or only a gentler manner of the same act. Jainism stakes its answer on the claim that *intent and passion change the kind* — exactly the consequence-vs-intention axis that runs through this whole tradition.

## 8. The whole apparatus — a summary visual {#apparatus}

How right conduct (the third jewel) is built, and where each piece acts on the karma chain:

```
RATNATRAYA (TS 1.1) — the one path
  ├── samyak-darśana   (right faith — Ch 03)
  ├── samyak-jñāna     (right knowledge — Ch 07)
  └── samyak-CĀRITRA   (right conduct — THIS CHAPTER)
        │
   THE FIVE VOWS (TS 7.1): ahiṃsā · satya · asteya · brahmacarya · aparigraha
        │   each at two intensities (TS 7.2):
        │     MAHĀVRATA  (total)   → monks/nuns      = sakala-cāritra
        │     AṆUVRATA   (partial) → laypeople       = vikala-cāritra
        │        └─ +3 guṇavrata +4 śikṣāvrata = the 12 lay vows
        ▼
   THE MECHANISM (Ch 03):  passion (KAṢĀYA, TS 6.5) is the glue
        every kept vow drains a passion →
            ├── SAṂVARA  : stops new karmic influx   (closes the channel)
            └── NIRJARĀ  : sheds karma already bound  (drains the residue)
        ▲
   THE ACTIVE TOOLS:
        TAPAS (TS 9.19) — 6 external + 6 internal austerities; literally BURNS off karma
        DHYĀNA (TS 9.28) — 4 meditations; ārta/raudra BIND, dharmya/śukla SHED
        ŚAḌĀVAŚYAKA — the daily loop: sāmāyika→stava→vandana→pratikramaṇa→kāyotsarga→pratyākhyāna
        ▼
   SALLEKHANĀ (TS 7.22) — the terminal vow: tapas + aparigraha carried to death, in equanimity
                          (≠ suicide — the contested edge)
```

The shape to carry away: **every item in Jain ethics is a move in a karma economy.** Abstain (vows) to stop the inflow; burn (tapas) and audit (the daily rite) to shed the residue; and at the end, when the body can do no more, give up even the body — calmly. Nothing here is a free-floating "value." It is all one system for getting the soul un-stuck.

## 9. Check yourself {#check}

**9.1** A monk takes the mahāvrata of ahiṃsā; a layperson takes the aṇuvrata of ahiṃsā. Is the layperson following a *different, lesser ethic*, or *the same ethic at a different intensity*? Defend your answer using the "one ladder" idea — and explain why the twelve-vow scheme (the guṇavratas and śikṣāvratas) is evidence for it.

**9.2** Jain *tapas* and Hindu *tapas* share a word and a root meaning ("to burn"). Yet one *fills* and the other *empties*. Explain what each is supposed to burn and what the burning produces — and why importing the Hindu "spiritual energy" sense into Jain austerity gets the whole system backwards. (Hint: ask what tapas is meant to *do to the karma already stuck to the soul*.)

**9.3** Someone argues: "Sallekhanā is obviously suicide — the person intends to stop eating and die; the calm manner is irrelevant." A Jain replies that intent and passion change the *kind* of act. Lay out three of the six axes on which the tradition distinguishes the two, and then say precisely where the disagreement actually lies — is it about the *facts* of what happens, or about *what makes two acts the same kind*?
