## 1. The problem: why Jainism needs its own theory of knowledge {#problem}

*→ concept files: [naya.md](../../concepts/naya.md) · [anekantavada.md](../../concepts/anekantavada.md)*

Chapter 01 established that every substance (dravya) simultaneously has permanent qualities (guṇa) and changing modes (paryāya). This creates an immediate problem for knowledge: **any single description of a substance is going to capture only one of its aspects.**

If I say "the clay is one" — I'm attending to the substance (guṇa/dravya side). If I say "the clay is many" — I'm attending to the modes (different shapes it takes on). Both are true. But they seem to contradict each other. So are they? And if they aren't, how do I assert truths about anything without either lying or falling into endless qualification?

The Jain answer has three layers:

1. **Naya** — the theory of standpoints: finite cognition always grasps *one aspect* of a substance at a time. That's not a defect; it's how finite knowing works. But each partial grasp is valid, if it doesn't claim to be the whole.

2. **Anekāntavāda** — the doctrine of many-sidedness: reality genuinely has all those aspects simultaneously. The appearances of contradiction are not real contradictions; they arise from confusing aspect-claims with whole-claims.

3. **Syādvāda + saptabhaṅgī** — the predication machinery: the disciplined method of prefixing every assertion with a standpoint-qualifier ("in some respect…") so that it's always clear what aspect you're asserting.

These three are a single system. Naya is the ontological structure; anekāntavāda is the philosophical principle; syādvāda/saptabhaṅgī is the linguistic method.

## 2. Standpoints — *naya* {#naya}

*→ concept file: [naya.md](../../concepts/naya.md)*

### 2.1 Two independent definitions {#naya-defs}

The Tattvārtha Sūtra uses *naya* in TS 1.6 (*pramāṇanayaiḥ* — "by means of valid cognitions and standpoints"), identifying it as one of the two instruments of knowledge. The concept is fully defined in the commentarial tradition.

Two commentators, five centuries apart, give independent definitions that converge:

| source | definition |
|---|---|
| **Pūjyapāda**, *Sarvārthasiddhi*, Digambara, ~5th–6th c. (via Vijay K. Jain 2018): | "Ascertainment, without contradiction, of one particular state or mode of the object, with a view to describe truly the substance having infinite attributes." |
| **Prabhācandra**, *Prameyakamalamārtaṇḍa*, Digambara, ~10th–11th c. (via Deepa Baruah 2017): | "The apprehension of the knower of a part of an object without discarding its opposite views." |

The key phrase in both is the restraint clause: a naya is valid **only while it does not deny** the aspects it isn't attending to. The moment a standpoint claims to be the complete truth and rejects all others, it becomes a **nayābhāsa** — a distorted/false standpoint.

The nayābhāsa is the failure mode. A Buddhist philosopher who takes momentariness as the complete truth about reality is, in Jain terms, elevating one valid naya (ṛjusūtra — see §3.2) into a nayābhāsa by refusing to pair it with the substance-aspect nayas.

### 2.2 Naya vs pramāṇa (complete knowledge) {#naya-pramana}

TS 1.6: *pramāṇanayaiḥ* — "by means of valid cognitions (*pramāṇa*) and standpoints (*naya*)." These are not the same thing:

| | pramāṇa | naya |
|---|---|---|
| What it covers | the whole object, all aspects | one aspect of one object |
| Who uses it | only a being with omniscience (kevala-jñāna) | all finite knowers |
| Output | complete, fully adequate knowledge | partial but genuine knowledge |

Finite knowers — everyone except a liberated Jina — cannot use pramāṇa directly. We use nayas. The point of the naya system is not to resign us to ignorance but to give partial knowledge a rigorous structure. Nayas are not guesses; they are disciplined partial apprehensions.

## 3. The seven nayas (TS 1.33) {#seven-nayas}

*→ concept files: [dravyarthika-naya.md](../../concepts/dravyarthika-naya.md) · [paryayarthika-naya.md](../../concepts/paryayarthika-naya.md)*

TS 1.33 (Vijay K. Jain 2018, directly fetched) names the seven nayas:

> **naigamasaṃgrahavyavahārarjusūtraśabdasamabhirūḍvaivaṃbhūtā nayāḥ**

They split into two groups based on which aspect of sat (Ch 01 §1) they foreground:

| group | nayas | attends to | maps onto sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dravyārthika** (substance-standpoint) | naigama · saṃgraha · vyavahāra | guṇas, class membership, what persists | dhrauvya (permanence) side |
| **Paryāyārthika** (mode-standpoint) | ṛjusūtra · śabda · samabhirūḍha · evaṃbhūta | specific modes, present moment, fine distinctions | utpāda/vyaya (change) side |

### 3.1 Dravyārthika nayas — the substance-side {#dravyarthika}

These three nayas attend to what is permanent and general about a substance:

| naya | Sanskrit | key move | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Figurative/intentional | naigama | sees the whole substance-with-its-purpose; treats what is in progress as already done | "I am cooking" — said while only gathering ingredients; the end-state is the naigama object |
| Generic/class | saṃgraha | treats all members of a class as one under a shared essence | pointing to a pot and saying "this is clay" — attending to material, ignoring shape |
| Practical/particular | vyavahāra | differentiates within the class; practical distinctions | "this is a pot, that is a cup" — discriminating within the class of clay-things |

**Structure:** saṃgraha is maximum abstraction (all is one class), vyavahāra unpacks the class into practical distinctions, naigama is teleological. None of these is the "right" view — each captures a real dimension of the substance's being.

### 3.2 Paryāyārthika nayas — the mode-side {#paryayarthika}

These four nayas attend to specific, momentary, fine-grained aspects:

| naya | Sanskrit | key move | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight/momentary | ṛjusūtra | attends ONLY to the object's present mode, discarding past and future | "the jar right now" — not "the clay that was a lump yesterday" |
| Verbal/synonymous | śabda | treats synonyms as having identical meaning regardless of derivation | *kumbha*, *kalaśa*, *ghaṭa* (three words for "jar") are interchangeable |
| Etymological | samabhirūḍha | treats synonyms as expressing different aspects via their etymological roots | *kumbha* (root: swell) ≠ *ghaṭa* (root: unite/close) — same jar, different connotation |
| Functional/specialised | evaṃbhūta | a term applies ONLY when the object is actively performing what the term denotes | "cow" (*go*, from root "to go") applies strictly while the animal is moving |

**The progressive narrowing:** The series runs from most general (ṛjusūtra: present moment, whole object) to most specific (evaṃbhūta: this word, right now, in this act). Evaṃbhūta is the most fine-grained of all seven nayas.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — ṛjusūtra ≠ Buddhist momentariness (Abhidharma)**
>
> Ṛjusūtra focuses only on the object's present mode, ignoring past and future. Buddhist Abhidharma momentariness holds that dharmas (ultimate constituents) exist for only one moment. The surface similarity is real: both doctrines isolate the present moment.
>
> The difference is the status of the claim. For Jains, ṛjusūtra is one valid partial standpoint among seven. Taking it as the complete truth — denying substance and permanence — turns it into a nayābhāsa. Buddhist Abhidharma presents momentariness as the *ultimate* analysis of reality, not a partial standpoint. From the Jain view, Buddhist Abhidharma is exactly the nayābhāsa that elevates ṛjusūtra to the whole truth and thereby misses the dravyārthika half of reality.

## 4. Many-sidedness — *anekāntavāda* {#anekantavada}

*→ concept file: [anekantavada.md](../../concepts/anekantavada.md)*

### 4.1 Status: CONTESTED {#anekantavada-status}

**This is one of three contested concepts in the corpus.** The divergence among scholars is itself the finding; there is no single "correct" reading to give you. The table below is the finding.

**Philological caution first:** The term *anekāntavāda* does not appear in the earliest Śvetāmbara canonical texts. The Tattvārtha Sūtra provides the naya foundation (TS 1.6, 1.33) on which the doctrine is built, but the named doctrine is a post-TS development: Samantabhadra (*Āptamīmāṃsā*, ~2nd c. CE), Siddhasena Divākar (~5th c., credited with coining the term), Mallavādin (~5th–6th c.), Akalanka (~8th c.).

### 4.2 The divergence table {#anekantavada-divergence}

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-one-sidedness** (negative/polemical): the doctrine *rejects* absolutist single-perspective claims; what it affirms beyond that is implicit | Paul Dundas, *The Jains* (2002): "a fearsome weapon of philosophical polemic" | Anekāntavāda is primarily a dialectical tool, not a positive metaphysical claim |
| **Many-sidedness** (positive/ontological): reality is genuinely multifaceted; the doctrine claims substances have simultaneous apparently-contradictory attributes | Most popular presentations; Charitrapragya; general Jain tradition | Substances (dravya) genuinely are both permanent and impermanent, one and many — not paradox but full truth |
| **Epistemological locus**: the doctrine concerns the limits of finite knowledge; no finite standpoint grasps the whole; only omniscience (kevala-jñāna) can | Anil Mundra (U. Alabama, 2022); John Koller; Matilal: "syādvāda is not a theory of uncertainty" | The doctrine is about human cognitive limitation, not about metaphysical indeterminacy |
| **Methodological/hermeneutical locus**: a systematic framework for incorporating partial perspectives in philosophical argument | Samantabhadra *Āptamīmāṃsā* v. 105 (via Mundra): syādvāda "illuminates substances" indirectly | Anekāntavāda is a philosophical procedure, not a doctrine about ultimate reality's structure |
| **Tolerance/pluralism/intellectual ahiṃsā** (modern reading) | Matilal (following Dhruva, Kapadia): "intellectual ahiṃsā" | Ethics of epistemic tolerance; widely criticised as importing modern liberal values into an ancient polemical tradition |

### 4.3 The one point of scholarly consensus {#anekantavada-consensus}

Across all these readings, there is one firm point: **syāt does NOT mean "maybe" or "perhaps."**

Early Western translators read *syāt* as the Sanskrit optative ("perchance, may be") and concluded that anekāntavāda is a form of skepticism — no proposition can be asserted unequivocally. This reading was rejected by Matilal, Koller, and virtually all subsequent scholars. In Jain technical usage, *syāt* is a **standpoint-relativiser**: "in some respect / from a defined standpoint." The assertion is definite within its specified naya; it is not indefinite.

The skeptical reading would make Jainism unable to assert anything — including that it has omniscience (kevala-jñāna). The standpoint-relative reading keeps that coherent.

## 5. Conditional predication — *syādvāda* {#syadvada}

*→ concept file: [syadvada.md](../../concepts/syadvada.md)*

### 5.1 What it is {#syadvada-def}

Syādvāda is the **method of qualified assertion**: every philosophical claim about an object is prefixed with *syāt* — "in some respect / from a certain standpoint." It is the linguistic-logical arm of anekāntavāda.

Where anekāntavāda says "reality is many-sided," syādvāda is the disciplined procedure for talking about *one* side at a time without pretending it's the whole. It is naya made into a linguistic practice.

Deepa Baruah (2017, citing *Syādvādamañjarī* and Prabhācandra): "When an object which possesses many characters is expressed in a particular form of judgment [prefixed with *syāt*], the expression is known as syādvāda."

**Samantabhadra, *Āptamīmāṃsā* v. 105** (reported via scholarly aggregation; text not directly fetched): "Syādvāda and kevala-jñāna are both illuminators of substances — syādvāda indirectly [through conditioned predication], kevala-jñāna directly [through omniscience]." Syādvāda is the finite knower's best available substitute for omniscience.

**Textual note:** Like *anekāntavāda*, the term *syādvāda* as a named doctrine does not appear in the TS itself. TS 1.6 uses *pramāṇanayaiḥ*, which is the foundation it systematises. The named, formalised doctrine is attributed to Samantabhadra and later commentators.

### 5.2 Śaṅkara's charge and the Jain response {#syadvada-shankara}

Śaṅkara (Advaita Vedānta, ~8th c.) charged that syādvāda collapses into relativism: if every assertion is only "in some respect," then no definite claim can be made. The doctrine undermines itself.

Jain response (via New World Encyclopedia): Each syāt-qualified assertion is **definite within its specified standpoint**. The system is perspectival, not probabilistic. "The jar is, in some respect, one" is a definite claim about the substance-aspect of the jar. It is no more uncertain than "the jar is, in some respect, many" (attending to its modes). Together they constitute a richer, not weaker, account. A **nayābhāsa** — a standpoint that claims exclusivity — is explicitly invalid; the valid naya is characterised precisely by its restraint from over-claiming.

## 6. Sevenfold predication — *saptabhaṅgī* {#saptabhangi}

*→ concept file: [saptabhangi.md](../../concepts/saptabhangi.md)*

### 6.1 The seven predications {#seven-predications}

Saptabhaṅgī is the **formal logical completion** of syādvāda: the complete enumeration of all canonical ways a syāt-qualified assertion can be made about any subject-predicate pair.

**Authorship note:** Saptabhaṅgī is **not in the Tattvārtha Sūtra**. It was first systematised by **Mallavādin** (5th–6th c. CE, Śvetāmbara); the key text is Mallisena's *Syādvādamañjarī* (1292 CE). The TS provides the naya foundation; saptabhaṅgī is a subsequent formalisation. (Wikipedia citing Matilal, Dundas, Ganeri.)

Three primitives: **asti** (is), **nāsti** (is not), **avaktavya** (inexpressible). All seven predications are combinations of these three, each prefixed with *syāt*:

| # | Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *syād asti* | in some respect, it is |
| 2 | *syān nāsti* | in some respect, it is not |
| 3 | *syād asti nāsti* | in some respect, it both is and is not (sequentially — from two standpoints) |
| 4 | *syād avaktavyaḥ* | in some respect, it is inexpressible |
| 5 | *syād asti avaktavyaḥ* | in some respect, it is, and is inexpressible |
| 6 | *syān nāsti avaktavyaḥ* | in some respect, it is not, and is inexpressible |
| 7 | *syād asti nāsti avaktavyaḥ* | in some respect, it is, is not, and is inexpressible |

### 6.2 Why *avaktavya* (inexpressibility) is not ignorance {#avaktavya}

Predications 1 and 2 assert *asti* and *nāsti* from two different standpoints, in sequence. Predication 3 holds them together but still sequentially — "from standpoint A it is, from standpoint B it is not."

Predication 4 (*avaktavya*) arises when *asti* and *nāsti* must be asserted **simultaneously**, not in sequence. Language, which is linear, cannot express "it is and it is not" at the same moment — you have to say one thing at a time. Inexpressibility is not ignorance or vagueness; it is a **structural feature of finite language** when confronted with genuinely simultaneous complex attributes.

Predications 5–7 are combinations: "it is, and [when I try to hold both at once] it is inexpressible," and so on.

### 6.3 The Buddhist critique {#saptabhangi-buddhist}

Dignāga and Dharmakīrti (Buddhist logicians) charged that predications 3 and 7 violate the **law of non-contradiction**: you cannot assert both A and not-A of the same thing.

Jain response (via Wikipedia, citing Matilal): *asti* and *nāsti* in predication 3 are **relativised to different standpoints** — not asserted simultaneously of the same object under the same description. "The jar is [from the dravyārthika standpoint]" and "the jar is not [from the paryāyārthika standpoint, attending to a mode it has ceased to have]" do not violate non-contradiction — they are assertions under different parameters. Non-contradiction is not violated; it is parametrised.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — saptabhaṅgī ≠ Łukasiewicz three-valued logic**
>
> Łukasiewicz (1920) proposed a three-valued logic with truth values: True, False, and Indeterminate (used for future contingents). The surface parallel to saptabhaṅgī is the three primitives: asti / nāsti / avaktavya ≈ T / F / Indeterminate?
>
> **Why the parallel fails:** Saptabhaṅgī is a *qualified-assertion scheme*, not a *truth-value algebra*. The seven predications are not seven truth-values; they are seven **modes of standpoint-qualified assertion**. Łukasiewicz's indeterminate value addresses temporal contingency (a future event is neither true nor false yet). Avaktavya addresses the structural limit of linear language when expressing simultaneous attributes — that is a different problem entirely. The mathematical apparatus of many-valued logic does not transfer. Wikipedia's own source note: "some researchers caution against reinterpreting it" as many-valued logic.
>

## 7. The prime conflation: *anekāntavāda* ≠ quantum complementarity {#quantum}

*→ concept file: [quantum-complementarity.md](../../concepts/quantum-complementarity.md) · [anekantavada.md](../../concepts/anekantavada.md)*

This is the prime-directive conflict this corpus was built to address. It deserves its own section.

**The analogy** (D.S. Kothari, "The Complementarity Principle and Eastern Philosophy," *Niels Bohr Centenary Volume*, Harvard UP): Bohr's complementarity says that certain quantum properties (particle/wave nature) cannot be measured simultaneously — the experimental setup for one precludes the setup for the other. Kothari drew a parallel to anekāntavāda's claim that substances are "both and" — permanent and impermanent, one and many.

**Why the parallel fails — three precise differences:**

| | Jain anekāntavāda | quantum complementarity |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | logic and metaphysics (a priori reasoning about substances) | experimental physics (empirical measurement outcomes) |
| The "and" | both aspects exist simultaneously and can be **rationally asserted** from different standpoints | the two properties **cannot be measured simultaneously** — measurement of one destroys the experimental context for the other |
| The excluded middle | Jain syāt-assertions are definite within their standpoints; there is no measurement exclusivity | Heisenberg uncertainty and measurement-context exclusivity are **operational** constraints, not standpoint-relative assertions |

The surface similarity is that both doctrines involve "holding two apparently contradictory things together." The mechanisms are entirely different: Jain anekāntavāda holds them together by parametrising to standpoints (both are rationally assertible, just from different nayas). Quantum complementarity excludes them experimentally (you can only observe one, not both, in any given experiment).

Mapping quantum complementarity onto anekāntavāda silently imports an empirical/physical constraint into an a priori/philosophical system, which violates the prime directive of this corpus.
