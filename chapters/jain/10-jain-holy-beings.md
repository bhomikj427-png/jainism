## How to use this chapter

Chapters 01–09 built the Jain *system* — its ontology, epistemology, soteriology, ethics, knowledge-theory, and cosmos. This chapter is the **human and historical layer**: *who* Jains actually bow to, *which texts* carry the teaching, *how the community split in two*, and *where Jainism sits* among the renouncer movements of ancient India. It is the chapter that turns a philosophy into a religion with saints, scriptures, sects, and a timeline.

It runs in five movements. §1 gives the **objects of reverence** — the five supreme beings of the Ṇamokāra mantra — and makes the chapter's first big point: *Jain veneration is not worship of gods*. §2 gives the **historical founders**, Pārśva and Mahāvīra, and the second big point: *Mahāvīra did not found Jainism, he reformed it*. §3 gives the **great schism** — Digambara vs. Śvetāmbara — and shows all five of their differences radiating from a single disagreement about non-possession. §4 gives the **texts and the two truths** — Kundakunda, the Samayasāra, and the niścaya/vyavāhara device — including this chapter's prime-directive moment. §5 closes the karma machinery with **merit, demerit, and the eight karma-natures**. §6 places Jainism **among the śramaṇas** by contrast with the fatalist Ājīvika.

A thread runs through the whole chapter, worth holding from the start: **Jainism is non-theistic, and its history is a history of *human* effort, not divine intervention.** The beings it reveres are perfected *people*; its founders are *reformers*, not creators; its liberation is *earned*, not granted. Watch how every section turns on this.

## 1. Who Jains bow to — the five supreme beings — *Ṇamokāra mantra* {#namokara}

*→ concept file: [namokara.md](../../concepts/namokara.md)*

The most universal Jain prayer — recited by every sect — is the **Ṇamokāra** (or Namaskāra / Navkār) mantra. It is a five-fold salutation followed by two closing lines:

> **Ṇamo Arihantāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *arihants* (omniscient, still embodied)
> **Ṇamo Siddhāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *siddhas* (fully liberated, bodiless)
> **Ṇamo Āyariyāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *ācāryas* (heads of the order)
> **Ṇamo Uvajjhāyāṇaṃ** — I bow to the *upādhyāyas* (preceptors)
> **Ṇamo Loe Savva-sāhūṇaṃ** — I bow to all the *sādhus* (ascetics) in the world
>
> **Eso pañca-ṇamokkāro, savva-pāvappaṇāsaṇo / maṅgalāṇaṃ ca savvesiṃ, paḍhamaṃ havaī maṅgalaṃ**
> "This five-fold salutation destroys all sins; of all that is auspicious, it is the foremost."

These five are the **pañca-parameṣṭhī** (five supreme beings). The single most important fact about the mantra is what it does *not* contain: **no name of any individual, and no god.** Not Mahāvīra, not any tīrthaṅkara, not a creator. It bows only to *categories of attainment*.

| line | being | status | venerable because | file |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **arihant** | liberated, body remains | omniscient and still teaching | arihant.md (Ch 07) |
| 2 | **siddha** | fully liberated, bodiless | all eight karmas shed; at the summit | siddha.md (Ch 03) |
| 3 | **ācārya** | non-liberated monk | leads + disciplines the order (36 qualities) | acarya.md |
| 4 | **upādhyāya** | non-liberated monk | teaches the āgamas (25 qualities) | upadhyaya.md |
| 5 | **sādhu/sādhvī** | non-liberated monk/nun | keeps the five mahāvratas (27/28 qualities) | sadhu.md |

The order is **soteriological, not institutional**: the two *liberated* beings come first, then the three *living* ranks (ācārya leads, upādhyāya teaches, sādhu is the broad base — every ācārya and upādhyāya is first a sādhu). Their attribute-counts sum to **12 + 8 + 36 + 25 + 27 = 108** — which is exactly why a Jain rosary (*mālā*) has 108 beads: one circuit salutes the whole pañca-parameṣṭhī, attribute by attribute.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — the pañca-parameṣṭhī are not gods, and the mantra is not a petition**
>
> A Western reader sees "supreme beings" + "destroys all sins" and imports a pantheon being prayed *to* for forgiveness. That reading is wrong on both halves. (1) **No deities.** Lines 1–2 honour beings who, by doctrine, have *no agency in the world* — a siddha cannot hear you, answer you, or intervene; lines 3–5 honour *living humans* still on the path. (2) **No petition.** The mantra is *aspirational*: it works on the *reciter*, orienting the mind toward the qualities it names (omniscience, liberation, right conduct). "Destroys all sins" describes what the *act of reverent recollection* does to the reciter's own karma, not a favour granted from above. This is the chapter's opening statement of Jain non-theism — and it is liturgical, not merely philosophical: the most-recited prayer in the tradition asks nothing of anyone.

## 2. The historical founders — *Pārśvanātha* and *Mahāvīra* {#founders}

*→ concept files: [parshvanatha.md](../../concepts/parshvanatha.md) · [mahavira.md](../../concepts/mahavira.md)*

The 24 tīrthaṅkaras (Ch 09) are doctrine, not chronicle: the first 22 leave no historical trace. History begins with the **23rd, Pārśvanātha** — "the earliest Jaina tīrthaṅkara acknowledged as a historical figure" (both Wikipedia and Britannica concur). Traditionally placed in the **9th–8th century BCE**, ~250 years before Mahāvīra, born at Vārāṇasī, emblem the multi-hooded serpent. His doctrinal signature: a **four-fold restraint (*cāturyāma*)** — ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, aparigraha.

The **24th and last, Mahāvīra** (birth name *Vardhamāna*, "Great Hero"), is the towering historical figure — but **not the founder of Jainism.** He *reformed* Pārśva's existing order. The decisive piece of internal evidence is the vow-count:

| | Pārśva's *cāturyāma* (4) | Mahāvīra's mahāvratas (5) |
|---|---|---|
| ahiṃsā · satya · asteya · aparigraha | ✓ | ✓ |
| **brahmacarya (celibacy)** | folded into aparigraha | **split out as its own fifth vow** |

Mahāvīra took Pārśva's four and made celibacy explicit, producing the five mahāvratas of Chapter 08. (The Digambara tradition disputes that this is a *real* change — holding the content was always five.) And the clincher: **Mahāvīra's own parents were followers of Pārśva's order.** A man cannot found a religion his parents already belonged to.

Mahāvīra was a near-contemporary of the **Buddha** and of **Makkhali Gosāla** (§6), in the *śramaṇa* ferment of the Gangetic plain; he appears in the Buddhist canon as *Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta*, an external attestation of his historicity. His dates are contested:

| dating | who holds it | commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| c. 599–527 BCE | Śvetāmbara tradition | Mahāvīra somewhat older than the Buddha |
| 2nd half of 5th c. BCE (d. ~425) | much modern scholarship | a near-exact contemporary of the Buddha |

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Mahāvīra ≠ "the founder of Jainism"**
>
> Outsider summaries routinely call Mahāvīra Jainism's founder, the way the Buddha founded Buddhism. The tradition flatly denies it, and the evidence backs the tradition: Pārśva's pre-existing order, Mahāvīra's Pārśva-following parents, and the cāturyāma→pañca-mahāvrata *upgrade* (you upgrade something that already exists). The honest statement: **Mahāvīra is the historical *reformer* who gave Jainism its present shape — five vows, four-fold community, codified teaching — and the last jina of this age, not the originator of the faith.** Jainism's self-understanding is a beginningless succession; Mahāvīra is merely its most recent peak.

## 3. The great schism — *Digambara* and *Śvetāmbara* {#schism}

*→ concept files: [digambara.md](../../concepts/digambara.md) · [svetambara.md](../../concepts/svetambara.md)*

Within a few centuries of Mahāvīra, Jainism divided into the two sects it has today: **Digambara** ("sky-clad") and **Śvetāmbara** ("white-clad"). The names point straight at the fault line — **monastic dress** — but the disagreement runs deeper, and the remarkable thing is that *all five* of their differences radiate from a *single* root question: **does the vow of non-possession (*aparigraha*, Ch 08) require literal nudity?**

| issue | Digambara | Śvetāmbara |
|---|---|---|
| monastic dress | **nudity** for the advanced muni (true aparigraha) | **white robes** permitted |
| women's liberation (*strī-mokṣa*) | **denied** — must be reborn male first | **affirmed** — women liberate in this birth |
| scriptural canon | original Pūrvas **lost**; Śvetāmbara Āgamas rejected | the **Āgama canon preserved** (council-fixed) |
| omniscient's eating (*kevali-bhukti*) | a kevalin **does not eat** | a kevalin **still takes food** |
| 19th tīrthaṅkara Mallinātha | **male** | **female** (Mallī Devī) |

Trace the chain from the root. *If* true renunciation means owning **nothing**, then (a) the monk wears nothing; (b) a being who (in the Digambara view) cannot practise nude for reasons of modesty — a woman — cannot complete the path in that body, so *strī-mokṣa* is denied and Mallinātha *must* have been male; (c) the perfected being is beyond even the "possession" of needing food, so *kevali-bhukti* is denied. The Śvetāmbara softening — robes are a *protection of equanimity*, not a binding possession — loosens the whole chain at once: robes are fine, a clothed woman can liberate, Mallinātha can be female, a kevalin can eat. **One fork, five consequences.**

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — don't assume one sect "broke away" from the other**
>
> The tidy story — "Jainism split when one group left" — hides a real subtlety: **each sect narrates the *other* as the breakaway.** Digambaras hold that *they* preserve Mahāvīra's original nude, canon-less practice, and that the white-robed northerners innovated under the pressure of a 12-year Magadha famine (the Bhadrabāhu migration south, ~3rd c. BCE; those who stayed under Sthūlabhadra relaxed the rule and fixed a 12-Aṅga canon at the Council of Pāṭaliputra, ~300 BCE). Śvetāmbara sources instead blame a later figure, Śivabhūti, who reintroduced nudity out of pride. What is *agreed* is the **result** (two sects, five differences); *who left whom* is contested. The corpus reports the schism as a contested origin, not a settled sequence.

## 4. The texts and the two truths — *Kundakunda*, the *Samayasāra*, and *niścaya/vyavāhara* {#texts}

*→ concept files: [kundakunda.md](../../concepts/kundakunda.md) · [samayasara.md](../../concepts/samayasara.md) · [nishchaya-vyavahara.md](../../concepts/nishchaya-vyavahara.md)*

The most influential post-canonical Jain philosopher is the Digambara monk **Kundakunda** (traditionally 2nd–3rd c. CE; scholarly dating ranges widely and is openly contested). In the Digambara tradition his authority ranks *third*, "right after Mahāvīra and [his disciple] Gautama." His chief work, the **Samayasāra** ("Essence of the Self," 439 Prākrit verses), is the foundational text of Jain mysticism, and his enduring device is the **two standpoints**:

- **niścaya-naya** — the *absolute / transcendental* standpoint: a thing in its own intrinsic nature. From here, the soul (*jīva*) is *always* pure consciousness, **never** the doer of material karma.
- **vyavāhara-naya** — the *conventional / empirical* standpoint: a thing in relation to other substances, attributes, space, and time. From here, the soul binds karma, suffers, and climbs the gunasthāna ladder (Ch 03).

This dissolves an apparent contradiction. The Tattvārtha Sūtra (and all of Chapter 03) says the soul *binds karma and works free*; the Samayasāra says the soul *never does karma and is always pure*. These are **not rival claims** — they are the *same soul from the two standpoints*. The TS speaks *vyavāhara* (the soul as entangled and progressing — the ladder); the Samayasāra speaks *niścaya* (the soul as essentially pure — what stands at the top of the ladder). Kundakunda's framework requires *both*. The Samayasāra makes this vivid in its merit/demerit chapter: *puṇya is a gold chain and pāpa an iron chain* — and **both are still chains** (the bridge to §5).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — niścaya/vyavāhara is NOT the Buddhist or Advaita "two truths"**
>
> This is the chapter's prime-directive moment. The "two levels of truth" shape recurs across Indian thought, and it is irresistible to flatten them into one idea. The *structures* are parallel; the *verdict on the conventional world* is not — and that is the whole difference:
>
> | system | the two levels | status of the *conventional* level |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Jain** niścaya / vyavāhara | absolute nature / relational description | **both genuinely true** — the world is real; vyavāhara is no falsehood |
> | **Buddhist** (Madhyamaka) paramārtha / saṃvṛti | ultimate / conventional | conventional is **empty of inherent essence** — provisional (sunyata.md, Ch 04/06) |
> | **Advaita** pāramārthika / vyāvahārika | Brahman-only / appearance | conventional is ultimately **false / sublated** — māyā (maya-advaita.md, Ch 04) |
>
> Jainism is the odd one out: a **pluralist-realist** two-*standpoints*, not a two-*truths* in which one level dissolves the other. Even the strongest niścaya-privileging reading of Kundakunda does *not* say the world is illusory — the six dravyas stay real (Ch 09); it says the soul's *essence* is purer than its entangled appearance. Calling niścaya/vyavāhara "the Jain version of the two truths" smuggles in either Buddhist emptiness or Advaita illusionism — the exact term-mapping the charter forbids. (Within Jainism itself the *relative authority* of the two standpoints is contested — which is why nishchaya-vyavahara.md is tagged *contested* — but neither side makes the world unreal.)

## 5. Merit, demerit, and the eight karma-natures — *puṇya · pāpa · karma-prakṛti* {#karma-detail}

*→ concept files: [punya.md](../../concepts/punya.md) · [papa.md](../../concepts/papa.md) · [karma-prakriti.md](../../concepts/karma-prakriti.md)*

Chapter 03 ran the karma *machinery* (influx → bondage → stoppage → shedding). This section adds the two pieces the machinery needs to be complete: the **moral polarity** of karma and its **eight species**.

**Puṇya (merit) and pāpa (demerit)** are the *auspicious* (*śubha*) and *inauspicious* (*aśubha*) polarities of bound karma. Puṇya — from charity, compassion, devotion, pure thought — yields pleasant results (good rebirth, ease). Pāpa — from violence, lying, theft, unchastity, and the kaṣāyas — yields suffering. They are **sub-types of āsrava and bandha**, which is why the canonical Tattvārtha Sūtra lists **seven** tattvas (TS 1.4; Ch 03) while the popular *nava-tattva* scheme lists **nine**: the nine simply break puṇya and pāpa out of their parents (āsrava/bandha). Same reality, different count. *(Popular sources disagree about which sect prefers which count; the corpus does not assert a settled sectarian mapping — only that the two counts are equivalent.)*

The Jain twist — and §4's gold-chain image cashed out — is that **merit is not the goal.** Most religions tell you to maximize merit; Jainism says puṇya is *better* than pāpa but **still binds the soul.**

> **Sit with this.** You do not reach liberation by accumulating a *positive karmic balance*; you reach it by exiting the merit/demerit economy *entirely*. A soul weighed down by golden karma is still weighed down. Good karma can buy better *conditions* for practice — a human birth, a clear mind, a body fit for asceticism — but mokṣa requires shedding puṇya *along with* pāpa, via saṃvara (stop the influx of both) and nirjarā (burn off the stock of both). This is the niścaya standpoint made practical: from the soul's true nature, *all* karmic adhesion is foreign matter, gold or iron.

And the **eight karma-natures (*karma-prakṛti*)** — the *kinds* of karmic matter. TS 8.4, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **ādyo jñānadarśanāvaraṇavedanīyamohanīyāyurnāma gotrāntarāyāḥ** (TS 8.4)
> "[Nature-bondage] is of eight kinds: knowledge-obscuring (*jñānāvaraṇa*), perception-obscuring (*darśanāvaraṇa*), feeling-producing (*vedanīya*), deluding (*mohanīya*), life-determining (*āyuḥ*), physique-making (*nāma*), status-determining (*gotra*), and obstructive (*antarāya*)."

The eight split into **four *ghāti*** (destructive — they obscure the soul's own powers: jñānāvaraṇa, darśanāvaraṇa, mohanīya, antarāya) and **four *aghāti*** (non-destructive — they only shape embodiment: vedanīya, āyu, nāma, gotra). This is the master-table behind the arihant/siddha distinction (§1, Ch 07): an **arihant** has destroyed the four *ghāti* (hence omniscience) while the four *aghāti* keep the body running until death, at which point the soul becomes a **siddha**. Of the eight, **mohanīya (delusion) is the linchpin** — it generates the kaṣāyas that drive fresh influx and corrupts both faith and conduct; the entire gunasthāna ladder is essentially the staged demolition of mohanīya, after which the other ghāti karmas collapse and kevala-jñāna dawns.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain karma is neither a moral scoreboard nor "balanced"**
>
> Two errors to avoid. (1) **Merit does not *offset* demerit.** You cannot cancel pāpa by piling up puṇya — both are bondage, both must be *shed*, not netted against each other. (2) **Karma is not a divine ledger.** No god tallies your deeds; the eight natures are *physical matter* (kārmaṇa pudgala, Ch 03/09) that adheres according to impersonal law. The moral quality (śubha/aśubha) is real, but it is a property of *matter-influx driven by the kaṣāyas*, not a verdict handed down from above. This keeps §5 consistent with the chapter's thread: even the moral accounting is non-theistic and effort-governed.

## 6. Jainism among the renouncers — *Ājīvika* and the limits of fate {#ajivika}

*→ concept file: [ajivika.md](../../concepts/ajivika.md)*

Mahāvīra, the Buddha, and a third teacher — **Makkhali Gosāla** — were contemporaries in the same 5th-century-BCE *śramaṇa* (renouncer) world. Gosāla founded the **Ājīvika** school, and the corpus includes it for one sharp reason: it is the **precise inversion of the Jain account of agency**, and the contrast defines Jainism by its negative.

The Ājīvika's defining doctrine is **niyati** — *absolute determinism*. Everything is preordained; there is **no free will**; and human effort and karma have **no power** to change one's destiny. The soul transmigrates, but through a *fixed* number of rebirths that no amount of asceticism can shorten. Liberation comes to everyone *eventually, on schedule, whether they strive or not.*

| | Ājīvika | Jainism |
|---|---|---|
| destiny (*niyati*) | the **sole** determinant | **one** factor among five |
| effort (*puruṣārtha*) | **powerless** | **decisive** — asceticism directly sheds karma |
| karma | exists but **inert** | physical matter the soul **controls** |
| liberation | reached by all, on a fixed schedule | reached **only** by deliberate effort |

Here is the exact philosophical fork. Jainism recognizes **five co-operating factors (*samavāya*)** in any event: *svabhāva* (nature), *niyati* (destiny), *kāla* (time), *pūrva-karma* (prior karma), and *puruṣārtha* (effort). The Ājīvika error, from the Jain side, is **absolutizing one of the five — niyati — and annihilating the rest**, above all *puruṣārtha*. And effort is the factor the entire Jain path stands on: the vows, tapas, saṃvara, nirjarā (Ch 08, Ch 03) all presuppose that *striving works*. If niyati were total, every page of the preceding chapters would be pointless. The Ājīvika is the foil that makes Jain free-will realism visible.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jainism is not fatalist, and the Ājīvika portrait is its enemies' caricature**
>
> Two cautions. (1) Because Jain *karma* sounds deterministic to modern ears ("your present is shaped by past deeds"), it gets fused with niyati-style fatalism. Resist it: Jain karma is *the very thing effort acts on* — you bind it, you shed it, you choose. Karma is the *medium* of agency, not its denial. (2) **Source-bias caveat, sharper than usual:** every surviving account of the Ājīvika comes from its rivals — the Jain *Bhagavatī Sūtra* (which dramatizes a bitter split between Gosāla and Mahāvīra after ~6 years together) and the Buddhist canon. No Ājīvika self-statement survives. So the fatalism we attribute to them is the *Jain/Buddhist rendering* of their view; the contrast is real and useful, but the portrait may be unfair. The corpus flags this rather than smoothing it over.

## 7. The whole human-and-historical layer — a summary visual {#summary}

```
WHO JAINS BOW TO (Ṇamokāra mantra — names no god, no individual):
   arihant · siddha          ── LIBERATED (beyond agency; cannot be petitioned)
   ācārya · upādhyāya · sādhu ── LIVING ranks (lead / teach / keep the vows)
   108 attributes total → 108 mālā beads.  Aspirational, never petitionary.

HISTORICAL FOUNDERS (not creators — reformers in a beginningless line):
   Pārśva (23rd, ~9-8c BCE, cāturyāma = 4 vows)
        └─→ Mahāvīra (24th, reformer; adds brahmacarya → 5 mahāvratas; 4-fold saṅgha)
            [contemporary of Buddha + Gosāla; "founder of Jainism" is FALSE]

THE SCHISM (one root question: does aparigraha require nudity?):
   DIGAMBARA (yes) ── nudity · strī-mokṣa denied · canon lost · kevalin doesn't eat · Mallinātha male
   ŚVETĀMBARA (no) ── robes  · strī-mokṣa affirmed · canon kept · kevalin eats     · Mallinātha female
   (each sect calls the OTHER the breakaway)

TEXTS & TWO TRUTHS:
   Kundakunda → Samayasāra → niścaya (soul always pure) / vyavāhara (soul binds karma)
        same soul, two standpoints — BOTH real (≠ Buddhist emptiness, ≠ Advaita māyā)

KARMA, COMPLETED:
   puṇya (gold chain) + pāpa (iron chain) — BOTH bind; shed, not balanced
   8 karma-prakṛti (TS 8.4): 4 GHĀTI (obscure the soul) + 4 AGHĀTI (shape the body)
        arihant = 4 ghāti destroyed; siddha = all 8 gone

AMONG THE ŚRAMAṆAS:
   Ājīvika (Gosāla): niyati = total fate, effort powerless  ──  the exact INVERSE of
   Jainism: niyati is 1 of 5 factors; puruṣārtha (effort) is decisive
```

The shape to carry away: **Jainism is a non-theistic religion of human effort with a deep history.** Its holy beings are perfected *people* one aspires to become, not gods one prays to; its great teacher *reformed* a tradition older than himself; its community split over how literally to take a single vow; its mystics taught that the soul is already pure beneath its karmic entanglement; and it defined itself, in part, *against* a fatalism that denied effort could change anything. Every distinctive move traces back to the same commitment the whole corpus has been circling: **the soul works itself free.**

## 8. Check yourself {#check}

**8.1** The Ṇamokāra mantra promises that the five-fold salutation "destroys all sins," and bows to "supreme beings." A visitor concludes Jains pray to a pantheon of gods for forgiveness. Give *two* specific reasons this is wrong — one about *whom* the mantra addresses (and their capacity to respond), and one about *how* the recitation is supposed to work on the reciter.

**8.2** Mahāvīra is often called "the founder of Jainism." State the tradition's own counter-claim, and give the *single strongest* piece of internal evidence for it (hint: it involves the number of vows, or Mahāvīra's parents). Then explain why the cāturyāma→pañca-mahāvrata change is evidence of *reform*, not *founding*.

**8.3** The Digambara and Śvetāmbara sects disagree about (a) monastic dress, (b) whether women can be liberated, (c) the scriptural canon, (d) whether an omniscient being eats, and (e) the gender of the 19th tīrthaṅkara. Show how *all five* can be derived from *one* underlying disagreement, and name that disagreement.

**8.4** Kundakunda's Samayasāra says the soul "never does karma and is always pure," while Chapter 03 says the soul "binds karma and works free." A reader cries contradiction. Resolve it using the niścaya/vyavāhara distinction — then say precisely why this is *not* the same as the Buddhist claim that the conventional world is empty.

**8.5** A practitioner reasons: "I'll do many good deeds, bank a large store of *puṇya*, and that merit will carry me to liberation."
