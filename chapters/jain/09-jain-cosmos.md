## How to use this chapter

Chapter 01 introduced Jain ontology through a handful of concepts — substance, mode, matter, the atom. This chapter is the **complete blueprint**: it lays out the entire furniture of the Jain universe — *what kinds of thing exist, what it means for them to exist, and the shape of the world they make* — in one place, grounded as far as possible in directly-fetched Tattvārtha-Sūtra verses.

The Jain cosmos is unusual among Indian systems in being **pluralist, realist, eternal, and uncreated**. There is no creator god (Ch 03, tirthankara.md), no single underlying reality that the many are appearances of (contrast Advaita's Brahman, Ch 04), and no reduction of substance to momentary flashes (contrast Buddhist anicca, Ch 04). There are *six* irreducible kinds of substance; all six are equally real; none was made; none will end. This chapter is the systematic statement of that view.

It builds in three movements. §§1–2 give the *general theory of substance* — what a dravya is (§1) and what existence itself is (§2). §§3–4 give the *six substances* — the astikāya property-cut (§3) and then each of the six in turn (§4). §§5–6 give the *world they constitute* — its spatial shape (the loka, §5) and its temporal rhythm (the wheel of ages, §6). Read §2 slowly: the Jain definition of *being* as "permanence-in-change" is the seed from which both this chapter's pluralism and Chapter 02's many-sidedness (anekāntavāda) grow.

## 1. What there is — the six substances — *dravya* {#dravya}

*→ concept file: [dravya.md](../../concepts/dravya.md)*

A *dravya* is a **substance** — an ultimate, irreducible existent. The Tattvārtha Sūtra defines it twice over. TS 5.38, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **guṇaparyāyavat dravyam**
> "That which has qualities (*guṇa*) and modes (*paryāya*) is a substance (*dravya*)."

A *guṇa* is a permanent attribute, inseparable from the substance (a soul's consciousness, matter's colour). A *paryāya* is a passing modification (this thought, this shade of red right now). Every substance is a *guṇa*-bearer persisting *through* a stream of changing *paryāyas*. The Jain universe is made of exactly **six** such substances — no more, no fewer:

| # | dravya | Sanskrit | nature | sentient? | has body (astikāya)? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | soul | *jīva* | consciousness (*cetanā*) | yes | yes |
| 2 | matter | *pudgala* | touch, taste, smell, colour | no | yes |
| 3 | medium of motion | *dharma* | enables movement | no | yes |
| 4 | medium of rest | *adharma* | enables rest | no | yes |
| 5 | space | *ākāśa* | provides room (*avagāha*) | no | yes |
| 6 | time | *kāla* | enables change (*vartanā*) | no | **no** |

The first split is **jīva vs. ajīva** — the one sentient substance against the five non-sentient. The whole drama of Chapter 03's soteriology is the entanglement of substance 1 (souls) with substance 2 (matter, as karma) and the working-free of the one from the other. This chapter steps back from that drama to map the *whole* board on which it plays out.

## 2. What it means to exist — *sat* {#sat}

*→ concept file: [sat.md](../../concepts/sat.md)*

Before naming the six, the TS says what *existing* even is — and this is the philosophical keystone of the entire system. TS 5.30, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **utpādavyayadhrauvyayuktaṃ sat**
> "Existence (*sat*) is endowed with origination (*utpāda*), destruction (*vyaya*), and permanence (*dhrauvya*)."

To exist, for a Jain, is to do *all three at once*. At every instant a real thing is **originating** a new mode, **losing** its old mode, and **enduring** as the same substance underneath. Pūjyapāda's classic image (sat.md) is a lump of gold: melt the bracelet into a ring, and the bracelet-mode perishes (*vyaya*), the ring-mode arises (*utpāda*), and the gold persists (*dhrauvya*) — one event, three aspects.

> **This is the seed of the whole system — sit with it.** Most metaphysics forces a choice: either reality is fundamentally *permanent* (and change is illusion — Advaita's Brahman, Ch 04) or fundamentally *impermanent* (and permanence is illusion — Buddhist momentariness, anicca.md, Ch 04). Jainism refuses the choice. It builds *both* into the definition of being itself: the permanent (*dravya* + *guṇa*) and the changing (*paryāya*) are *equally real, simultaneously*. This is why the Jain universe can be eternal *and* dynamic, why substances never perish *and* never stop changing. And it is exactly why Jain epistemology had to become **many-sided** (anekāntavāda, Ch 02): if every real thing is permanent-*and*-impermanent, then any single-sided assertion ("it is permanent," "it is not") captures only one face. The ontology of §2 *forces* the logic of Chapter 02. Being is three-fold, so truth must be many-sided.

## 3. The extended bodies — *astikāya* {#astikaya}

*→ concept file: [astikaya.md](../../concepts/astikaya.md)*

Now a property-cut across the six. Five of the substances have *spatial body* — extension, magnitude, a manifoldness of points. One does not. The substances with body are the **astikāyas** (*asti* "exists" + *kāya* "body"). The TS signals this in the very word it uses to introduce the non-soul substances. TS 5.1, Sanskrit + Pūjyapāda directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **ajīvakāyā dharmādharmākāśapudgalāḥ**
> "The non-soul *bodies* (*ajīva-kāya*) are the medium of motion, the medium of rest, space, and matter."

Why *kāya*, "body"? Pūjyapāda: "Just as the body is a conglomeration of matter, so also there is **manifoldness of space-points** (*pradeśa*) in the medium of motion, etc." A substance is a *kāya* because it occupies *more than one* point. The unit is the **pradeśa** — the indivisible space-point, defined by ākāśa. To have extension is to span many pradeśas.

And exactly one substance fails the test. Pūjyapāda again: time "is of the extent of one atom … it pervades one space-point only. Therefore it has no second space-point." So **kāla is *anastikāya*** — the one bodiless substance.

```
SIX DRAVYAS
 ├── FIVE ASTIKĀYA  (spatial body / many pradeśa):  jīva · pudgala · dharma · adharma · ākāśa
 └── ONE ANASTIKĀYA (a single point, no body):       kāla
```

This is not a second inventory; it is the *same six*, sorted by whether they have magnitude. "The world is five extended substances plus time" and "the world is six substances" say the same thing. (Kundakunda's *Pañcāstikāyasāra* — "essence of the five extended substances" — is named for exactly this five-fold count.)

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — six dravyas ≠ five astikāyas (don't lose kāla in the count)**
>
> Because Jain texts cheerfully use both "six substances" and "five extended bodies," readers blur them — either dropping time from the substance-list or smuggling it into the astikāyas. Hold the distinction: time *is* a full substance (TS 5.39, *kālaśca*, "time also [is a substance]"), with its own job (enabling change), but it is *not* an astikāya, because it has no spatial spread. The astikāya scheme is a statement about *extension*, not about *reality* — kāla is fully real and fully bodiless at once.

## 4. The six, one by one {#the-six}

*→ concept files: [jiva.md](../../concepts/jiva.md) · [pudgala.md](../../concepts/pudgala.md) · [dharma-dravya.md](../../concepts/dharma-dravya.md) · [adharma-dravya.md](../../concepts/adharma-dravya.md) · [akasha-dravya.md](../../concepts/akasha-dravya.md) · [kala-dravya.md](../../concepts/kala-dravya.md)*

### 4.1 Soul — *jīva* {#jiva}
The one sentient substance; its mark is consciousness (TS 2.8, *upayogo lakṣaṇam*, Ch 07). Souls are *infinitely many* and individually distinct — Jainism is pluralist about consciousness, against both Advaita's single Ātman and Buddhism's no-self (Ch 04). A jīva is an astikāya: it occupies the space of the body it animates, and a liberated soul keeps the dimensions of its last body. Everything in Chapter 03 — bondage, the vows, liberation — happens to *this* substance.

### 4.2 Matter — *pudgala* {#pudgala}
The one substance with sensory qualities (touch, taste, smell, colour). It ranges from the partless atom (*paramāṇu*, Ch 01) up to vast aggregates (*skandha*); karma itself is fine pudgala (kārmaṇa-varganā). Pudgala is the only ajīva substance that *interacts* with souls — which is why it, and it alone among the insentient five, can bind a jīva.

### 4.3 & 4.4 The media of motion and rest — *dharma* and *adharma* {#dharma-adharma}
Here is Jainism's most distinctive ontological invention. TS 5.17, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **gatisthityupagrahau dharmādharmayorupakāraḥ**
> "The functions of the medium of motion and the medium of rest are to assist motion (*gati*) and rest (*sthiti*)."

*Dharma-dravya* (nothing to do with moral dharma — see the alert) is the **medium that makes motion possible**; *adharma-dravya* is the **medium that makes rest possible**. They do not *push* or *stop* anything — they are *enabling conditions*, like water for a fish (the classic image): water does not propel the fish, but without water there is no swimming. Both pervade the entire occupied cosmos uniformly. Their importance is structural: because the *motion-medium fills only the loka and stops at its edge*, nothing can move beyond the world — which is precisely what gives the cosmos a definite boundary (§5) and what stops a liberated soul at the very top (§5, Ch 03).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *dharma-dravya* ≠ a physics "field," ether, or spacetime**
>
> This is the chapter's prime-directive moment. A medium that "permeates all space and conditions whether motion can occur" sounds *irresistibly* like the luminiferous ether, or a field, or the geometry of spacetime — and modern enthusiasts have mapped it onto all three. Resist it. The parallel is real but the identity is false, and on several axes:
>
> | | Jain dharma-dravya | physics field / spacetime |
> |---|---|---|
> | what it does | a *passive enabling condition* — permits motion, never causes or quantifies it | *dynamical* — carries energy, exerts forces, has values that evolve |
> | how it's known | inferred from doctrine (motion needs a medium; the world needs a boundary) | measured, with predictive equations |
> | why it exists | to *bound the cosmos* and explain why souls stop at the top | to describe and predict physical interaction |
>
> Dharma-dravya answers a *metaphysical* question ("what makes the world finite and motion intelligible?"), not a *physical* one ("how do bodies move?"). It has no equations, no measurable value, no energy. Calling it "the Jain ether" or "a 5th-century field theory" imports a quantitative, dynamical apparatus that simply is not there — the exact "the sages already knew physics" error this whole project exists to prevent. The honest label is `structurally-parallel-to`, never `is-a`. (See dharma-dravya.md.)

### 4.5 Space — *ākāśa* {#akasha}
TS 5.18, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018): **ākāśasyāvagāhaḥ** — "The function of space is to *provide room* (*avagāha*)." Ākāśa is the container that gives every other substance somewhere to be. It is the only substance extending *beyond* the cosmos: it splits into *lokākāśa* (the occupied space, full of all six dravyas) and *alokākāśa* (infinite empty space beyond, containing nothing but itself — no motion-medium, so nothing can ever go there). Ākāśa defines the *pradeśa* by which §3 measured everyone's extension.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *ākāśa* ≠ Vaiśeṣika *ākāśa* (ether)**
>
> Same Sanskrit word, different category. Vaiśeṣika ākāśa is the *ether that bears sound* — sound is its quality (*śabda-guṇa*). Jain ākāśa is a *featureless container* with no sensory quality at all; its only job is to provide room. Don't let the shared name import a sound-bearing ether into Jain space. (See akasha-dravya.md.)

### 4.6 Time — *kāla* {#kala}
The sixth substance, and the bodiless one (§3). TS 5.22, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018): **vartanāpariṇāmakriyāḥ paratvāparatve ca kālasya** — time assists substances through *vartanā* (continuity/persistence-through-change), modification, motion, and the relations of before/after. TS 5.39, directly fetched: **kālaśca** — "time also is a substance." Kāla is what lets every other substance *undergo its paryāyas* on a schedule — the enabling condition of change itself, just as dharma is the enabling condition of motion. (The Digambara tradition treats time as innumerable distinct one-point time-atoms, *kālāṇu*; the Śvetāmbara status of kāla as a full substance is more disputed — a sectarian wrinkle noted in kala-dravya.md.)

## 5. The shape of the world — *loka* {#loka}

*→ concept file: [loka-jain.md](../../concepts/loka-jain.md)*

The six substances together constitute a **bounded world**, the *loka*. Where dharma-dravya reaches, the world is; where it stops, the world ends, and beyond is only infinite empty *aloka* (§4.5). So the cosmos is *finite* — vast but not infinite — and uncreated and everlasting.

| region | Sanskrit | content |
|---|---|---|
| lower world | *adholoka* | the seven hells (*naraka*), descending in suffering |
| middle world | *madhyaloka* | the human realm — continents, oceans, Mount Meru; the only place liberation happens |
| upper world | *ūrdhvaloka* | the heavens (*deva* realms) |
| the summit | *siddha-śilā* | the crescent at the very top where liberated souls (*siddhas*) reside |

The classic image is a **cosmic man** (*loka-puruṣa*), standing with hands on hips — the narrow hells below, the slim middle world at the waist, the broad heavens at the chest, and the siddha-śilā as a crown. The summit closes the loop with Chapter 03: a soul, the instant it sheds its last karma, shoots *straight up* (motion being its freed nature) and comes to rest at the top of the loka — and stops there, because at the boundary the motion-medium ends and there is nothing in aloka to move through. **The geography of liberation is literal**: mokṣa has an address, and it is the ceiling of the world (§4.3–4.5 explain *why* the freed soul stops exactly there).

## 6. The wheel of ages — *utsarpiṇī / avasarpiṇī* {#kalachakra}

*→ concept file: [utsarpini-avasarpini.md](../../concepts/utsarpini-avasarpini.md)*

Finally, the cosmos's *temporal* shape. Jain worldly time is a **wheel (*kālacakra*)**, not a line — fitting an uncreated universe with no first moment. It turns through two half-cycles: **avasarpiṇī**, the *descending* half (conditions worsen) and **utsarpiṇī**, the *ascending* half (conditions improve). Each half has six "spokes" (*aras*), running from a near-utopia of wish-fulfilling trees down to an age where religion is impossible — and then back up.

| ara | descending (avasarpiṇī) | what happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *suṣama-suṣamā* | utopia; no religion needed |
| 2 | *suṣamā* | idyllic, diminishing |
| 3 | *suṣama-duḥṣamā* | the age turns; **Ṛṣabhanātha, the 1st Tīrthaṅkara, appears** near its end |
| 4 | *duḥṣama-suṣamā* | the religious age; **the other 23 Tīrthaṅkaras, through Mahāvīra, appear** — renunciation and liberation possible |
| 5 | *duḥṣamā* | **the present age**; religion survives, but liberation is no longer attainable |
| 6 | *duḥṣama-duḥṣamā* | nadir; religion practically impossible; then the wheel reverses |

**Twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras arise per half-cycle.** We live in the *fifth ara of a descending half*, after Mahāvīra (this cycle's last ford-maker): the path is fully known, but final liberation must await the wheel's turning. Crucially, **this rise-and-fall operates only in the *karma-bhūmi*** — the "lands of action" (Bharata, Airāvata) where moral-religious life and liberation occur — not across the whole cosmos. The kālacakra is the clock of the *inhabited, striving* world, not a universal metronome.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — the Jain *kālacakra* ≠ the Hindu *yuga* cycle / *avatāra* descent**
>
> Both are cyclic-time schemes with declining ages and periodic renewers of dharma, so they get fused. Two differences keep them apart. (1) **The renewers run opposite ways.** A Hindu *avatāra* is a *deity descending* into the world to restore order (Viṣṇu, BG 4.7–8; avatara-vedanta.md). A Jain *Tīrthaṅkara* is a *liberated human ascending out* — no god comes down; a person climbs free and, on the way, re-founds the ford. Renewal is *ascent*, not descent. (2) **The Jain cycle has no controller.** The yuga wheel can be presided over by Īśvara; the kālacakra turns *on its own*, in an uncreated cosmos with no god at the hub. The structural parallel is genuine (`structurally-parallel-to`); the identity is false. (See utsarpini-avasarpini.md.)

## 7. The whole architecture — a summary visual {#architecture}

```
WHAT IT IS TO EXIST (TS 5.30):  sat = origination + destruction + permanence  (being is change-in-permanence)
        │   ⇒ forces anekāntavāda (Ch 02): many-sided truth for a many-sided being
        ▼
SIX SUBSTANCES (dravya; TS 5.38 = guṇa-bearer + paryāya-stream):
        │
        ├── jīva      — soul; sentient; infinitely many, distinct        ┐
        ├── pudgala   — matter; paramāṇu→skandha; karma is fine pudgala  │  FIVE
        ├── dharma    — medium of MOTION (TS 5.17); fish-in-water        │  ASTIKĀYA
        ├── adharma   — medium of REST  (TS 5.17)                        │  (have body;
        ├── ākāśa     — SPACE; provides room (TS 5.18); loka + aloka     ┘  many pradeśa)
        └── kāla      — TIME; enables change (TS 5.22, 5.39)  → ANASTIKĀYA (one point, no body)
        │
        ▼
THE WORLD THEY MAKE:
   SPACE  →  LOKA (loka-puruṣa): hells / middle world / heavens / siddha-śilā at the summit
             motion-medium stops at the edge ⇒ finite cosmos; freed souls rise and STOP at the top (Ch 03)
   TIME   →  KĀLACAKRA: avasarpiṇī ↓ + utsarpiṇī ↑, 6 aras each, 24 Tīrthaṅkaras/half
             we are in ara 5 (descending); karma-bhūmi only
```

The shape to carry away: **the Jain universe is a closed, eternal, uncreated system of six real substances** — one of them conscious, five of them not, five of them extended, one of them not. Nothing made it; nothing sustains it; nothing will end it. Souls and matter tangle and untangle (Ch 03) inside a finite world-space (§5) on the turning of a beginningless wheel (§6). Every other chapter's drama happens *here*, on this board.

## 8. Check yourself {#check}

**8.1** The TS defines existence (TS 5.30) as origination *and* destruction *and* permanence, all at once. Explain how this single definition lets the Jain cosmos be *eternal* (substances never perish) and *dynamic* (substances never stop changing) at the same time — and then say why this ontology *requires* the many-sided logic of Chapter 02 rather than merely permitting it.

**8.2** Time (*kāla*) is one of the six substances but is *not* one of the five astikāyas. State the single property it lacks, quote the doctrine that fixes this (Pūjyapāda on *kāya*), and explain why "six substances" and "five extended bodies" are not a contradiction.

**8.3** Dharma-dravya "permeates all of cosmic space and conditions whether motion can occur." A modern reader says: "That's just the physics concept of a field — the Jains discovered field theory." Give three specific respects in which dharma-dravya is *not* a physical field, and name what kind of question it is actually answering. (Hint: ask what it *does*, how it's *known*, and what equations it has.)
