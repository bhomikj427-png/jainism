## How to use this chapter

Chapter 02 covered *how the Jains reason* about a known object — naya (standpoints), syādvāda (qualified assertion), anekāntavāda (many-sidedness). This chapter goes one layer deeper: it covers *the knowing itself* — the five kinds of cognition (*jñāna*) a soul can have, from a glance across a field to the omniscience of a liberated being.

It can be read stand-alone, but it leans on two earlier chapters. §1 places this whole topic inside the three-jewels frame from Ch 03's soteriology. §3's omniscience (kevala-jñāna) is the same event that Ch 03 §9 located at guṇasthāna 13. And the chapter's spine — §4, the parokṣa/pratyakṣa inversion — is meant to be read *against* Chapter 05's Nyāya, Buddhist, and Mīmāṃsā accounts of perception. Jainism uses the same two words those schools use and assigns them to the opposite things. That is not a quirk; it is the signature of Jain epistemology, and §4 is the heart of the chapter.

## 1. Which jewel is this? — *ratnatraya* {#ratnatraya}

*→ concept file: [ratnatraya.md](../../concepts/ratnatraya.md)*

### 1.1 TS 1.1 — knowledge is one of three {#ratnatraya-verse}

The very first sūtra of the Tattvārtha Sūtra names the whole path. TS 1.1, directly fetched from Vijay K. Jain 2018 (with Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi*):

> **samyagdarśanajñānacāritrāṇi mokṣamārgaḥ**
> "Right faith (*samyak-darśana*), right knowledge (*samyak-jñāna*), and right conduct (*samyak-cāritra*), together, constitute the path to liberation (*mokṣa-mārga*)."
> — TS 1.1, Vijay K. Jain (tr.), Motilal Banarsidass 2018

Three jewels (*ratnatraya*), one path. Two grammatical facts in the commentary fix the doctrine:

- **"Path" is singular.** Pūjyapāda stresses that *mārga* is one word, not three. This "refutes the view that each of these singly constitutes the path to liberation." You do not get to liberation on knowledge alone, or conduct alone. The three are one integrated cause.
- **They are not a staircase.** Right faith and right knowledge "are attained by the soul simultaneously," like the heat and light of the sun appearing together the instant the clouds part. The dependence between the jewels is *logical*, not chronological: right faith is what makes knowledge and conduct *samyak* — "right" — in the first place.

This chapter is about the **second jewel**: *samyak-jñāna*, right knowledge. Ch 03 already mapped the third jewel (right conduct → [charitra.md](../../concepts/charitra.md), the mahāvratas) and named the first ([samyak-darshana.md](../../concepts/samyak-darshana.md), conviction in the seven tattvas). Here we open up what "knowledge" actually means in the Jain system — and it turns out to have a precise five-part architecture.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *ratnatraya* ≠ Buddhist *triratna***
>
> Both phrases are routinely Englished as "the Three Jewels," and the surface match is total. They name completely different *kinds* of thing.
>
> | | what the "three jewels" are | their role |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Jain *ratnatraya*** | right faith · right knowledge · right conduct | three **components of one path** the practitioner enacts |
> | **Buddhist *triratna*** | Buddha · Dharma · Saṅgha | three **objects of refuge** the practitioner takes shelter in |
>
> The Jain jewels are *things you do and have*; the Buddhist jewels are *a teacher, his teaching, and his community you commit to*. Mapping one onto the other because both are "three jewels" imports a refuge-structure into Jainism that is not there. (The genuine Jain counterpart of "objects of reverence" is the *pañca-parameṣṭhin* of the Ṇamokāra mantra — a different list, treated in a later chapter.) The honest structural parallel to the *ratnatraya* on the Buddhist side is the **Noble Eightfold Path**, which likewise bundles faith/wisdom/conduct into one path — see [nirvana-buddhist.md](../../concepts/nirvana-buddhist.md).

## 2. The two instruments of knowing — *pramāṇa* and *upayoga* {#pramana-upayoga}

*→ concept files: [pramana.md](../../concepts/pramana.md) · [upayoga.md](../../concepts/upayoga.md)*

Before the five jñānas, two framing concepts. Right knowledge is not free-floating: it is an *activity of the soul*, and it comes in a complete form and a partial form.

### 2.1 Consciousness is the soul's defining mark — *upayoga* {#upayoga}

From Ch 03 §2: TS 2.8, *upayogo lakṣaṇam* — "consciousness (*upayoga*) is the distinctive characteristic of the soul." Every jīva, however karma-obscured, is *doing* upayoga: actively knowing and perceiving. Upayoga splits into two modes (TS 2.9):

| mode | Sanskrit | character |
|---|---|---|
| Undifferentiated apprehension | *darśana* (nirākāra) | bare, pre-conceptual registering that *something* is there |
| Differentiated cognition | *jñāna* (sākāra) | structured, determinate knowing of *what* it is |

The five jñānas of this chapter are all forms of the *jñāna* (sākāra, "with-form") mode of upayoga. They are the soul's determinate knowing in its five graded reaches. Soteriology, in Ch 03's terms, is the story of this upayoga being progressively un-obscured until it becomes total — which is the fifth jñāna, omniscience.

### 2.2 Complete vs. partial cognition — *pramāṇa* and *naya* {#pramana}

TS 1.6, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **pramāṇanayairadhigamaḥ**
> "The [seven categories] are known by means of *pramāṇa* and *naya*."

Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi* gives the cleanest formula in the whole Jain corpus for the difference:

| instrument | Sanskrit | what it grasps |
|---|---|---|
| **pramāṇa** | *sakalādeśa* | the **whole** substance with all its attributes at once — complete valid cognition |
| **naya** | *vikalādeśa* | **one** attribute or aspect of the substance — partial valid cognition |

Both are valid; neither alone is enough for a finite knower. Naya was Chapter 02's subject. **Pramāṇa is this chapter's** — and the five jñānas of TS 1.9 are exactly the *content* of pramāṇa: the five ways a soul achieves valid cognition. The perfected, fully-actual pramāṇa is the omniscient being's kevala-jñāna (§9); everyone else approximates it through nayas arranged by syādvāda.

## 3. The five-fold ladder — *the jñānas* {#five-jnanas}

### 3.1 TS 1.9 — the canonical list {#five-jnanas-verse}

TS 1.9, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **matiśrutāvadhimanaḥparyayakevalāni jñānam**
> "Knowledge is of five kinds: sensory knowledge (*mati*), scriptural knowledge (*śruta*), clairvoyance (*avadhi*), telepathy (*manaḥparyāya*), and omniscience (*kevala*)."

These are not five unrelated faculties. They form an **ascending ladder of directness and reach**:

| # | Sanskrit | gloss | direct or indirect | what it reaches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | mati-jñāna | sensory / empirical | **parokṣa** (indirect) | objects via the five senses + mind |
| 2 | śruta-jñāna | scriptural / testimonial | **parokṣa** (indirect) | objects via language, symbol, scripture |
| 3 | avadhi-jñāna | clairvoyance | **pratyakṣa** (direct) | material objects, at a distance, bounded |
| 4 | manaḥparyāya-jñāna | telepathy | **pratyakṣa** (direct) | the thought-contents of other minds |
| 5 | kevala-jñāna | omniscience | **pratyakṣa** (direct) | all substances, all modes, all three times |

Two things to notice immediately, both developed below. First, the **dividing line falls between rungs 2 and 3**: the first two are "indirect," the last three "direct." Second — and this is the shock — **the senses are on the *indirect* side.** Hold that; §4 is entirely about it.

## 4. The signature: sensory knowledge is *indirect* — *parokṣa / pratyakṣa* {#paroksha-pratyaksha}

*→ concept file: [paroksha-jnana.md](../../concepts/paroksha-jnana.md)*

This is the single most distinctive claim in Jain epistemology, and the reason this chapter must be read against Chapter 05.

### 4.1 The division {#paroksha-division}

The five jñānas are sorted into two classes by *how the soul gets to the object*. TS 1.12, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018), settles the second class in two words:

> **pratyakṣam anyat**
> "The remaining [three] are direct (*pratyakṣa*)."

Pūjyapāda glosses: direct knowledge "manifests only in the soul on the destruction-cum-subsidence or destruction of karmas, **without the help of the senses**." So the division is:

| class | Sanskrit | members | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indirect | **parokṣa** | mati, śruta | the soul reaches the object *through an instrument* — sense organ, mind, or language |
| Direct | **pratyakṣa** | avadhi, manaḥparyāya, kevala | the soul apprehends the object *itself*, with no instrument between |

The criterion is purely about the **soul's** access. A sense organ is made of matter (*pudgala*); it is a tool external to the soul. Any knowledge that has to run through such a tool is, by definition, *mediated* — the soul is not in direct contact with the object, the eye is. So seeing, hearing, touching — all of ordinary empirical knowledge — is *parokṣa*, indirect. The soul only knows *directly* when it dispenses with the body's instruments entirely: clairvoyance, telepathy, omniscience.

### 4.2 Why this inverts everyone else {#paroksha-inversion}

Every other Indian school treats the words the opposite way. To them, *pratyakṣa* ("before-the-eyes") *is* sense perception — it is the very paradigm of directness. The Jains take the same word and exile sense perception from it.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *pratyakṣa* ≠ Nyāya *pratyakṣa* ≠ Dignāga's *pratyakṣa***
>
> The single term *pratyakṣa* names *opposite* things across these schools. This is the place where importing one school's usage into another does the most damage.
>
> | tradition | what counts as *pratyakṣa* (direct) | where sense perception lands |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Jain** | the **soul's** unmediated awareness — clairvoyance, telepathy, omniscience | *parokṣa* (indirect) — the sense organ mediates |
> | **Nyāya** | sense–object contact (*indriyārtha-sannikarṣa*) | *pratyakṣa* — the paradigm case of directness |
> | **Buddhist (Dignāga)** | non-conceptual, pre-verbal bare sensation | *pratyakṣa* — the only perception there is |
>
> For Nyāya and Dignāga, "direct" means *the object is right there at the sense*. For Jainism, "direct" means *the soul touches the object with nothing in between* — and a sense organ is precisely a something-in-between. So the very faculty that is the **gold standard** of directness for Nyāya and the Buddhists is, for Jainism, the **lowest, most mediated** rung. Same word, inverted ontology: what is at stake is *who the knower is*. If the knower is the embodied perceiver, the senses are direct. If the knower is the soul, the senses are a screen. (See Ch 05 for the Nyāya, Buddhist, and Mīmāṃsā accounts this inverts: [pramana-nyaya.md](../../concepts/pramana-nyaya.md), [dignaga-pramana.md](../../concepts/dignaga-pramana.md), [mimamsa-pramana.md](../../concepts/mimamsa-pramana.md).)

The deeper point: this is not a vocabulary accident the Jains could fix by relabelling. It is a *substantive metaphysical commitment*. To call sense perception "indirect" is to assert that the real subject of knowing is the soul, not the body — exactly the claim Buddhism denies (no soul) and Nyāya structures differently (self knows *through* the senses as its instruments). The terminology carries the whole soul-ontology with it.

## 5. The first rung — sensory knowledge — *mati-jñāna* {#mati}

*→ concept file: [mati-jnana.md](../../concepts/mati-jnana.md)*

### 5.1 TS 1.15 — the four stages {#mati-stages}

Mati-jñāna is what you get from the senses and the mind in contact with an object. Far from being a single flash, it is a structured four-step arc. TS 1.15, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **avagrahehāvāyadhāraṇāḥ**
> "Impression, inquisitiveness, comprehension, and retention [are the stages of sensory cognition]."

Pūjyapāda's worked example — a white shape across a field:

| # | stage | Sanskrit | what happens | the crane |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | impression | *avagraha* | raw sensory contact, before identifying | "something white, out there" |
| 2 | inquisitiveness | *īhā* | the wish to resolve it; weighing options | "a crane, or a white flag?" |
| 3 | comprehension | *avāya* | definite identification | "a crane — the wings are moving" |
| 4 | retention | *dhāraṇā* | the cognition fixed for later recall | "the same crane I saw this morning" |

Note that stages 2–4 are *conceptual* and *judgemental* — and yet the whole process is still classed as parokṣa. (This matters for the conflation alert below: Dignāga would deny that anything past stage 1 is "perception" at all.)

### 5.2 What it can reach — TS 1.26 {#mati-scope}

TS 1.26, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **matiśrutayor nibandho dravyeṣv asarvaparyāyeṣu**
> "The range of sensory and scriptural knowledge extends to all six substances but not to all their modes."

So mati can, in principle, get at *every kind of substance* — but never at the *infinite modes* (paryāyas) of any of them. Full access to the modes is reserved for omniscience (§9). This scope ceiling is shared exactly by śruta (§6).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *mati-jñāna* ≠ Dignāga's *pratyakṣa***
>
> It is tempting to line up "mati-jñāna = perception" with "Dignāga's pratyakṣa = perception" and call them the same faculty under two names. They are not. Dignāga's *pratyakṣa* is **non-conceptual** — bare sensation *only*, stripped of all naming and judging; the instant you say "that is a crane," you have left perception for inference/construction (*kalpanā*). Jain mati-jñāna is the **opposite shape**: it *includes* the naming and judging stages (*īhā, avāya, dhāraṇā*) as integral parts of one cognition — and the whole thing is still *parokṣa*, indirect. So the two terms disagree twice over: on whether perception is conceptual (Jain yes, Dignāga no) and on whether it is direct (Jain no, Dignāga yes). See [dignaga-pramana.md](../../concepts/dignaga-pramana.md).

## 6. The second rung — scriptural knowledge — *śruta-jñāna* {#shruta}

*→ concept file: [shruta-jnana.md](../../concepts/shruta-jnana.md)*

### 6.1 Knowledge through language {#shruta-def}

Śruta-jñāna is knowledge that arrives through *words* — heard, read, taught — rather than through direct sensory contact with a thing. It is the second parokṣa type, and it is **causally downstream of mati**: per Pūjyapāda on TS 1.20, "scriptural knowledge is preceded by sensory knowledge" (*śruta-jñāna* is preceded by *mati-jñāna*). (TS 1.20 is from the commentary, not independently fetched this run.) You cannot understand a sentence without first sensing the marks or sounds that carry it; śruta is mati put to work on meaning.

But śruta *extends* mati: properly understood testimony can convey realities the senses never touch — the existence of the soul, the structure of the cosmos, the mechanics of karma. It reaches further, but it is no more *direct*: language is still an instrument between the soul and the fact.

| feature | mati-jñāna | śruta-jñāna |
|---|---|---|
| trigger | sense/mind contact with an object | hearing/reading verbal content |
| medium | sense organs + manas | language, symbol, scripture |
| scope (TS 1.26) | all 6 substances, partial modes | all 6 substances, partial modes (same) |
| dependency | needs no prior jñāna | preceded by mati (TS 1.20) |
| reach beyond the senses | none | conveys trans-sensory realities via text |

### 6.2 Scripture is not knowledge — the *Samayasāra* clarification {#shruta-authority}

A sharp and important Jain move (Kundakunda's *Samayasāra*, via the Wikipedia "Jain epistemology" relay): **the text itself is not the knowledge.** The āgamas are *vehicles*, not guarantors. Reading a sūtra produces śruta-jñāna only if it generates *understanding* (*parijñāna*) in the learner; the cognition lives in the knower's own upayoga, not on the page. This blocks a naive "scripture is authoritative because it is scripture" view — even revealed knowledge has to become the knower's own cognitive act. (Jain scholastics further sort śruta into *aṅgapraviṣṭa*, the twelve canonical aṅgas, and *aṅgabāhya*, the literature outside them — a classification of the *vehicle*, not of the knowing.)

## 7. The third rung — clairvoyance — *avadhi-jñāna* {#avadhi}

*→ concept file: [avadhi-jnana.md](../../concepts/avadhi-jnana.md)*

With avadhi we cross the line from §4: this is the **first direct (pratyakṣa)** type — the soul apprehending without any sense organ. "Avadhi" means *bounded*, and the name is the whole point: this direct knowledge is real but **limited** — by substance, place, time, and mode — and it reaches **only physical matter** (*pudgala*). Souls, the media of motion and rest, space, and time are all outside its grasp.

### 7.1 Born with it, or earned — TS 1.21 {#avadhi-types}

TS 1.21, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **bhavapratyayo 'vadhir devanārakāṇām**
> "Clairvoyance based on birth (*bhava-pratyaya*) is possessed by celestial and infernal beings."

So avadhi comes two ways:

| type | Sanskrit | cause | who has it |
|---|---|---|---|
| birth-based | *bhava-pratyaya* | one's very mode of existence | gods (*deva*) and hell-beings (*nāraki*) — innate, unearned |
| merit-based | *guṇa-pratyaya* | destruction/subsidence of the obscuring karma | humans and animals — acquired through ascetic purification |

Pūjyapāda adds a crucial qualifier: gods and hell-beings get it "not because of any special merit … but by reason of their state of existence" — and only *right-believing* beings have *genuine* avadhi; wrong-believers have a vitiated, erroneous version. Directness of access does not guarantee correctness of view.

The earned (guṇa-pratyaya) form is itself graded — TS 1.23 lists six varieties by stability (does it persist when the knower moves? does its sphere grow or shrink?), and the extent ranges from *deśāvadhi* (partial) up to *sarvāvadhi* (total clairvoyance, available only to Tīrthaṅkaras).

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — *avadhi-jñāna* ≠ *kevala-jñāna***
>
> Both are "direct" (pratyakṣa), and both are extra-sensory, so they are easily blurred into "psychic knowledge." The gap between them is total. Avadhi is **bounded on every axis**: matter only, limited range, limited duration, partial modes. Kevala (§9) is **bounded on none**: all substances, all modes, all times, all space, at once. Avadhi can be lost (some varieties extinguish when you change location); kevala, once arisen, never recedes. Treating clairvoyance as a "lesser omniscience" misses that it is a different *kind* of knowing, not merely a smaller amount.

## 8. The fourth rung — telepathy — *manaḥparyāya-jñāna* {#manah-paryaya}

*→ concept file: [manah-paryaya-jnana.md](../../concepts/manah-paryaya-jnana.md)*

Manaḥparyāya is the second direct type, ranked **above** avadhi. The name means "modes of mind": the knower directly cognizes the **thought-objects present in another person's mind** — strictly, the physical modes (*pudgala-paryāya*) that the other mind is engaged with, not the mind-substance itself.

Its possession is the **narrowest** of all five jñānas: only **human ascetics**, in the karma-regions (*karma-bhūmi*), holding right faith and maintaining self-discipline. Gods, hell-beings, and animals — who *can* have avadhi — cannot have this. It arises by karma-purification (*guṇa-pratyaya*) alone; you cannot be born with it.

TS 1.23, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **ṛjuvipulamatī manaḥparyayaḥ**
> "Telepathy is of two kinds: *ṛjumati* and *vipulamati*."

| type | temporal reach | spatial reach | quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ṛjumati* | 2–8 births of self/others | up to ~1 yojana | simpler; **can be lost** |
| *vipulamati* | 7 to innumerable births | up to the Mānuṣottara range | purer; **never lost** (*apratipāta*) |

Vipulamati is the higher form precisely because it is *infallible and non-regressing* — once won, it stays.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — *manaḥparyāya* ≠ *avadhi***
>
> The two middle rungs both read "what's out there" without the senses, so they get flattened into "extra-sensory perception." Their *object-domains differ*: avadhi reads **physical objects directly**; manaḥparyāya reads **the physical modes being cognized inside another mind**. Avadhi looks at the world; manaḥparyāya looks at what another soul is thinking *about*. They also differ in who can have them (avadhi: gods, hell-beings, humans, animals; manaḥparyāya: human ascetics only). Ranking telepathy above clairvoyance is not about "more power" — it is about a rarer purification reaching a subtler object.

## 9. The fifth rung — omniscience — *kevala-jñāna* {#kevala}

*→ concept file: [kevala-jnana.md](../../concepts/kevala-jnana.md)*

Kevala-jñāna is the top of the ladder and the goal of the whole epistemology: **omniscience** — complete, unmediated, unobstructed cognition of *all substances in all their modes across all three times*, simultaneously. It is pramāṇa (§2.2) in its perfected form: the *sakalādeśa*, the total grasp, made fully actual in a single soul.

### 9.1 The condition: destroying the four ghātīya karmas {#kevala-condition}

Kevala-jñāna is not learned or accumulated; it is what remains when obstruction is removed. It arises the instant all four *destructive* (*ghātīya*) karmas are annihilated — jñānāvaraṇīya (knowledge-obscuring), darśanāvaraṇīya (perception-obscuring), mohanīya (deluding), antarāya (obstructive). This is exactly the event Ch 03 §9 placed at **guṇasthāna 13** (*sayoga-kevalī*): the soul becomes omniscient *while still embodied*, and stays so until the four non-destructive (*aghātīya*) karmas exhaust at death — which is mokṣa. (TS references 1.29 and 10.1 are cited via Wikipedia relay; not independently fetched this run.)

Recall the upayoga arc from §2.1: every soul is *always* doing jñāna, but karma-obscured. Kevala-jñāna is simply upayoga with the last obscuration gone — the soul knowing as it always could have, had nothing been in the way. Mokṣa, as Ch 03 §10 put it, is not the *acquisition* of omniscience but the *removal of everything that hid it*.

### 9.2 Contested: what is the omniscient body like? {#kevala-sects}

The two Jain traditions split sharply on the kevalin's physical condition (via Wikipedia "Kevala jnana"):

| reading | who holds it | what it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| The omniscient body is **transformed**: the kevalin is motionless, feels no hunger or thirst, sits in lotus posture, and teaches through *divyadhvani* (sacred sound not shaped by lips) | **Digambara** | normal embodiment is *incompatible* with omniscience |
| The kevalin **keeps ordinary human needs**: eats, travels, preaches, interacts with disciples | **Śvetāmbara** | omniscience is a *cognitive* state, not a bodily transformation |

This dispute (*kevalī-bhukti* — "does the omniscient one eat?") is one of the doctrinal fault-lines between the two sects, and will return in the chapter on the schism.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *kevala-jñāna* ≠ Vedāntic omniscience (*sarvajñatva*)**
>
> Both describe a knower of all things, and both use *sarvajña*. The structures are opposite. Jain kevala-jñāna is **achieved by an individual soul** through the removal of its own karma — it is a *result*, attained one liberated jīva at a time, and there are infinitely many omniscient siddhas, each distinct. Vedāntic omniscience is an **inherent attribute of the one absolute Being** (Brahman / Īśvara) — not earned, not multiplied, and on the Advaita reading not even ultimately an "individual's" knowing at all. Calling both "omniscience" hides the gulf between *a soul that became omniscient* and *Being that always was*. See [omniscience-vedanta.md](../../concepts/omniscience-vedanta.md).

## 10. The hidden discipline: which sense of the word? — *nikṣepa* {#nikshepa}

*→ concept file: [nikshepa.md](../../concepts/nikshepa.md)*

There is one more piece of the knowledge apparatus, and the TS puts it right at the front — *before* the five jñānas, immediately after listing the seven tattvas. It is the rule that you must fix *which sense of a term you are using* before you reason with it. TS 1.5, directly fetched (Vijay K. Jain 2018):

> **nāmasthāpanādravyabhāvatas tan nyāsaḥ**
> "These [tattvas] are installed (denoted) in four ways: by name, by representation, by substance(-in-potential), and by actual state."

Any word, the Jains hold, can point in four different registers; arguments go wrong when a speaker slides between them. Nikṣepa (also *nyāsa*, "placement") makes the registers explicit. Pūjyapāda's worked example uses the soul (*jīva*):

| register | Sanskrit | what it picks out | *jīva* example |
|---|---|---|---|
| name | *nāma* | the bare label, assigned regardless of qualities | a *person named* "Jīva" |
| representation | *sthāpanā* | a symbol/likeness — statue, image, token — taken as "this is that" | an *idol* standing for a soul |
| substance-in-potential | *dravya* | the bearer that *will* (or once did) have the qualities | a soul *not yet* manifesting its nature |
| actual state | *bhāva* | the substance in its **present mode**, actually in the condition named | a soul *actually conscious right now* |

So "is *that* a soul?" has four legitimate yes-answers depending on the register — and an argument that equivocates across them is confused, not deep. Nikṣepa is the **disambiguation layer beneath the rest of Jain method**: it tunes the *term*, naya (Ch 02) then tunes the *standpoint on the object*, and syādvāda assembles the standpoints. It is why the TS teaches it on the threshold, before anything else.

> Keep nikṣepa and naya distinct — a common slip. Naya asks *"from which valid angle is this real object being asserted?"* (substance-view vs mode-view). Nikṣepa asks the prior question *"which of the four things does this word even denote?"* (label / image / potential / actual). The *dravya/bhāva* pair in nikṣepa rhymes with the *dravyārthika/paryāyārthika* pair in naya — same substance/mode vocabulary — but they answer different questions. They are complementary tools, not two names for one tool. See [naya.md](../../concepts/naya.md).

## 11. The whole apparatus — a summary visual {#apparatus}

How the pieces of "right knowledge" (the second jewel) fit together:

```
RATNATRAYA (TS 1.1) — the one path
  ├── samyak-darśana  (right faith — Ch 03)
  ├── samyak-JÑĀNA    (right knowledge — THIS CHAPTER)
  └── samyak-cāritra  (right conduct — Ch 03)

samyak-jñāna is the soul's UPAYOGA (TS 2.8) in its jñāna (sākāra) mode,
  organised as PRAMĀṆA — complete cognition (sakalādeśa), TS 1.6
        │
        │  every term first disambiguated by NIKṢEPA (TS 1.5):
        │     nāma · sthāpanā · dravya · bhāva
        ▼
  THE FIVE JÑĀNAS (TS 1.9), one ladder:

   PAROKṢA (indirect — through an instrument)
     1. mati-jñāna   — senses + mind; 4 stages (TS 1.15); all dravyas, partial modes (TS 1.26)
     2. śruta-jñāna  — language/scripture; preceded by mati (TS 1.20)
   ──────────────  the inversion line: the senses are BELOW it  ──────────────
   PRATYAKṢA (direct — the soul itself, no instrument; TS 1.12)
     3. avadhi-jñāna       — matter only, bounded (TS 1.21)
     4. manaḥparyāya-jñāna — other minds; human ascetics only (TS 1.23)
     5. kevala-jñāna       — ALL substances/modes/times; ghātīya karma destroyed
                              → arises at guṇasthāna 13 → mokṣa (Ch 03)
```

The shape to carry away: **the more direct the knowing, the less the body is involved.** Ordinary sense knowledge is the *most* mediated thing a soul does; perfect knowledge is the soul touching reality with nothing in between. That single ranking is the whole of Jain epistemology, and it is upside-down relative to every school in Chapter 05.

## 12. Check yourself {#check}

**12.1** This chapter and Chapter 05 both use the words *pratyakṣa* and *parokṣa*. In Jainism, sense perception is *parokṣa* (indirect); in Nyāya and in Dignāga's Buddhism it is *pratyakṣa* (direct). The two sides are not making opposite empirical observations about perception — they agree on what seeing *is*. So what exactly are they disagreeing about? (Hint: ask *who the knower is* in each system.)

**12.2** Avadhi-jñāna and kevala-jñāna are *both* "direct" (pratyakṣa). Name three respects in which they differ. Then: a being has clairvoyance from birth — does that put it closer to liberation than an ordinary human who has only mati and śruta? Why or why not? (Hint: recall Pūjyapāda's qualifier about *who* has genuine avadhi.)

**12.3** Someone points at a statue of Mahāvīra and asks "is that the Tīrthaṅkara?" Using nikṣepa, give the register in which the answer is *yes* and the register in which the answer is *no* — and explain why an argument that ignores the distinction is equivocating rather than discovering a paradox.
