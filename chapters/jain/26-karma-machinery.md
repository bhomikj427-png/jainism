## How to use this chapter

The four concepts here were the Jain leftovers: nodes that Chapters 01–10 either never mentioned (**leśyā**, **kārmaṇa-vargaṇā**) or mentioned in passing without ever making primary (**mohanīya**, **ajīva**). Collected, they turn out not to be scraps. They are the **mechanism** underneath everything Chapter 03 described.

Chapter 03 gave you Jain soteriology as a sequence of tattvas: influx (*āsrava*), bondage (*bandha*), stoppage (*saṃvara*), shedding (*nirjarā*), liberation (*mokṣa*). That is the *narrative*. This chapter is the *engineering drawing* behind it, and it answers four questions the narrative leaves open:

| question | the answer | §|
|---|---|---|
| What category of thing is karma, if it is not the soul? | **ajīva** — non-soul; and specifically *pudgala*, matter | §2 |
| What is karma physically *made of*? | **kārmaṇa-vargaṇā** — the finest of eight grades of matter | §3 |
| What does bonded karma *do* to the soul's condition? | **leśyā** — it colours it, in six grades | §5 |
| Which karma-species actually keeps you bound? | **mohanīya** — deluding karma, the linchpin, with 28 sub-types | §6 |

Put those four together (§7) and you get a closed causal loop, which is the real content of the chapter: passion attracts matter, matter colours the soul, the colouring deludes, delusion generates more passion.

Two warnings shape how the chapter is written. First, **§4 is not optional** — the most systematic-looking material here (the eight-*vargaṇā* table) rests on the weakest sources in the chapter, and a reader who takes §3 at the same confidence as §2 will have been misled by the tidiness of a table. Second, **this is the corner of Jainism most exposed to §0's failure mode.** "Jain karma is literally particles" is true, startling, and one short step from "the Jains anticipated particle physics," which is false. §9 does that work explicitly.

Read after Ch 03 (soteriology) and Ch 09 (the six substances); Ch 01 (paramāṇu, skandha) helps in §3. Leave `> stuck` on its own line where you get lost; paste the teaching prompt and it will re-explain in place.

## 1. Karma is not a moral law {#not-a-law}

Start with the claim that makes Jain karma theory distinctive, because every section of this chapter is a consequence of it.

> **In Jainism, karma is fine physical matter** — *sūkṣma pudgala*, the "fine" *skandha* type — that flows into the soul, adheres to it, and conditions its embodiment, cognition, lifespan, and experienced pleasure and pain. **It is not an abstract moral law and not a residual intention. It is literally a class of infinitesimally fine particles.**

That is the corpus's gloss on [karma](../../concepts/karma.md), and it is worth registering how unusual it is. Most karma doctrines are theories of *desert* — an act now, a consequence later, with some principle (cosmic, divine, or psychological) connecting them. Jainism makes the connector a **substance**. What links the deed to its fruit is not a law but a quantity of stuff, which arrives, sticks, matures, and eventually falls away.

Everything follows mechanically from that:

- If karma is matter, it belongs to the **non-soul** side of the ontology → §2.
- If it is matter, it must be some *particular grade* of matter → §3.
- If matter adheres to the soul, the soul must be in some resulting **condition** → §5.
- And if the whole system is machinery, then one part of it must be the part that keeps the machine running → §6.

The five causes of bondage are given at **TS 8.1** — *mithyādarśanāviratipramādakaṣāyayogā bandhahetavaḥ*: wrong belief (*mithyā-darśana*), non-abstinence (*avirati*), negligence (*pramāda*), passions (*kaṣāya*), and activity of mind, speech and body (*yoga*). Pūjyapāda notes that these operate differently by guṇasthāna: all five in wrong-believers, and **only *yoga* remaining in advanced ascetics**. Hold that detail — it explains why even an omniscient being still accumulates something (§6.4).

## 2. Ajīva — the non-soul half of everything {#ajiva}

*→ concept file: [ajiva.md](../../concepts/ajiva.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium** (TS 5.1 and 5.39 fetched directly in a critical edition with commentary)*

**Ajīva** is *non-soul*: the collective term for everything lacking the defining mark of *jīva*, which is ***upayoga*** — consciousness. The *Tattvārtha Sūtra* uses the word at two different levels, and keeping them apart prevents a common muddle:

**As a tattva (TS 1.4).** One of the seven fundamental categories — jīva, **ajīva**, āsrava, bandha, saṃvara, nirjarā, mokṣa. At this level it simply covers everything that is not a conscious soul.

**As a dravya-class (TS 5.1 with 5.39).** The five non-soul substances collectively:

| Sanskrit | what it is | nature | *astikāya*? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **pudgala** | matter | has colour, taste, smell, touch; the locus of *paramāṇu*s and *skandha*s | yes |
| **dharma** | medium of motion | a passive enabling condition for movement (**not** moral dharma) | yes |
| **adharma** | medium of rest | a passive enabling condition for rest — unique to Jain ontology | yes |
| **ākāśa** | space | provides locus for all other dravyas | yes |
| **kāla** | time | enables sequence and change; one atom per space-point, non-combining | **no** |

**TS 5.1:** *ajīvakāyā dharmādharmākāśapudgalāḥ* — "The non-soul substances (bodies) are the medium of motion, the medium of rest, space, and matter." **Kāla is listed separately at TS 5.39** ("Time also is a substance") because it has no multiple space-points and therefore no *kāya*, no extension. Pūjyapāda: "Time is of the extent of one atom and pervades one space-point only."

### 2.1 A definition that is purely negative — and why that is elegant rather than lazy

Pūjyapāda's definition of ajīva is that "the characteristic mark (*lakṣaṇa*) of jīva is absent." That is all. No positive account is offered, and none is needed, because jīva has already been defined *positively* by *upayoga*. Everything without consciousness is ajīva by subtraction.

This is a genuinely economical piece of system-building: **one positive definition partitions the whole of reality.** Compare the alternative — defining "matter" positively, then "soul" as what matter is not — and notice that Jainism does it the other way round. Consciousness is the primitive; everything else is its complement. For a tradition whose entire soteriology is about disentangling a conscious thing from an unconscious one, that is the right primitive to choose.

### 2.2 Dharma and adharma — the strangest substances in Indian philosophy

Two of the five have no counterpart in any other major Indian school: **dharma-dravya** (medium of motion) and **adharma-dravya** (medium of rest), posited as *eternal, distinct substances* whose entire function is to make movement and rest possible.

They are **passive conditions** — *nimitta-kāraṇa*, occasioning causes — not active ones. The standard image: **water enables a fish to swim; it does not push the fish.** Remove the water and swimming becomes impossible, but the water never initiates anything.

> ⚠️ **Dharma-dravya is not moral dharma.** Identical word, unrelated concept: one is a physical medium of motion, the other is duty/law/righteousness (Ch 04, Ch 19). The corpus keeps them as separate nodes for this reason, and it is worth noticing that the collision is *inside* one tradition's vocabulary, not between traditions — the hardest kind to see.

### 2.3 Why ajīva belongs at the head of this chapter

Because it locates karma. Karma is *pudgala*; pudgala is *ajīva*; therefore **karma is on the non-soul side of the deepest partition in Jain metaphysics.** The soul's bondage is not the soul being morally stained. It is a conscious substance getting entangled with an unconscious one — and liberation is disentanglement, not forgiveness.

That is why Jain soteriology has the shape it has. There is nobody to forgive you, because there is no debt: there is only material adhering to a substance, which must be stopped (*saṃvara*) and shed (*nirjarā*). The absence of grace in classical Jainism is not austerity for its own sake; it is what this ontology permits.

> **A defect flagged, not fixed.** The `ajiva.md` node stores the edge `part-of: jiva` with the note "jīva and ajīva together constitute the complete ontology." The note is right and the edge is wrong — as stored, it asserts that *ajīva is part of jīva*, which inverts the very partition the file describes. The honest repair is not obvious, because the controlled vocabulary (§5 of CLAUDE.md) has no **complement-of** relation, and inventing one is a schema change rather than a typo fix. Recorded here as a follow-up for a maintenance pass rather than silently rewritten.

## 3. Kārmaṇa-vargaṇā — the eight grades of matter {#vargana}

*→ concept file: [karma-vargana.md](../../concepts/karma-vargana.md) — `converged`, confidence **low**. Read §4 before relying on anything in this section.*

A ***vargaṇā*** is a **class of pudgala aggregates defined by fineness of particle**: all particles of the same fineness-grade constitute one vargaṇā. **Kārmaṇa-vargaṇā** is the **finest of eight** — matter so subtle that it constitutes karma when it adheres to the soul.

The eight, from most subtle to most gross:

| # | vargaṇā | what it forms |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **kārmaṇa** | the **karma-body** — the fine matter that bonds to the soul in *bandha* |
| 2 | **taijas** | the *tejas* body — heat/energy body; digestive fire and aura |
| 3 | **āhāraka** | the *āhāraka* body — a subtle projection used by advanced monks to receive knowledge |
| 4 | **vaikrīya** | the transformable body of celestial and infernal beings |
| 5 | **bhāṣā** | the matter constituting **speech** |
| 6 | **manas** | the matter constituting **thought / mind** |
| 7 | **śvāsocchvāsa** | the matter constituting **breath** |
| 8 | **audārika** | the **gross body** of humans and animals — the visible physical one |

Three things about this list deserve attention.

**It is a single continuum, not a body/mind divide.** Speech, thought, and breath are *grades of matter* on the same scale as flesh. Jainism does not put mind on one side of a line and body on the other; it puts consciousness (*upayoga*, the mark of jīva) on one side, and grades everything else — including thought-stuff — by fineness. §2's partition again, doing real work.

**Karma is the finest grade of all.** Finer than mind, finer than speech, finer than the subtle bodies. That is the position the system needs: karma must be able to interpenetrate the soul's own space-points, so it has to be the most penetrating thing there is.

**The mechanism is specific.** When a soul acts **with passion** (*kaṣāya*), it attracts a "cluster of innumerable kārmaṇa particles." Those particles adhere via the *yoga* + *kaṣāya* mechanism, and once adhering they **are** karma. So the identity is exact: **karma *is* kārmaṇa-vargaṇā bonded to jīva.** Not caused by it, not represented by it — it is that matter, in that relation.

This is the layer that makes Jain karma theory a *physics*. Chapter 01 established *paramāṇu* (the partless ultimate unit) and *skandha* (aggregates); the vargaṇā scheme grades those aggregates by fineness, and then hands the finest grade to soteriology. The physics and the salvation-theory are one continuous account, joined at exactly this point.

## 4. Why §3 is marked `low` — and what that costs {#low}

The eight-vargaṇā table looks like the most rigorous thing in this chapter. It is the least well-sourced.

> **No critical scholarly source was fetched for it.** Not the *Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama*, not Vīrasena's *Dhavala*, not Nemicandra's *Dravyasaṃgraha*, not Jaini's *The Jaina Path of Purification*. The eight-vargaṇā list comes from **derivative Jain educational websites** — consistent with one another, and all of the same kind. The corpus's second signal was an *internal* cross-check against its own `karma.md`, which is consistency, not confirmation.

Three consequences, and the third is the one that matters.

**First, consistency across derivative sources is weak evidence.** Educational sites of this kind draw on a shared pool of modern Jain expository literature. Their agreement tells you the scheme is standard in that literature; it does not independently attest it. This is the same failure the corpus caught in Ch 25 §7.1, where two sources agreeing turned out to be two reports of one philologist.

**Second, and specifically: the eight-vargaṇā system is not where the rest of this project's Jain material comes from.** It is elaborated in the **Digambara** *Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama* and its *Dhavala* commentary (Vīrasena, ~9th c.), and in Nemicandra's *Dravyasaṃgraha* (~10th c.). The *Tattvārtha Sūtra* — this corpus's anchor text, and the one text both sects accept — **mentions kārmaṇa pudgala but does not systematise the eight-vargaṇā hierarchy.** And the scheme is not in the Śvetāmbara Āgamic canon in this form.

So §3 is **later Digambara technical physics**, presented in a chapter otherwise built on a shared root text. That does not make it wrong. It makes it a *particular school's elaboration*, roughly a millennium after the tradition's founding, and it should be read with the same care Ch 25 applied to attributions.

**Third — the honest cost.** The single most quotable fact in this chapter is that karma is the finest of eight grades of matter, and it is the fact this corpus can least well support. A reader who wants to build on it should treat the *ordering* and the *existence of a graded scheme* as reasonably safe, and treat the **precise eight-item list and its exact ordering** as pending verification against Nemicandra (*Dravyasaṃgraha* vv. 14–17, Soni's 2007 critical edition with translation) or the *Dhavala*.

That is a slightly deflating way to write a section, and it is the correct one. The alternative — presenting a well-formatted table and letting its formatting imply an authority the sources do not carry — is the specific failure this project exists to avoid.

## 5. Leśyā — the six colours of a soul {#leshya}

*→ concept file: [leshya.md](../../concepts/leshya.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium***

**Leśyā** is **the karmic colouring of the soul**: its psycho-physical complexion, resulting from its association with karma-matter.

The tradition's own image is a **crystal**: just as a crystal takes on the colour of whatever surrounds it, the soul's leśyā reflects the qualities — colour, taste, smell, touch — of the karma-matter presently bonded to it. Note that the crystal itself is not dyed. It shows the colour while remaining what it is, which is exactly the relation Jain soteriology needs between a soul and its karma.

The six, from most inauspicious to most auspicious:

| # | leśyā | colour | moral quality | destination |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **kṛṣṇa** | black | violent, uncompassionate, cruel | inauspicious → hell |
| 2 | **nīla** | blue | proud, lazy, deceitful | inauspicious → animal / plant |
| 3 | **kāpota** | grey | sad, jealous, unstable | inauspicious → lower human |
| 4 | **tejo** | red | discriminating, generous, benevolent | auspicious → human |
| 5 | **padma** | yellow | forgiving, austere, calm | auspicious → celestial |
| 6 | **śukla** | white | pure, meditative, liberated | auspicious → highest heaven, or liberation |

### 5.1 Both psychic and physical — and the reading that loses the doctrine

Leśyā is **simultaneously** a psychic state (a pattern of mental dispositions) and a physical indicator (the colouring of karma-matter associated with the soul). The sources do not treat this as an ambiguity to be resolved.

> ⚠️ **Two tempting resolutions both destroy the concept.** Read leśyā as *merely psychological* — "black leśyā" as a poetic name for a cruel disposition — and you have deleted the physics that the whole system runs on (§3): there would be nothing for the colour to be a colour *of*. Read it as *merely physical* — a literal tint on a literal substance, with the moral vocabulary as decoration — and you have deleted the reason anyone cares. The doctrine's content is that these are **one thing described from two sides**, which is only coherent in a system where mental states and matter-grades are already continuous (§3's single scale).

The concept file is careful here: it notes that whether leśyā is *primarily* psychic or *primarily* physical is a question present in the tradition, but that **Wikipedia does not flag it as a sharp scholarly dispute**, and most sources treat it as both at once. So the corpus does **not** mark the node `contested` — the presence of a question is not the same as an attested split, and manufacturing a controversy would be as dishonest as suppressing one.

### 5.2 Where leśyā correlates with the guṇasthāna ladder — and how firmly

Broadly: kṛṣṇa/nīla/kāpota map to the lower stages (1–3, false belief and vice); tejo/padma to the practice stages (4–12); śukla to the kevalin's condition (13–14) — perfectly pure leśyā, or its complete transcendence.

> ⚠️ **This correlation is the weakest claim in §5.** It comes from **search-result aggregation only** — it is *not* in the fetched Wikipedia article. It is reported here because it is useful and standard, and flagged because it has one derivative source.

Note the interesting wobble at the top: "perfectly pure leśyā *or its complete transcendence*." Those are different claims. A liberated *siddha* has **no leśyā at all** (the concept file is explicit: leśyā is an attribute of the jīva *in its saṃsāric state*), so the white leśyā of stage 13–14 is the last colouring before there is none — which is coherent, but the sources phrase it two ways and this chapter does not choose between them.

### 5.3 A source fact worth dwelling on

The six leśyās and their moral and rebirth correlations are fully elaborated in the **Uttarādhyayana Sūtra, Chapter 34** — a **Śvetāmbara canonical Āgama**. They are **not** in the *Tattvārtha Sūtra* in this form; the anchor text of this entire project references karma-types and their effects, but does not supply the colour-scheme.

So: §3 came from a **Digambara** source outside the anchor text, and §5 comes from a **Śvetāmbara** source outside the anchor text. §8 takes that up.

## 6. Mohanīya — the linchpin, and its twenty-eight parts {#mohaniya}

*→ concept file: [mohaniya.md](../../concepts/mohaniya.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium***

***Mohanīya*** ("deluding karma") is the fourth of the **eight karma species** (Ch 03's [karma-prakriti](../../concepts/karma-prakriti.md)), and the most consequential of all of them.

Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi* gives the definition by image: it is the karma that **"makes the soul intoxicated like alcohol."** It corrupts the soul's discriminating capacity, generating *moha* — delusion-attachment — which drives all further influx.

### 6.1 Why it is the linchpin

Of the eight species, four are ***ghāti*** — destructive, obscuring the soul's intrinsic qualities: *jñānāvaraṇa* (knowledge-obscuring), *darśanāvaraṇa* (perception-obscuring), *mohanīya* (deluding), and *antarāya* (obstructing). Mohanīya is the load-bearing member of that four:

> **Once mohanīya is fully destroyed, the other three ghāti karmas collapse almost at once, and omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*) dawns.**

That is a strong structural claim and it has a clean rationale. Knowledge-obscuring karma is *maintained* by the influx that delusion drives; remove the delusion and the mechanism that keeps the obscuration replenished is gone. Mohanīya is not merely the worst of the four — it is the one holding the other three in place.

Which yields the chapter's most elegant single sentence, and it is the concept file's own:

> **The guṇasthāna ladder is essentially the staged demolition of mohanīya's 28 sub-types.**

The fourteen-stage ladder of spiritual progress (Ch 03) is not a sequence of achievements laid alongside the karma theory. It **is** the karma theory, read as a schedule: at each rung, a specified block of deluding karma is eliminated. Stages and sub-types are two views of one structure.

### 6.2 The twenty-eight

Mohanīya divides into two branches.

**Branch 1 — *darśana-mohanīya* (3 sub-types): clouds right faith.**

| sub-type | what it does |
|---|---|
| *mithyātva* | wrong belief; inverts the soul's grasp of truth |
| *samyaktva-mithyātva* | mixed right-and-wrong belief; unstable oscillation |
| *samyaktva* (subsidential) | a quiescent state where wrong belief is **suppressed but not eradicated** |

The third entry repays a second look. A *karma sub-type* whose effect is that things are going well — right belief, achieved by suppression rather than destruction — is still deluding karma, because suppression is unstable and can fail. The system will not credit you for a good state that has a mechanism of relapse still in place.

**Branch 2 — *cāritra-mohanīya* (25 sub-types): clouds right conduct.**

*Kaṣāya-vedanīya*, passion-causing — **16**, being four passions × four intensities. **TS 8.9:** *anantānubandhyapratyākhyānapratyākhyānasaṃjvalana vikalpāścaikaśaḥ krodhamānamāyālobhāḥ*.

| intensity | what it blocks | eliminated at guṇasthāna |
|---|---|---|
| ***anantānubandhī*** (infinite-binding) | right faith itself | **4** — right faith arises |
| ***apratyākhyāna*** (prevents partial renunciation) | lay vows | **5** — the *aṇuvrata* level |
| ***pratyākhyāna*** (prevents full renunciation) | monastic vows | **6** — the *mahāvrata* level |
| ***saṃjvalana*** ("smouldering") | nothing outright; residual passion delays mokṣa | **10** — only subtle passion remains |

Each intensity applies to all four *kaṣāyas* — *krodha* (anger), *māna* (pride), *māyā* (deceit), *lobha* (greed): 4 × 4 = **16**.

*No-kaṣāya-vedanīya*, quasi-passion-causing — **9**: *hāsya* (laughter), *rati* (liking), *arati* (disliking), *śoka* (sorrow), *bhaya* (fear), *jugupsā* (disgust), and the three *vedas* — *strī* (female), *puṃ* (male), *napuṃsaka* (neuter) sex-passion. These go at guṇasthāna **12**, before mohanīya's final eradication.

**3 + 16 + 9 = 28**, matching `karma-prakriti.md`'s count of mohanīya's share of the traditional 148 *uttara-prakṛti*s.

### 6.3 What the four intensities actually encode

The most philosophically interesting thing here is the intensity scheme, because it is a claim about how moral failure is structured.

Anger is not one thing that you have more or less of. It comes in **four grades that block four different things**, and they are eliminated in a fixed order across six rungs of the ladder. The anger that prevents you from even seeing correctly (*anantānubandhī*) goes first, at stage 4. The anger that prevents lay vows goes at 5. The anger that prevents monastic vows goes at 6. And a "smouldering" residue of anger persists all the way to stage 10 — in a practitioner who has held full monastic vows for a lifetime.

Two implications. **The same-named passion recurs at every level of attainment**, which is a strikingly unflattering and psychologically credible picture of spiritual progress. And **the grades are defined functionally, by what they obstruct**, not by felt intensity — the criterion is not how angry you feel but which capacity the anger forecloses.

### 6.4 One loose end, honestly reported

Chapter 03's ladder ends at stage 14 with all karma gone. But TS 8.1 (§1) says that in advanced ascetics **only *yoga* remains** as a cause of bondage — activity of mind, speech, and body, without passion. So influx does not stop when passion does: a kevalin still acts, and activity still attracts. What that residual influx amounts to, and how it is discharged, is *aghāti*-karma territory — the four non-destructive species — which this chapter does not cover because none of the four nodes here treat it. Flagged rather than smoothed: the machinery described here is the *ghāti* half of the machine.

## 7. The loop {#loop}

Now assemble it. The four concepts of this chapter, plus Ch 03's tattvas, form a **cycle** — and seeing it as a cycle rather than a sequence is the point.

| step | what happens | concept |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The soul acts through mind, speech, or body — ***yoga*** — while under the influence of passion — ***kaṣāya*** | Ch 03 |
| 2 | That activity attracts a cluster of the finest grade of matter: ***kārmaṇa-vargaṇā*** | §3 |
| 3 | The particles adhere — ***bandha*** — and thereby *become* karma; the four aspects (*prakṛti, sthiti, anubhāga, pradeśa*, TS 8.3) fix their species, duration, intensity and quantity | Ch 03 |
| 4 | The soul now shows the complexion of what is bonded to it: ***leśyā***, one of six colours | §5 |
| 5 | Among the species bonded, ***mohanīya*** intoxicates the soul's discrimination, generating *moha* | §6 |
| 6 | Deluded discrimination produces **more passion** — and passion is step 1 | ↺ |

That is a self-sustaining loop, and it explains the *shape* of the Jain path. If the cycle is closed, you cannot exit by acting better within it: better action still involves *yoga*, and *yoga* under passion still attracts matter. The exits have to be cuts into the loop itself, which is precisely what the remaining tattvas are:

- ***saṃvara*** (stoppage) cuts the loop at **step 1–2** — restrain activity and passion, and the influx stops.
- ***nirjarā*** (shedding) removes what is already bonded, at **step 3** — which is why Jain *tapas* is austerity aimed at burning off existing karma rather than earning credit.
- and the **guṇasthāna ladder** is the schedule on which step 5's twenty-eight sub-types are demolished (§6.1).

Read this way, the famously severe Jain ascetic discipline stops looking like extremism and starts looking like the only intervention the model permits. **If bondage is a physical process driven by activity-plus-passion, then reducing activity and eliminating passion is not a pious extra — it is the mechanism.** *Sallekhanā*, the fast to death (Ch 08), is the limiting case of the same logic: activity reduced to zero.

## 8. Three canons, one chapter {#canons}

Here is the chapter's methodological finding, and it emerged from the sourcing rather than being looked for.

The four concepts here come from **three different textual strands**:

| concept | where the doctrine actually lives | sect | fetched? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ajīva** | *Tattvārtha Sūtra* 5.1, 5.39, with Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi* | **both** (the shared text) | **yes**, critical edition |
| **mohanīya** | *Tattvārtha Sūtra* 8.4, 8.9, with commentary | **both** | **yes**, critical edition |
| **leśyā** | ***Uttarādhyayana Sūtra* Ch. 34** | **Śvetāmbara** Āgama | **no** |
| **kārmaṇa-vargaṇā** | ***Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama*** + Vīrasena's *Dhavala*; Nemicandra's *Dravyasaṃgraha* | **Digambara** | **no** |

The pattern is not a coincidence, and it has two lessons.

**First: the anchor text has holes, and they are systematic.** The *Tattvārtha Sūtra* is the corpus's anchor precisely because **both sects accept it** — which is exactly why doctrine developed *after* the sects diverged, or *within* one of them, is not in it. Ch 03 could be built almost entirely on the TS. This chapter could not: half of it lives in sectarian canons. Where a project anchors on a shared text, it will systematically under-represent whatever is not shared, and the gap will look like an absence of doctrine rather than an artefact of the choice.

**Second: "Jainism holds that…" is doing more work than it can bear.** The eight vargaṇās are Digambara technical physics, roughly a millennium after Mahāvīra. The six leśyās are in a Śvetāmbara Āgama. A sentence beginning "in Jain doctrine, karma is the finest of eight classes of matter and colours the soul in six grades" silently fuses two sectarian traditions and one shared root text into a single voice that no Jain author ever had. Every sentence in §§3 and 5 of this chapter should be read as **"in this strand of the tradition."**

This is the same lesson Ch 24 §4.4 drew about the Chaldean Oracles and Ch 25 §10 drew about the commentary anchor, arriving from a third direction. **Name the channel, and size the claim to it.** And note the specific asymmetry here: the two concepts on the shared text reached *medium* confidence with fetched critical editions; the two on sectarian texts reached *medium* and *low* with nothing fetched. Sectarian material is harder to source, so it gets weaker sourcing, so it should carry weaker claims — and instead it tends to get repeated most confidently, because the eight-vargaṇā table is the most memorable thing in the chapter.

## 9. The inflation this chapter is most exposed to {#inflation}

Now the §0 work, because no chapter in this corpus sits closer to the failure mode than this one.

The claim "**Jain karma is literally particles**" is *true*, well-attested for the general thesis, and irresistible to a certain kind of comparative writing. From there the slide is short: karma-particles → subatomic particles → "the Jains knew about particle physics." That inference is wrong at every joint, and the corpus already carries the edges that break it.

**On the atom itself**, Ch 01 and the [paramanu](../../concepts/paramanu.md) node store `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent: modern-atom` with the reasons stated plainly:

| Jain *paramāṇu* | modern atom |
|---|---|
| **partless** — indivisible by definition | **has parts** — protons, neutrons, electrons, quarks |
| **posited a priori**, by an infinite-regress argument | **found empirically** |
| carries colour, taste, smell, touch as **intrinsic properties** | has none of these intrinsically |

Three separate breaks: *mereological* (partless vs structured), *epistemic* (argued vs discovered), and *qualitative* (sensory properties built in vs not). And the third one is decisive for *this* chapter specifically — because **leśyā depends on it**. The soul takes on the colour of the karma-matter bonded to it (§5), which requires that matter to have colour intrinsically, at the finest grade. That is precisely the property modern matter does not have. **The doctrine that makes leśyā work is the doctrine that makes the modern identification impossible.**

Two further breaks belong to the vargaṇā scheme itself:

> ⚠️ **The eight vargaṇās are not a particle taxonomy.** They are graded by *fineness*, and the grades are defined by **function in a soteriology** — this grade makes the gross body, that grade makes speech, this one bonds to souls. A modern taxonomy of matter is not organised by what each kind does for a soul, because it has no soul-shaped slots. The organising principle, not just the content, is different in kind.

> ⚠️ **"Karma is matter" is not a physical hypothesis.** It is not offered as an explanation of any observable, it makes no measurable prediction, and no observation would count against it. It is an **ontological commitment** doing work in a theory of bondage and release. Reading it as proto-physics misidentifies what kind of claim it is — and, in doing so, makes it *worse*, since as physics it is empty, while as ontology it is a coherent and unusual position.

Note what is *not* being said. The Jain position is genuinely materialist about karma in a way most Indian karma doctrines are not, and that is a real and interesting fact. The corpus records `paramanu structurally-parallel-to paramanu-vaisheshika` — Jain and Vaiśeṣika atomisms *are* honestly comparable to each other, both positing partless ultimate units on an infinite-regress argument (Ch 16). **Comparison within the period is often solid; comparison across the modern break is where the inflation happens.** The right test is the one Ch 24 §8.3 arrived at: ask what each posit *does* in its own system. Jain paramāṇus explain how a soul gets entangled. Modern atoms explain spectra and stoichiometry. Nothing that answers those two questions could be one thing.

### 9.1 Three karmas, three mechanisms

A quick honest comparison to close, because "karma" is exactly the sort of shared word that flattens three doctrines into one:

| | **Jain** (Ch 03, here) | **Vedic/Hindu** (Ch 04) | **Buddhist** (Ch 12) |
|---|---|---|---|
| what karma **is** | **fine matter** — *kārmaṇa-vargaṇā* — bonded to the soul | act-and-consequence within *ṛta*/dharma; ritual action generating result | **volitional action** (*cetanā*) conditioning future experience |
| what carries it | the *jīva*, a permanent substance | the ātman / the moral order | no permanent self — *paṭiccasamuppāda*'s conditioned continuity |
| how it ends | **shed physically** (*nirjarā*) by austerity and stoppage | exhausted, transferred, or transcended by knowledge/devotion | ends when craving ends; the conditioning stops arising |
| intention required? | **no** — *yoga* alone attracts matter; passion determines the *bond*'s severity | varies by school | **yes** — intention is definitional |

> ⚠️ **The last row is the sharpest break, and it is often reversed in popular accounts.** Buddhism makes intention *constitutive* of karma. Jainism does not: activity attracts matter whether or not you meant anything, which is why Jain *ahiṃsā* (Ch 08) extends to inadvertent harm and why the discipline is about restricting *activity*, not just purifying motive. Attributing the Buddhist intention-criterion to Jainism dissolves the entire rationale for Jain ascetic practice.

## 10. Why this matters {#why}

**Because a mechanism explains a practice that otherwise looks like excess.** Jain asceticism has a reputation for severity that invites psychological or sociological explanation. §7 shows it needs neither: given a closed loop in which activity-plus-passion attracts adhering matter, restricting activity and eliminating passion is simply the intervention the model licenses. Understand the machinery and the practice stops being strange.

**Because this is a system whose ethics and physics are one theory.** In most traditions, the account of what the world is made of and the account of how to live are separable — you can hold the ethics and drop the cosmology. Here you cannot. Leśyā requires matter with intrinsic colour; nirjarā requires karma to be a substance that can be burned off; ahiṃsā's extension to inadvertent harm requires that matter adhere without intention. Pull the physics out and the ethics loses its rationale. That is rare, and it is worth seeing clearly before deciding what one thinks of it.

**Because §4 and §8 are the chapter's real contribution to the method.** The best-formatted material here is the worst-sourced, and the sourcing gaps fall along a sectarian fault-line rather than at random. Both facts are invisible if you read only the tables. This chapter tries to make the tables and their warrant visible at the same time — which is the discipline the whole project runs on, applied to itself.

**And because §9 is where the prime directive earns its keep.** "Ancient Indians said matter is made of particles" is the most quotable sentence available here and the most misleading. What the Jains actually built is stranger and better than a proto-physics: an ontology in which consciousness is the sole positive category, everything else is graded by fineness, and salvation is a materials problem.

## 11. Flags {#flags}

| flag | why the resemblance is tempting | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Jain karma ≠ a moral law** | every tradition's "karma" is a desert-principle | in Jainism it is **fine matter** — a substance, not a principle; the connector between deed and fruit is stuff |
| **Jain karma ≠ Buddhist karma** | shared word, shared broad function | **intention is definitional** in Buddhism and **not required** in Jainism; *yoga* alone attracts matter |
| **Jain karma ≠ Vedic karma** | both are act-and-consequence across lives | Vedic karma operates within *ṛta*/dharma on a permanent ātman; Jain karma is particulate influx onto a jīva, shed **physically** by austerity |
| **paramāṇu ≠ modern atom** | both are "the smallest unit of matter" | **partless vs structured**; **a priori vs empirical**; and *paramāṇu* carries **colour, taste, smell, touch intrinsically** — the very property leśyā requires and modern matter lacks |
| **the eight vargaṇās ≠ a particle taxonomy** | it is a graded, systematic classification of matter | graded by **fineness** and defined by **soteriological function** (this grade bonds to souls, that grade makes speech); the organising principle has no modern counterpart |
| **"karma is matter" ≠ a physical hypothesis** | it sounds like an empirical claim | it predicts nothing and no observation bears on it; it is an **ontological commitment** inside a theory of bondage |
| **dharma-dravya ≠ moral dharma** | identical word | a **passive medium of motion**; the collision is inside one tradition's own vocabulary, which makes it harder to see |
| **dharma/adharma ≠ forces** | they "enable" motion and rest | ***nimitta-kāraṇa*** — passive occasioning conditions. Water lets the fish swim; it does not push it |
| **leśyā ≠ a metaphor for mood** | the moral vocabulary is vivid and psychological | reading it as *merely* psychological deletes the karma-matter the colour is a colour **of** |
| **leśyā ≠ a literal tint with decorative morals** | the crystal analogy is concretely physical | reading it as *merely* physical deletes the point; it is **one thing described from two sides** |
| **white leśyā ≠ the liberated state** | it is the highest of the six | the *siddha* has **no leśyā at all** — leśyā belongs to the jīva in *saṃsāra*; the sources phrase the top rung two ways and the corpus does not choose |
| **a well-formatted table ≠ a well-sourced one** | §3's eight-vargaṇā list is the tidiest thing here | it rests on **derivative educational websites**, no critical edition — the chapter's `low`-confidence node |
| **consistency across sources ≠ independence** | the vargaṇā sites all agree | they draw on a shared pool of modern expository literature; agreement shows a scheme is *standard*, not that it is *attested* |
| **"Jainism holds…" ≠ one voice** | the four concepts read as one system | ajīva and mohanīya are from the **shared** *Tattvārtha Sūtra*; leśyā is **Śvetāmbara** Āgama; the vargaṇās are later **Digambara** physics |
| **a present question ≠ an attested dispute** | the psychic/physical question about leśyā is real | no source flags it as a scholarly split, so the node is **not** marked contested — manufacturing a controversy is as dishonest as hiding one |
