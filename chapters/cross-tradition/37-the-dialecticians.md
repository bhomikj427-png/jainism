# Chapter 37 — The Dialecticians: What Refutation Is For

> **Primary concepts:** `udayana` · `śrīharṣa` · `ānandabodha` · `citsukha` · `nṛsiṃhāśrama` · `malliṣeṇa` · `vyāsatīrtha` · `madhusūdana-sarasvatī`
> **Folder:** `cross-tradition/` — because the eight span Nyāya, Advaita, Dvaita **and** Jainism, and the chapter's thesis is about a *shared technique*, not a school.
> **Reads after:** [Ch 34 (The Commentator)](34-the-commentator.md) and [Ch 36 (Founders and the Lineages)](../hindu/darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md). Ch 34 asked what a commentator does *for* a text; this chapter asks what an author does *against* one.

---

## How to use this chapter

Indian philosophy is argued in an adversarial form. A treatise states the opponent's position (*pūrvapakṣa*), demolishes it, and states its own (*siddhānta*). Every text in this chapter lives at the demolition step, and the eight men here occupy every available position on one question:

> **Does refuting your opponent commit you to a view of your own?**

They answer it differently, and — this is the chapter's real subject — **their answers determined what happened to them afterwards.** A man who refutes without asserting can be claimed by a school he may never have joined. A man who builds after clearing cannot. A man who transmits faultlessly and adds nothing gets praised for it and forgotten.

Four things to carry out:

1. **The spectrum**, §2 — eight positions on one axis, and what each costs.
2. **The rule**: *what a polemicist declines to assert determines who can later claim him.*
3. **A five-century argument** that is still running (§6) — and what it means that we can say that.
4. **The avidyā quarrel with both ends in the corpus** for the first time (§4).

⚠ One caution before starting. It is easy to read this chapter as being about *cleverness* — Indian philosophy's tradition of dazzling technical destruction. That is the least interesting thing here and the popular literature already over-supplies it. The chapter is about **evidence**: what a body of refutation lets a later reader know, and what it makes unknowable.

| # | who | when | tradition | the work | position on the axis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Udayana** | disputed, §3 | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | *Nyāyakusumāñjali*; *Lakṣaṇāvalī* | **builds** — the definitions everyone else attacks |

## 2. The spectrum, and what each position costs {#spectrum}

### 2.1 Śrīharṣa: assert nothing, and be claimed by anyone {#sriharsa}

**Śrīharṣa**'s *Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya* — "The Sweets of Refutation" — attacks the **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika method of definition** itself. His instrument is the *khaṇḍana-yukti*, the refutation-argument, and he holds a definition to three requirements:

1. **extensional adequacy** — conditions both necessary and sufficient;
2. **non-circularity** — it must not presuppose what it defines;
3. **uniformity** — a *single* property holding across all instances.

Applied to *pramāṇa* itself, the result is that "there may not after all be any satisfactory definition of knowledge."

**And he never says what he thinks.** SEP (Nilanjan Das): "Śrīharṣa didn't affiliate himself explicitly to any philosophical text tradition active in classical India."

The consequence is that his affiliation is a live scholarly dispute eight centuries later:

| reading | who |
|---|---|
| **an Advaitin** — the *Khaṇḍana* defends non-dualism by indirect means | Phillips (1995, 1997); Ram-Prasad (2002) |
| **not a standard Advaitin** — he rejects the provisional reality of the world, and holds the object of awareness non-distinct from awareness | Granoff (1978) |
| **Granoff is half right** — the second departure holds, the first does not | Das (SEP, 2018) |
| **affiliated with nothing** | Das, as a statement about the text |

> **A book made entirely of refutations has no thesis to read off.** Its author's position must be inferred from what he declines to defend — and that kind of evidence supports more than one reconstruction. **This is not a gap in the scholarship. It is a property of the genre.**

The reception proves the point better than the argument does. **Advaitins adopted him** — Citsukha wrote the *Khaṇḍanabhāvadīpikā* on it, Ānandapūrṇa the *Khaṇḍanaphakkikāvibhajana*. **Naiyāyikas answered him at length** — Vardhamāna, Śaṃkara Miśra, Pragalbha, Gokulanātha from Mithilā; Raghudeva and Raghunātha Sārvabhauma from Bengal. A single book drawing a refutatory literature from the school it attacked *and* a devotional-commentarial literature from the school that claimed it is **the strongest evidence that both readings had something to work with.**

⚠ **Two things deliberately not said**, and both are corrections the corpus made against its own first instinct.

- **Not *vitaṇḍā*.** "Refutation without a counter-thesis" is the Nyāya name for exactly this manoeuvre, and it was the term this node was expected to carry. **SEP states the text does not use it.** So it is recorded as a *description that fits* and an *attribution not made*. Calling Śrīharṣa a *vaitaṇḍika* would put a technical — and pejorative — classification in his mouth on no authority.
- **Not "rejects reason entirely in favour of faith and the Upaniṣads"** (Wikipedia). SEP describes something far narrower: pessimism about whether **definitions** can be given. *Pessimism about definability is not rejection of reason*, and the slide between them is the [Ch 28](../comparanda/28-modern-physics.md) move in miniature.

### 2.2 Citsukha: destroy, then define anyway {#citsukha}

**Citsukha** kept Śrīharṣa's apparatus and then did the thing Śrīharṣa refused. **Dasgupta**: Citsukha "had all Śrīharṣa's powers of acute dialectical thought" but went further by **formally defining key Vedānta concepts, not merely refuting Nyāya positions** — *svaprakāśa* as "that which is entitled to be called immediate, though it is not an object of any cognition"; *mithyātva* as "the non-existence of a thing in that which is considered to be its cause."

That is a **cost accepted deliberately.** A definition is a target; Śrīharṣa's whole book is an argument that no definition survives contact. Citsukha supplies definitions knowing that.

**And the corpus can now say this with unusual confidence**, because two independent sources converge on it: **Dasgupta (1932)** and **Nilanjan Das (SEP, 2018)** — different authors, 86 years apart, writing about *different men* — both record that Citsukha took Śrīharṣa's arguments further in the *Tattvapradīpikā*. Neither is derivable from the other.

### 2.3 Ānandabodha: the arsenal {#anandabodha}

Behind Śrīharṣa stands the man whose arguments the Advaita school actually ran on. **Dasgupta**: "**Most of Ānandabodha's arguments were borrowed by the later writers of the Vedānta school.**"

Two of them, and both matter downstream:

**Against difference** — "Difference cannot be perceived by sense-perception, since difference cannot be perceived without perceiving both the object and all else from which it differs."

Set that beside Śrīharṣa's sharpest claim: *no awareness can present one object as distinct from another without also presenting them as non-distinct.* **The same move, one generation apart, tightened.** §2.1 said the destructive method "visibly points somewhere" exactly here; Ānandabodha is the generation it points back to.

**And avidyā** — "the cause of world-appearance cannot be either real or unreal … it must have for its cause something which is neither real nor unreal … the **neither-real-nor-unreal** entity is *avidyā*." §4 takes this up.

### 2.4 Nṛsiṃhāśrama: transmit, and add nothing {#nrisimhasrama}

The opposite extreme, and the corpus's most surprising case. **Nṛsiṃhāśrama** (16th c.) commented his way across the **entire Vivaraṇa stack** — Padmapāda's *Pañcapādikā*, Prakāśātman's *Vivaraṇa*, Sarvajñātman's *Saṃkṣepaśārīraka* — and wrote the *Bhedadhikkāra*, "Condemnation of Difference," against the Madhva school.

His own tradition's verdict, recorded in `nrisimhasrama.md`, is that he "has not put forward any new interpretation … **yet as a commentator he is superb**."

> **That is a real position on the axis, not an absence of one.** Faithful transmission is a choice with a cost — invisibility — and the tradition that made it has a vocabulary of praise for it. Ch 34's list of commentarial operations needed this row: *add nothing, accurately.*

⚠ **And a caution about how he entered the corpus.** He was queued on a claim that **did not survive checking**: three sources with every occasion to link him to Appayya Dīkṣita's *Parimala* do not mention the other man. Both `nrisimhasrama.md` and `appayya-dikshita.md` were qualified. **A batch that never falsifies its own queue is not checking it** — compare Ch 35 §9.3, where "Candraṭa was Vāgbhaṭa's grandson" failed the same way.

### 2.5 Malliṣeṇa: refute six schools on behalf of a doctrine about refutation {#mallisena}

**Malliṣeṇa Sūri**, a Śvetāmbara monk, wrote the *Syādvādamañjarī* — "a cluster of flowers of the *syādvāda* doctrine" — a commentary on Hemacandra's thirty-two-verse *Anyayogavyavacchedadvātriṃśikā*, arguing against Vaiśeṣika, Nyāya, Vedānta, Sāṃkhya, Buddhism and Cārvāka.

He is here for two reasons beyond making the chapter cross-traditional.

**First — the position is philosophically distinctive.** The Jain defends *anekāntavāda* and *syādvāda*: the doctrine that reality is many-sided and that **every assertion is conditional**. So Malliṣeṇa's refutation of six schools is not "you are wrong and I am right" but something closer to "**each of you has absolutised one standpoint.**" A dialectic conducted on behalf of a theory about the limits of assertion sits at an angle to every other entry in §1's table. ([Ch 02](../jain/02-jain-epistemology.md) has the doctrine.)

**Second — the colophon.** The work was completed **"on Saturday, Āśvina Kṛṣṇa Amāvāsyā in the Śaka year 1214"** — **1292 CE**, exactly the date **Dasgupta** independently assigned in 1922.

⚠ Set that against everything [Ch 35](../hindu/shastra/35-dating-a-literature-without-dates.md) had to do to bracket seven Āyurvedic authors, and against [Ch 36 §2](../hindu/darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md#lifespans)'s three 120-year lifespans. **Malliṣeṇa names the weekday.** Where a colophon like this survives, the dating problem simply does not arise — which is a useful reminder that **the discipline's chronic vagueness is a fact about what survived, not about how these authors wrote.**

And the same clause carries something the corpus should not skip: the work was completed **"with the help of Jinaprabhasūri."** The commentary is, by its own statement, **not the work of one man alone** — complicating the genre's working picture of one author, one base text, one gloss, in the very first colophon this corpus read directly.

### 2.6 Vyāsatīrtha: demolition precedes construction {#vyasatirtha}

**Vyāsatīrtha**, *rājaguru* of Vijayanagara, "the Prince of Dialecticians of the Dvaita school." B. N. K. Sharma is emphatic that the *Nyāyāmṛta*'s "object and purpose is **not purely negative or destructive. Demolition precedes construction.** The realistic metaphysics rises on the ashes of the Monistic Advaita." Its four *paricchedas* run through *pañcabheda*, bondage, the *sādhana*s, bhakti and grace, and the graded bliss of mokṣa.

**Set him against Śrīharṣa and the chapter's rule falls out.** Śrīharṣa's silence made him claimable by a school he may not have joined; Vyāsatīrtha's constructive half makes his allegiance unmistakable and uninteresting to argue about.

> **Rule. What a polemicist declines to assert determines who can later claim him.**

### 2.7 What the *Nyāyāmṛta* actually is {#collection}

And now a result that two independent sources reach from opposite ends. **The *Nyāyāmṛta*'s method is collection.**

**Dasgupta** (1932): Vyāsatīrtha "collected standard Vedānta arguments from **Ānandabodha and Prakāśātman** for refutation."
**Mm. Anantakrishna Sastri** — who edited the main works of *both* sides for the Calcutta Oriental Series, and so is the least partisan witness available: "It was Vyāsatīrtha who, for the first time, took special pains to **collect together from the vast range of Advaita literature all the crucial points for discussion** … He also exhibited in his work **more than a hundred points of departure**."

And Vyāsatīrtha describes his own aim identically — gathering "the threads of doctrine and details of Madhva Siddhānta, found piecemeal and scattered here and there … and re-presenting them in a co-ordinated form."

**So the *Nyāyāmṛta* is a double compilation**: it assembles the Advaita case so as to have something worth refuting, and the Dvaita case so as to have something to refute it with.

⚠ Which makes it **a source for Advaita, not only an attack on it** — and that is exactly why [Ch 36 §5.2](../hindu/darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md#madhva-verdict) can use the *Nyāyāmṛta*'s quotations as evidence in the Madhva authenticity dispute. It is also [Ch 35's Rule 3](../hindu/shastra/35-dating-a-literature-without-dates.md#archive) again, in the fourth literature it has now been seen in: **compilation is how positions survive.**

## 3. Udayana, and the verse that dates its own author {#udayana}

The definitions everyone in §2 is attacking are largely **Udayana**'s. His *Lakṣaṇāvalī*, "Garland of Definitions," is the *Khaṇḍana*'s principal target; he is the culminating figure of **old Nyāya**, and Gaṅgeśa calls him simply *Ācāryaḥ*, the Master.

He also supplies this chapter's best cautionary tale about evidence.

The *Lakṣaṇāvalī* carries a verse dating it to **Śaka 906 = 984/985 CE**. Self-dating is vanishingly rare in Sanskrit philosophical literature; the verse is famous because it appeared to fix, **from the inside**, a chronology the rest of Nyāya hangs on. Wikipedia says the discovery "settled scholarly controversies."

**It did not.** D. C. Bhattacharyya's case, in ascending force:

1. **The verse occurs in only one manuscript.** A single-witness reading is a *variant*, not a datum.
2. Udayana **names and refutes Jñānaśrī**, who was in Tibet in **1038–41** — after Udayana is supposed to have died.
3. His preceptor **Śrīvatsa** belongs to c. **1000–1050**.
4. **Emend one syllable** — *tarkāvarāṅka* for *tarkāmvarāṅka* — and the same verse gives **Śaka 976 = 1054 CE**. Seventy years, one syllable.

`udayana.md` adopts **neither** date. Not 984, because (1) disqualifies it as *settled*. Not Bhattacharyya's conclusion either, because the two secondary reports of it **disagree** — "1025–1100" in one, "the twelfth century" in another — and the evidence listed points to the later *eleventh*.

> **Where a secondary source's stated conclusion does not follow from the evidence it reports, record the evidence.**

⚠ And note the article that carries all this: Wikipedia presents Śaka 906 as settled **and** cites **D. C. Bhattacharya** approvingly elsewhere in the same entry — the scholar whose whole argument is that Śaka 906 settles nothing. Two paragraphs, one page, mutually destructive, and nobody noticed. Compare [Ch 36 §5.1](../hindu/darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md#one-charge).

### 3.1 A synchronism the corpus found by accident {#candupandita}

Bhattacharyya's fourth argument is a synchronism, and it landed on a node written **hours earlier in the same batch.**

The *Naiṣadhacarita* commentator **Cāṇḍupaṇḍita** attests that **Śrīharṣa's father was defeated in debate by Udayana**, placing the debate at 1070–80.

`sriharsa.md` had already recorded — from Wikipedia's article on Śrīharṣa, a source with no interest whatever in Nyāya chronology — that Śrīharṣa's father was **Śrīhira, a poet at Vijayacandra's court.** Two routes, the same father, the same court, the same generation.

It supplies a **motive** the corpus could not previously see. Śrīharṣa's choice of Udayana's *Lakṣaṇāvalī* as the thing to demolish is, on this tradition, not only doctrinal — **it is a son answering his father's defeat.** The observation that the *Khaṇḍana* is "shaped by the target it attacks" acquires a second, biographical sense.

⚠ **Held at arm's length, twice.** Cāṇḍupaṇḍita's report is a debate-anecdote of the genre [Ch 34](34-the-commentator.md) and `shankara.md` discount; its usable content is that a later commentator **believed the two men contemporaries**, not that the debate happened. And a **reign-date problem is left open**: Śrīharṣa is placed under Govindacandra and Vijayacandra (1104–54) in the dating argument, while the *Naiṣadhacarita* is dated **1174 under Jayacandra**. No source consulted reconciles them, and this chapter does not either.

### 3.2 The other reason Udayana is here {#kusumanjali}

His *Nyāyakusumāñjali* mounts **nine arguments for Īśvara** — the most sustained theistic case in Indian philosophy. Two of them the corpus already held from the other side: *from combination* (atoms are inert, so their motion requires an intelligence — the step by which [Vaiśeṣika atomism became a theistic system](../hindu/darsana/16-nyaya-vaisheshika.md)) and *from the unforeseen* (*adṛṣṭa* requires intelligent administration).

⚠ Note the shape of two others: the Veda is infallible **because** God authored it, and God exists **because** the Veda says so. **Whether Udayana runs these as a circular pair or as two arguments at different levels cannot be settled from a nine-item summary, and is not adjudicated here.**

## 4. The avidyā quarrel, with both ends in the corpus {#avidya}

For the first time this corpus holds **both sides of the most consequential polemic in Vedānta**, in nodes written a day apart.

**The Advaita statement** (§2.3): Ānandabodha's *anirvacanīya* characterisation — avidyā is the **neither-real-nor-unreal** cause of world-appearance.

**The attack**: [Rāmānuja](../hindu/darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md)'s ***saptavidhā anupapatti***, the "seven untenables," argued against exactly that doctrine and debated "for several centuries." One of its members is that **defining a thing as indefinable is self-contradictory** — aimed squarely at *anirvacanīya*.

⚠ **And here the corpus stops, deliberately.** `ramanuja.md` asserts only the scheme's **existence**, its **first member** (*āśraya* — avidyā has no possible locus), and its **target**. John A. Grimes devoted a monograph to it (*The Seven Great Untenables*, Motilal Banarsidass, 1990), whose ch. 2 is "The Locus of Avidyā," which confirms the first member. **The enumerated list of all seven came back only from a listserv archive and a blog, which name six between them and disagree on wording.** IEP does not use the sevenfold scheme at all.

So the six further heads sit in the node **marked unverified and not asserted.** A famous scheme that every summary mentions and no accessible source enumerates properly is precisely the situation §4 of the charter exists for: *if you cannot cite it, you do not assert it* — even when it is the obvious thing to write, and even when writing it would make a better chapter.

**Where the quarrel lives in the corpus:** [Ch 30](../hindu/darsana/30-locus-of-ignorance.md) already asks where ignorance *resides* — the Bhāmatī answer (in the jīva) against the Vivaraṇa answer (in Brahman). Rāmānuja's *āśraya-anupapatti* is the claim that **neither answer can work**, and that the Advaitins' inability to agree is itself the evidence. Ch 30 and this section are the two halves of one argument.

## 5. Madhusūdana: the answer, and the strange thing after it {#madhusudana}

**Madhusūdana Sarasvatī** wrote the ***Advaitasiddhi***, the line-by-line answer to the *Nyāyāmṛta*, having trained in **Navya-Nyāya at Nabadwīp** before taking sannyāsa. On any account he is one of the two or three most formidable dialecticians Advaita produced.

He also wrote the ***Bhaktirasāyana***, "the elixir of devotion" — **"the only independent treatise on bhakti written by a major Advaita preceptor"** (Lance Nelson) — and opened a work with a verse asking **"How can I know of any reality apart from Kṛṣṇa?"**

**The tension is not soft**, and Nelson states it hard:

- **Devotion requires** duality between lover and beloved, personhood, dependence on a divine other.
- **Advaita teaches** that ultimate reality is impersonal Brahman, that the self is identical with it, and that "personhood and relationship are false constructs."

An Advaitin may of course allow bhakti as a **preparation** — a practice at the *vyāvahārika* level that purifies the mind for knowledge. **That is not Madhusūdana's claim.** His is that **devotion is possible even *after* the realisation of identity with Brahman**, duality being "assumed for the sake of devotion" post-liberation.

That makes duality something **the liberated may elect.** And the hinge is what he identifies Kṛṣṇa *with*: not a *saguṇa* deity subordinate to a higher impersonal absolute, but **nirguṇa Brahman itself** — the opposite route to the same devotional intensity the Vaiṣṇava Vedāntas of Ch 36 reach by making Brahman **personal**.

⚠ **Whether it works is not settled, and this chapter will not settle it.** Nelson — the specialist who has studied it most closely — reports that Madhusūdana's thought "has actually received **little careful study**" and that existing scholarship is "**largely uncritical**," writing explicitly against the claim that these teachings are "the grand climax" of bhakti's evolution.

> **A position widely admired and rarely examined is exactly what the trust method says to distrust. Admiration is not evidence.**

This is also the sharpest modification of [Ch 11](../hindu/darsana/11-vedanta.md) in the corpus: `moksha-advaita.md` records Advaita liberation as the recognition of identity, which on Śaṅkara's account **leaves a devotee nothing to do**.

⚠ **On his dates**, five positions are on record — Burnouf and Lassen mid-14th c., Telang late 15th/early 16th, Winternitz and V. Rajagopalan early 16th, Wikipedia c. 1490–1580, and an outlier c. 1540–1647. **Only one comes with reasoning.** Rajagopalan fixes him by four synchronisms, of which the last closes on a node the corpus already held: **Śeṣagovinda, his student's student, studied with [Appayya Dīkṣita](34-the-commentator.md)** — whose c. 1520–1593 is anchored on a **dated 1580 inscription**. Working back two teaching generations supports the early-to-mid 16th century and tells against c. 1540–1647. *That inference is this corpus's own, not a source's, and is labelled so in the node.*

## 6. A five-century argument {#chain}

Put §2.6 and §5 together and something unusual appears: **a single philosophical controversy that can be traced continuously for five hundred years, and whose most recent round is within living memory.**

| round | work | side |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ***Nyāyāmṛta*** — Vyāsatīrtha | Dvaita |
| 2 | ***Advaitasiddhi*** — Madhusūdana, **fifty years later, from Bengal** | Advaita |
| 3 | *Nyāyāmṛtataraṅgiṇī* (Vyāsa Rāmācārya); *Nyāyāmṛtakaṇṭakoddhāra* (Ānanda Bhaṭṭāraka) | Dvaita |
| 4 | *Guru-/Laghucandrikā* — Brahmānanda Sarasvatī | Advaita |
| 5 | *Taraṅgiṇīsaurabha* / *Nyāyāmṛtasaugandhya* — Vanamāli Miśra | Dvaita |
| 6 | *Vitthaleśī* — Vitthaleśa Upādhyāya; after which it "seemed to come to an end" | Advaita |
| 7 | *A Critique of Mādhva Refutation of the Śāṅkara School* (**1964**) — K. Narain, Allahabad | Advaita |
| 8 | *Advaitasiddhi vs Nyāyāmṛta: An Up To Date Critical Re-Appraisal* (**1994**) — B. N. K. Sharma | Dvaita |

Sharma dates the Sanskrit phase "from Vyāsatīrtha in the first quarter of the 16th century and ending with the 18th," and says Narain "reopened it in modern garb in English." **Rounds 7 and 8 are the same controversy in another language, and one of the participants says so.**

Three observations worth more than the list:

- **The reply-lag is real information.** Fifty years passed before Bengal answered Vijayanagara. A controversy is not a conversation; it is a sequence of books moving across a subcontinent at the speed of manuscripts.
- **Nobody started it.** Sharma records that Vyāsatīrtha "modelled his *Nyāyāmṛta* on the *Vādāvalī* and *Vādaratnāvalī* of his predecessors." Even the founding move is an enlargement.
- **And a circuit closes on §2.1.** Among those predecessors' works is **Viṣṇudāsācārya's *Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍana*** — literally "the refutation of the *Khaṇḍana*," a **Dvaita reply to Śrīharṣa.** It does not survive. It is known because **Vyāsatīrtha quotes it.** SEP lists the Advaitin and Naiyāyika responses to the *Khaṇḍana* and no Dvaita one; there was one, and its only witness is the man who started the next war.

## 7. What this chapter rests on {#provenance}

**The strongest source in the batch is here**: **Nilanjan Das's SEP entry on Śrīharṣa** (2018) — peer-reviewed, named author, and it treats the affiliation question *as* a dispute, naming Granoff against Phillips and Ram-Prasad and then adjudicating half of it. Also load-bearing: **Dasgupta** (1932) on Ānandabodha and Citsukha; **B. N. K. Sharma's 1994 monograph**, read in full; **Anantakrishna Sastri** quoted within it; **Lance Nelson** (2021) on the *Bhaktirasāyana*; **V. Rajagopalan** in *Preceptors of Advaita*; **Ramen Bhadra** (2014) on Udayana; and — uniquely — **A. B. Dhruva's 1933 critical edition of the *Syādvādamañjarī***, read directly, which is where the colophon in §2.5 comes from.

**What is weak, and named as such:**

- **No work by any of the eight was read**, except Malliṣeṇa. Not the *Khaṇḍana*, not the *Nyāyāmṛta*, not the *Advaitasiddhi*, not the *Nyāyakusumāñjali*.
- **Granoff 1978, Phillips 1995/1997 and Ram-Prasad 2002** are known only through Das's reports of them.
- **B. N. K. Sharma is a committed Dvaita partisan** — the 1994 book *is* the reply on one side — and is used for bibliography, chronology and his quotation of others, not for verdicts. **Valerie Stoker** and **Michael Williams**, the two modern non-partisan specialists on Vyāsatīrtha, were **not consulted**.
- **The seven untenables could not be enumerated** from any source of adequate provenance (§4).

**The four cheapest tests left undone:** the *Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya* itself (a Sanskrit–Hindi edition is on the Internet Archive); **Grimes 1990** in full, the only clean route to the seven; **Nelson's translation of *Bhaktirasāyana* ch. 1**, already in English at the same site as the study; and **Stoker or Williams** on Vyāsatīrtha.

⚠ **One correction the chapter made against a peer-reviewed source.** Rao & Sharma (*Asiatische Studien*, 2003) call Appayya Dīkṣita "17th cent." in one section and "three centuries later" than Madhva nine pages on. Only the second fits `appayya-dikshita.md`'s **inscription-anchored** c. 1520–1593. **Weighting a source by provenance is not deferring to it.**
