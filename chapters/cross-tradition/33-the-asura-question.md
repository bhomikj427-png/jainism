## How to use this chapter

This chapter is about a single word and what four traditions did with it.

***Asura***. In the oldest layer of the Ṛgveda it is a **title of honour**. By the Purāṇas it means **demon**. In Iran the cognate ***ahura*** stays honorific and becomes the name of God. And in Jainism the whole Hindu cast of gods and demons is re-sorted by a different criterion altogether, with results that invert the Hindu ranking.

Following one word across that range does something a doctrine-by-doctrine treatment cannot: it shows **how the same materials get re-purposed when the question changes**, and it gives the corpus a controlled setting in which to draw Indo-Iranian and Hindu–Jain comparisons **with their disanalogies attached**, which is the charter's central discipline.

## 1. *Asura* before it meant demon {#asura}

### 1.1 The datum

**Varuṇa** — the Ṛgveda's sovereign of the sky and enforcer of ***ṛta*** — is "mentioned as the **king of asuras**, who gained the status of a *deva*, serving as chief of the **Ādityas**." He and Mitra are "referred to as **Asuras** (e.g. **RV 5.63.3**), though also addressed as **Devas** (e.g. **RV 7.60.12**), reflecting fluid theological roles."

⚠️ The original sense of *asura* is "**possessor of *māyā***" or of vital force, "**lacking later demonic connotations**."

> **The highest moral god in the Ṛgveda is called an asura, approvingly.**

Anyone who brings the Purāṇic *asura* = demon back to these verses gets the collection exactly backwards. This is the single most common misreading of early Vedic vocabulary, and the corpus flags it as a typed non-equivalence rather than leaving it to context.

### 1.2 And *māyā* too

The same caution applies to the word inside the gloss. Varuṇa's ***māyā*** is **cosmic creative and measuring power** — "through *māyā*, Varuṇa becomes not only the enforcer of law but also a **cosmic architect**," embracing night and measuring the earth with the sun (Maṇḍala VIII).

It is **not** Advaita's world-concealing illusion (Ch 11). Roughly fifteen centuries separate the two senses, and the shift is a **reversal**: from *power to make* to *appearance to see through*. Two words in one sentence of Vedic, both of which mean something close to the opposite of what a reader arriving from later material will assume.

That is worth generalising. **The words of a tradition are not stable across its own history**, and a corpus that treats "the Sanskrit term" as a fixed quantity will import fifteen hundred years of development into a hymn.

## 2. Varuṇa: the god who was narrowed {#varuna}

### 2.1 A god you can sin against

What distinguishes Varuṇa from every other major Vedic deity is that his hymns are **juridical and penitential** rather than laudatory. He is "the enforcer of *ṛta*, the cosmic and moral order that governs both the natural world and human conduct," "a vigilant upholder of truth, who **punishes transgressors while showing mercy to the penitent**," and his ordinances are "unshakable, akin to a mountain."

The enforcement apparatus is concrete, and its details are the point:

- the ***pāśa***, the **noose** — **his iconic weapon is a restraint, not a blade**;
- **spies**. His omniscience runs through "celestial spies"; the Atharvaveda gives him "**spies with a thousand eyes**" who "descend to observe all human actions." He sits in a thousand-gated palace on a golden throne.

**RV 7.86–7.88**, attributed to the Vasiṣṭha family, are hymns of **supplication and confession of sin** to avert his wrath. And the ritual follows the theology: in the ***Varuṇapraghāsa*** rite, a seasonal sacrifice, "the sacrificer's **wife was required to confess her sin** (i.e. conjugal infidelity) before the officiating priest."

### 2.2 The road not taken

Set that beside the rest of the corpus's Vedic material, which is overwhelmingly about **[[yajna|sacrifice as exchange and maintenance]]** — the mechanism by which *ṛta* is kept running (Ch 19). Varuṇa's hymns are about something else: **guilt, surveillance, confession and pardon, before a personal deity who notices.**

It is the nearest thing in the Ṛgveda to a conscience-religion. **And it did not become the mainline.** What Indian traditions built their ethics on instead was ***karma*** — an impersonal mechanism, with **no one to confess to**.

The corpus stores that as a typed non-equivalence, and it is one of the more consequential in the graph. A moral order enforced by a watching person and a moral order enforced by a property of actions are different in kind, and the Indian traditions chose the second early and almost without exception. **Varuṇa is what the alternative would have looked like.**

### 2.3 The decline, and how it differs from Indra's

| era | what he is |
|---|---|
| **Ṛgveda** | supreme sky-sovereign; enforcer of *ṛta*; moral judge; wielder of *māyā*; lord of the *āpaḥ* |
| later Vedic | *Sindhu-pati*, "lord of the ocean"; the waters called *Virāj* become his consorts, "representing *prakṛti*" |
| **Mahābhārata / Purāṇas** | "lord of all waters." "**He is no longer depicted as the supreme sky god or an omnipotent sovereign administering cosmic order and morality.** The epic **forgets his earlier associations with *ṛta***" |
| final | a ***Lokapāla*** of the **western** quarter — "a symbolic alignment with the setting sun and perhaps with darkness and night" |

Ch 18 documents **Indra**'s decline. Put the two side by side and they differ **in kind**:

> **Indra was displaced. Varuṇa was narrowed.**

Indra was outranked by Viṣṇu, humbled by Kṛṣṇa at Govardhana, his enemies rehabilitated (§5). There are *stories* about his demotion. **Nobody took Varuṇa's throne in a story.** His moral-juridical function simply stopped being the kind of thing Indian religion needed a god for, and what was left — the waters — was enough to keep him in the pantheon at reduced size. Ending as guardian of the *west* is almost too apt.

Two mechanisms of divine decline, then: **competitive displacement** and **functional obsolescence**. The first leaves narrative traces; the second leaves only absence, and is correspondingly easier to miss.

## 3. Vṛtra and Verethragna {#vritra}

### 3.1 The Vedic side

**Vṛtra** "kept the waters of the world captive until he was killed by Indra, who destroyed all the **99 fortresses** of Vṛtra before liberating the imprisoned rivers." **RV 1.32** is the fullest telling — Vṛtra breaks Indra's two jaws, is thrown down, and in falling crushes the already-shattered fortresses. From this Indra takes his standing epithet ***Vṛtrahan***, "slayer of Vṛtra," also glossed "slayer of the first-born of dragons."

### 3.2 The question that decides what the myth is about

| reading | evidence | commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **a serpent-dragon** | Vṛtra is "known in the Vedas as ***Ahi***" (अहि, 'snake'); the *Vṛtrahan* dragon-gloss; the comparative pull of Jörmungandr, Typhon and the Slavic dragon | the Vedic myth is an instance of the widespread Indo-European **combat myth**; Indra is a dragon-slayer of the standard type |
| **"the obstruction"** — **Benveniste and Renou** | *vṛtra* "literally means '**cover, obstacle**'," from PIE *\*wer-* "to cover, to obstruct." The myth's principal feature "wasn't slaying noxious creatures but **to overcome obstacles**" and to "**unblock the flow of *apas***" | the myth is about **resistance-breaking**, and the dragon is a personification grown around an abstract noun. **The demon is downstream of the word** |

The etymological point is the hinge and it is genuinely awkward for the dragon reading: *vṛtra* is a **neuter abstract** meaning "obstruction," and its personification as a masculine adversary is a development *within* the language. But the Ṛgveda already calls him *Ahi* — so if the abstraction came first, the personification was complete before the hymns we have.

**The corpus does not adjudicate.** What it will say is that **the reading changes what Indra is**: on the first he is a monster-killer; on the second he is *the power that makes flow possible*, and the Ṛgveda's obsessive interest in released waters becomes the point rather than the scenery. Note the fit with **ṛta** — a cosmos whose characteristic failure is **blockage** is a cosmos ordered by flow.

### 3.3 The Iranian side, and why it is the sharper case

Avestan ***vərəθraγna*** — Middle Persian **Warahrām**, whence **Bahrām** — is built from *vərəθra* ("obstacle") plus the root of *\*gan-* ("to smite"). The Proto-Aryan adjective *\*vṛtraghan* has an exact Vedic cognate in *vṛtrahan*. He is a *yazata* of **victory** who "plainly enjoyed the greatest popularity of old," who "confers **virility**," and who possesses the "ability to **heal**."

The *Bahrām Yašt* has him appear to Zarathustra in **ten** forms:

> an impetuous **wind** · an armed **warrior** · an **adolescent of fifteen** · a gold-horned **bull** · a **white horse** with golden ears and muzzle · a **camel in heat** · a **boar** · a **bird of prey** · a **ram** · a **wild goat**

**Notice what the list does not contain: an opponent.** Verethragna is never shown killing anything. He *is* the victory, taking whatever shape victory takes.

That is the strongest single datum on the table, and it is why the Iranian evidence sharpens the §3.2 question rather than merely echoing it.

### 3.4 Three readings of the Indo-Iranian evidence

| reading | who | the claim | commits you to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **shared war-god** | the traditional comparative view | Verethragna corresponds to Vedic Indra Vṛtrahan as "descendants of a shared Proto-Indo-Aryan war-god" | one inherited deity who kept his dragon-slaying character in India and lost it in Iran, where Zoroastrian reform demoted the *daēvas* |
| **abstraction first** | **Benveniste and Renou** | the principal feature was overcoming obstacles and unblocking *apas*, not slaying creatures | **there was no inherited dragon.** Iran preserves the original, and **India's dragon is the innovation** |
| **functional merger** | **Paul Thieme** | "Proto-Aryan *Indra* has **assumed the functions** of a Proto-Aryan god *\*Vṛtraghna*" | the Vedic Indra is a **composite** — an inherited god plus a separate inherited *function* that attached to him. Neither "same god" nor "same story," but a takeover |

Thieme's is the position most easily lost in summary and the most useful to hold, because **it dissolves the question rather than answering it**: the Iranian *yazata* and the Vedic epithet can both be old and neither derived from the other, if what Iran kept as a **god** India kept as a **job description**. That would explain the one asymmetry nobody disputes — *vṛtrahan* is an **epithet** in the Ṛgveda, something Indra is *called*, and a **name** in the Avesta.

### 3.5 Why the corpus keeps two nodes

Charter §8: same word, genuinely different tradition → separate keys plus a typed edge. Here the split is unusually stark, because the two nodes stand on **opposite sides of the same fight**:

| | **Vṛtra** (Vedic) | **Vərəθraγna** (Avestan) |
|---|---|---|
| what the word names | the **obstruction** — the thing slain | the **smiting of obstruction** — personified and worshipped |
| status | *asura*, adversary; later a wronged avenger and a devotee of Viṣṇu | *yazata*, "the greatest popularity of old" |
| relation to Indra | Indra's victim, and the source of his epithet | on two of three readings, a figure Indra either **is** or has **absorbed** |
| morphology | neuter abstract *vṛtra*, "cover, obstacle" | compound *vərəθra-γna*, "obstacle-smiting" |

> **An unwary reader meeting "Vṛtra ≈ Verethragna" will conclude that the Zoroastrians worshipped Indra's dragon. They worshipped its defeat.**

That sentence is what the honesty layer is for.

### 3.6 And the ten forms are not the ten avatāras

The *Bahrām Yašt*'s ten forms invite comparison with Viṣṇu's **daśāvatāra** (Ch 21). The corpus draws that edge **and** flags it, because sharing a count is not sharing a doctrine:

| | the ten avatāras | the ten Vərəθraγna forms |
|---|---|---|
| what they are | successive **descents into history** | **modes of a single appearance** to one prophet |
| chronology | ordered, with a future tenth | none |
| narrative | each has one — opponents, deeds, a soteriology | none attached to any |

The corpus stores `structurally-parallel-to` **and** `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` on that pair, drawn specifically so the coincidence of the number ten cannot be read as descent or borrowing.

## 4. A famous comparison, recorded as withdrawn {#dumezil}

**Georges Dumézil** proposed that Varuṇa and the Greek **Ouranos** belong to "the earliest Indo-European cultural level," both names from a PIE root *\*ŭer-* meaning "**to bind**" — which would have made the *noose* the etymology, elegantly.

The corpus records that this identification "**is now widely rejected** in favour of derivation from the root *\*wers-* 'to moisten, drip' (Sanskrit *vṛṣ* 'to rain, pour')."

Keep this because it is a **retraction**, and retractions are the rarest thing in comparative literature. The Varuṇa–Ouranos equation is still repeated in general books; the etymology it rested on has been abandoned; and on the current derivation his name means something like *the rainer* — which fits the water-god he **ended as** rather than the moral sovereign he **began as**.

> **A comparison can be famous, elegant, and wrong.**

That is §0's business, applied to a comparison made by a great scholar rather than by a popular enthusiast. It also shows the failure is not confined to the naïve: the same pull toward a satisfying cross-cultural identity operates on specialists, and the correction came from morphology.

### 4.1 The comparison that survives

By contrast the **Ahura Mazdā** parallel does real work. The Avestan name would be rendered in Vedic as *Asura Medhya*; the Vedas repeatedly call Varuṇa an *asura*; and

> "even as Ahura Mazdā rules by and upholds ***aša***, the cosmic moral order, in the Avesta, so too do Varuṇa and the Asuras uphold the analogous concept of ***ṛta*** in the Veda"

— *aša* and *ṛta* being **the same inherited word**.

So this is a **two-term correspondence in vocabulary and in function at once**: *asura/ahura* **and** *ṛta/aša*, the title and the thing it upholds, matching across both languages. That is a far stronger comparative datum than any single name-match, and it is the difference between §4 and §4.1.

It remains a **structural parallel and not an identity**: Ahura Mazdā is the sole uncreated creator of a dualist system; Varuṇa is chief of the Ādityas in a polytheistic one. The corpus draws it as a parallel and says so.

**Since this chapter was drafted, [[ahura-mazda|Ahura Mazdā]] has been written as a node, and the correspondence is now sourced from the Iranian side too — where it turns out to be *three*-term, not two:**

| term | Iranian | Indian |
|---|---|---|
| the title | ***ahura*** ← PIE *\*h₂ńsuros*, "cognate with Sanskrit *ásura*" | ***asura***, an approving title of Varuṇa at RV 5.63.3 |
| what it upholds | ***aṣ̃a*** | ***ṛta*** — and both "derive from Proto-Indo-Iranian *\*Hṛtá-* 'truth'" |
| its ritual guardian | **fire**, with which *Aṣ̃a Vahiṣta* is associated — it "detects" truth and is "the auxiliary of the truth" | **Agni**, *ṛtasya gopā*, "guardian of *ṛta*" (Ch. 19) |

A one-term match can be coincidence. A three-term match in which the second term is the first term's characteristic activity, and the third is the same functional pairing in both languages, is much harder to get by accident. **That is the difference between §4 and §4.1 made quantitative.** — with one caution the new node adds: the stronger claim in circulation, that Ahura Mazdā's name *derives* from *\*vouruna-miθra*, is attributed to "some scholars," is not its source's own voice, and the corpus does **not** assert it. Having just recorded a withdrawn etymology for Varuṇa's name in §4, it declines to accept a new one on the same evidence-grade.

### 4.2 The two Indo-Iranian cases pull opposite ways

Worth holding together:

- **§3**: Iran keeps as a **god** (*Vərəθraγna*) what India keeps as an **epithet** (*vṛtrahan*).
- **§4.1**: both keep the god, and it is **India** that lets the moral function lapse (§2.2).

Neither tradition is systematically the conservative one. **They conserved different things**, which is what makes the pair informative rather than merely parallel.

## 5. The rehabilitation {#rehabilitation}

### 5.1 Three figures, one movement

The Purāṇic tradition re-reads the Vedic adversaries. Three instances, and the corpus assembled them from four separate source-sets:

| figure | what the tradition does |
|---|---|
| **Vṛtra** | *Mahābhārata*: "an asura created by the artisan god **Tvaṣṭṛ** to avenge the killing of his son **Triśiras/Viśvarūpa** by Indra" — so he has a **motive**, and it is Indra's own prior crime, which (Viśvarūpa being a brahmin) incurs ***brahmahatyā***. Then the *Bhāgavata* "recognizes Vṛtra as a ***bhakta*** of Viṣṇu who was slain only due to his failure to live piously" |
| **Prahlāda** | an *asura* prince who is the **paradigm of devotion**; in *Bhāgavata* Book 7 "Prahlāda describes bhakti as the only way to please God," and Kṛṣṇa says at **BG 10.30**, "**Among the Daityas, I am the great devotee Prahlāda**" |
| **Bali** | Prahlāda's grandson (the line runs Hiraṇyakaśipu → Prahlāda → Virocana → **Bali** → Bāṇāsura): an asura king whose **righteousness** is the point of his story, dispossessed by Vāmana not for wickedness but for being too good at ruling (Ch 21) |

**Three generations of one family, plus the Ṛgveda's chief monster, all re-read as devotees.** And notice what it costs on the other side: **each rehabilitation debits Indra.** A tradition that reframes the enemy as a devotee has thereby reframed the hero as a problem. That is one of the mechanisms of §2.3's *displacement*.

### 5.2 Two readings, kept apart

Given §1, the rehabilitation admits two descriptions, and the corpus records both rather than collapsing them:

- **A recovery.** *Asura* originally meant "possessor of *māyā*," with no demonic sense, and Varuṇa held the title honorifically. The Purāṇic asura-devotee is in one sense the older meaning resurfacing.
- **A new device.** Making the model devotee an asura by **birth**, who remains one, detaches salvation from ancestry — which is precisely the claim *bhakti* needs in order to be a genuinely different path from ritual or knowledge, both of which had entry requirements.

These are not the same claim and neither entails the other. The first is about **semantics**; the second about **soteriological engineering**.

### 5.3 The boon and the loophole

Prahlāda's father **Hiraṇyakaśipu** held a boon that he could be slain "**neither by a man nor an animal, neither during the day nor at night, neither indoors nor outdoors, neither on land nor in the air nor in water**." **Narasiṃha** satisfies every clause by falling **between** each pair: part-human and part-animal, at **dusk**, on the **threshold**, on his own lap.

Name the type: the boon is defeated not by superior force but by **exhaustive disjunction failing to be exhaustive**. Every clause partitions a domain in two, and the killer occupies the boundary each partition forgot.

The corpus notes a **formal resonance** with Indian logic's interest in whether alternatives genuinely exclude — the ***catuṣkoṭi*** (Ch 06) adds exactly the *both* and *neither* corners that Hiraṇyakaśipu's boon assumed away. **A resonance, not a source.** The Purāṇa is not doing logic, the corpus asserts no influence in either direction, and the edge is typed to say so.

## 6. Nine forms, and a grammar independent of its object {#navavidha}

***Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 7.5.23–24**, spoken by Prahlāda, is the classical enumeration of ***navavidhā bhakti***:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ***śravaṇam*** | hearing |
| 2 | ***kīrtanam*** | chanting |
| 3 | ***smaraṇam*** | remembering |
| 4 | ***pāda-sevanam*** | serving the feet |
| 5 | ***arcanam*** | worship of the image |
| 6 | ***vandanam*** | offering prayers |
| 7 | ***dāsyam*** | being a servant |
| 8 | ***sakhyam*** | being a friend |
| 9 | ***ātma-nivedanam*** | surrendering everything |

**Two structural observations.**

**The list is a ladder of decreasing distance.** It runs from *hearing about* God, through *acting toward* him, to *being related to* him — servant, then friend — and ends in **self-surrender**. Presented as alternatives, they are usually read as a menu; **read in order they are a progression of intimacy**, and the terminal item is not an act at all.

**The list is portable.** The *Śiva Purāṇa* "also enlists these very 9 modes of bhakti, with slight variation in the order of enumeration, as forms of devotion to **Lord Śiva**."

> **The same nine, re-addressed.** Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva literature share the *form* of devotion and dispute only its *addressee*.

That is the corpus's clearest instance of a **devotional grammar independent of its object**, and it is why `bhakti` is one node rather than two. It also yields a caution the corpus types explicitly: **a shared practice is no evidence of a shared theology.** Two communities can perform the same nine acts and disagree entirely about who is on the other end of them.

## 7. Jainism re-sorts the cast {#jain}

### 7.1 Enrolment, not rejection

Jainism does not reject Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma. It **enrols** them, in its own scheme of the ***triṣaṣṭi-śalākāpuruṣa***, the **63 illustrious persons** of each half-cycle (Ch 09):

> **24 tīrthaṅkaras + 12 cakravartins + 9 Baladevas + 9 Vāsudevas (Nārāyaṇas) + 9 Prati-vāsudevas = 63**

The last three groups form a **triad repeated nine times**: a hero, his elder brother, and their adversary. **Kṛṣṇa is the ninth Vāsudeva; Balarāma the ninth Baladeva**, in Hemacandra's 12th-century *Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra*.

### 7.2 And then reverses the ranking

> "**Baladevas are gentle heroes and devout laymen, who attain liberation** corresponding to Tīrthaṅkaras. **Vāsudevas** are also devout Jain laymen and ultimately attain liberation, **but are first reborn in hell because of their violent actions.**"

| | Hindu | Jain |
|---|---|---|
| **Kṛṣṇa** | the supreme *avatāra*; God himself in the Gītā | the ninth **Vāsudeva** — a devout layman who **goes to hell first**, because he killed |
| **Balarāma** | elder brother; a *partial* or disputed avatāra, ninth at best (Ch 21 gives the ninth slot as "Buddha/Balarāma") | the ninth **Baladeva** — a gentle hero **liberated directly** |

**The two traditions rank the same pair of brothers in opposite order, and each does it for exactly the reason that tradition is about.** For Vaiṣṇavism the criterion is *who is the descent of Viṣṇu*, and Kṛṣṇa wins outright. For Jainism the criterion is ***ahiṃsā***, and Kṛṣṇa's violence is a debit payable in a hell-birth before liberation, while the brother who did not kill needs no detour.

### 7.3 Two misreadings blocked

**It is not a polemic.** Kṛṣṇa is a *śalākāpuruṣa*, an illustrious person, a devout layman, and **he does attain liberation**. Jainism honours him and then applies its own accounting to him. Honouring and auditing are the same act there.

**It is not a difference of emphasis.** In a system where karma is **matter** adhering by passion and violent action (Ch 26, Ch 32), no amount of righteousness in the cause can prevent the bond.

> **The hell-birth is not a judgement about Kṛṣṇa; it is an entailment of the physics.**

### 7.4 The exact contrast

Set the Jain verdict beside the Gītā's answer to the same problem — Arjuna may kill without binding if he acts without attachment to the fruit — and the difference is a single point:

> **The Gītā locates karmic bondage in the *intention*. Jainism locates it in the *act*.**

That one disagreement is the difference between **a scripture that can command a warrior to fight and a scripture that cannot.** It is not a nuance; it is the whole reason one tradition produced a battlefield theodicy and the other produced *sallekhanā* (Ch 08).

### 7.5 Two further points

**A division of moral labour.** The *Baladeva* is defined by upholding non-violence **while his brother does the killing** — the pair is not hero-and-sidekick but a two-role structure, and the tradition rewards the half that abstains. Jain retellings do the same to the *Rāmāyaṇa*: Rāma becomes a Baladeva, and the killing of Rāvaṇa goes to Lakṣmaṇa.

**Positions, not persons.** The 63 appear **once in each half-cycle** of the *utsarpiṇī/avasarpiṇī* wheel (Ch 09). So Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma are **slots in a repeating structure**, not unique historical descents. That is the deepest formal difference from the avatāra scheme, which is built around **unrepeatable interventions in a linear history** — and it means the Jain and Vaiṣṇava accounts disagree not only about the ranking but about what kind of thing a great figure *is*.

## 8. What this chapter can and cannot support {#limits}

Because everything above is drawn from encyclopedias and search aggregation, the limits should be stated in one place rather than inferred.

**Supported.** The vocabulary facts (*asura*'s Vedic sense; *vṛtra*'s etymology; *vərəθra-γna*'s morphology; *aša*/*ṛta*); the existence and attribution of the three Indo-Iranian scholarly positions; the narrative content of the Purāṇic and Jain material; the *navavidhā bhakti* list and the *Śiva Purāṇa* parallel; the 63-*śalākāpuruṣa* breakdown; and the Baladeva/Vāsudeva doctrine.

**Not supported, and not claimed.** Any reading of a specific hymn as a hymn. **No Ṛgvedic verse was read in translation** — RV 1.32, 5.63.3, 7.60.12, 7.86–88 are all known here by description. **No Avestan text was read**; the *Bahrām Yašt*'s ten forms are taken on an encyclopedia's word. **No Purāṇic passage was fetched from a critical edition** — *Bhāgavata* 7.5.23–24 comes through a devotional publishing house. **Hemacandra was not read**, though Helen Johnson's complete English translation exists and would settle the Jain material outright.

**Single-sourced items carrying real weight**, flagged individually in their nodes and repeated here: the *Varuṇapraghāsa* confession detail; the *Śiva Purāṇa* nine-forms parallel; the Baladeva/Vāsudeva liberation-vs-hell quotation, which carries §7 entirely.

**And one structural weakness.** For `verethragna`, **both signals are Wikipedia articles**. Two articles are not two authorities. §3.4's three-position map is therefore a map of *a summary of* a scholarly dispute, and the corpus would revise it without embarrassment on reading Benveniste and Renou's 1934 monograph or Thieme directly.
