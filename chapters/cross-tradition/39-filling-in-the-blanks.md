# Chapter 39 — Filling in the Blanks: How the Record Gets Made

> **Primary concepts:** `jayatīrtha` · `vedānta-deśika` · `vimuktātman` · `tīsaṭa` · `niścalakara` · `vṛnda`
> **Folder:** `cross-tradition/` — the six span **Dvaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Advaita and Āyurveda**, and the chapter's subject is not a doctrine but a *failure mode of evidence* that all four share.
> **Reads after:** [Ch 35 (Dating a Literature Without Dates)](../hindu/shastra/35-dating-a-literature-without-dates.md) and [Ch 36 (Founders and the Lineages That Made Them)](../hindu/darsana/36-founders-and-their-lineages.md). Ch 35 found that an entire literature dates itself by who quotes whom. **This chapter is about what happens in the gaps that leaves.**

---

## How to use this chapter

Ch 35 established that in Āyurveda "citation evidence is **ordinal, not cardinal**" — it tells you the order of authors, not their years, and every absolute date entered from outside the medical literature. Ch 36 found the same structure under the Śrī Vaiṣṇava *munitraya*.

This chapter takes the next step and asks a different question:

> **When the record has a hole in it, what goes into the hole?**

The answer, across four traditions, is disconcertingly uniform. **Something always goes in.** A text leaves a father unnamed and a colophon supplies a famous one. A lineage has an awkward gap and the lifespans stretch to close it. A panegyric verse gets read as a synchronism. A commentator's respectful epithet becomes a discipleship. An encyclopedia revises a date and the old value keeps circulating as if it were an independent witness.

**None of this is fraud.** Every case in this chapter is ordinary, explicable, and mostly done by people who were not trying to write history at all. That is what makes it worth a chapter: these are the *normal* operations of a manuscript tradition, and a reader who does not know them will mistake their products for evidence.

Five things to carry out:

1. **The six mechanisms** (§2) — a working checklist.
2. **The blank that gets filled**, twice, in the same literature, two centuries apart (§3).
3. **Lifespans stretched to make a lineage continuous** — the corpus inferred this in Batch 45 and now has a scholar and a citation for it (§4).
4. **Why commentators are the best historical sources we have**, and were never trying to be (§5).
5. **The counter-case** (§7) — because a chapter about spurious identifications can very easily produce spurious *splits*, and one of these six exists to stop that.

## 2. Six mechanisms {#mechanisms}

Stated compactly, so the rest of the chapter can be read against them.

| # | mechanism | worked example |
|---|---|---|
| **M1** | **A blank gets filled with a celebrity.** A text names an authority-figure without naming him; later apparatus supplies the most famous available name | Tīsaṭa's unnamed father becomes "Vāgbhaṭa" (§3) |
| **M2** | **Lifespans stretch to close a gap**, so a lineage can be continuous | 128 / 120 / 120-year ācāryas (§4) |
| **M3** | **Praise is read as chronology.** A panegyric about defeating famous rivals becomes evidence they were contemporaries | Madhusūdana at Navadvīpa (§4.3) |
| **M4** | **Respect becomes lineage.** An honorific for a predecessor is read as a teacher–pupil relation | Niścalakara and Vijayarakṣita (§5.3); Vimuktātman and Ānandabodha (§6.1) |
| **M5** | **One source, sampled at several ages, looks like several sources** | Gaṅgeśa's date (Ch 38 §5.1); Vimuktātman's (§6.2) |
| **M6** | **An external anchor turns out to be about something else** | al-Yaʿqūbī on the *Siddhayoga* (§6.3) |

⚠ **And a seventh that is the counterweight, not another failure: M7 — the false split.** Noticing M1–M6 makes a reader eager to divide people who share a name. §7 is the case where a distinguished scholar did exactly that and was wrong.

## 3. The blank, filled — and the same blank, left open {#the-blank}

### 3.1 A father who is praised and not named

**Tīsaṭa** (10th c.) wrote the *Cikitsākalikā*, a therapeutic manual of about 400 verses. Its **opening verse praises Sūrya, the Aśvins, Dhanvantari, Suśruta — and Tīsaṭa's father.** It does not say who his father was. Meulenbeld: he "was the son of a physician **whose name is not disclosed**."

And: **"The colophons of a number of MSS call Tīsaṭa the son of Vāgbhaṭa."**

Meulenbeld rejects it on three grounds — the colophons may be late; **the father's name is absent from the text and its commentary**; and "no information is available on the identity of this Vāgbhaṭa."

> **⭐ The middle reason exposes the mechanism, and this is M1 in its clearest form.** The text leaves a slot open. The tradition later fills it with the most famous name in the field. **We can watch it happen because the blank is still visible in the verse.**

### 3.2 The other half, and why Batch 45 could not see it

`candrata.md` (Batch 45) had reported the collapse of its own queue premise: **Candraṭa is called "Vāgbhaṭa's grandson"** only through colophons Meulenbeld distrusts. Reading the *Tīsaṭa* section supplies the missing generation — the same apparatus makes **Tīsaṭa the son of Vāgbhaṭa**, which is exactly what makes Candraṭa the grandson.

**One genealogical claim, asserted at two generations, in one manuscript tradition.** Batch 45 met the grandson and could not see where the claim came from; Batch 46 met the father and could.

⚠ A further, independent check the corpus can now make: **Vāgbhaṭa is "conspicuously absent"** from Tīsaṭa's own list of authorities — a list of a dozen names including Caraka, Suśruta, Hārīta, Parāśara and Bhela. **A man lists his authorities and omits the one the colophons make his father.** *(An argument from silence, and labelled as one: it shows he did not treat Vāgbhaṭa as an authority, not that he had not heard of him.)*

### 3.3 The blank that nobody filled

**Niścalakara** (12th c., Bengal) wrote the *Ratnaprabhā* on Cakrapāṇidatta's *Cakradatta*. In his introductory verses he says he will restore the text by consulting other treatises and **by having recourse to the words of his *guru*** (*gurukti*) — and Meulenbeld notes:

> "**he does not inform his readers who his guru was, although the latter is repeatedly quoted and referred to.**"

**Two Āyurvedic authors, two centuries apart, both leave the crucial name out of their prefatory verses.** In the first case we can watch the tradition invent one. In the second, the blank is still open — and a modern scholar, **P. V. Sharma**, reads Niścala's *guruparamparā* and *gurusampradāya* as the lineage established by Vijayarakṣita. **That is a plausible filling of a blank, and it is exactly the operation §3.1 warns about.** Not adopted here.

> **The general point.** Sanskrit prefatory verses honour teachers and fathers *without naming them* far more often than a modern reader expects — the audience knew. **Every such courtesy is a hole in the historical record, and holes attract fillings.**

## 4. Stretching a lineage {#lineages}

### 4.1 What Batch 45 inferred

`nathamuni.md` tabulated three consecutive Śrī Vaiṣṇava ācāryas:

| ācārya | conventional dates | span |
|---|---|---|
| Nāthamuni | 823–951 | **128 years** |
| Yāmuna | 918–1038 | **120 years** |
| Rāmānuja | 1017–1137 | **120 years** |

and concluded: *"One such figure is a hagiographic flourish; three in a row, in one lineage, is a **convention** — the munitraya's dates are a system of stitching, not a record."* **That was arithmetic, and the corpus's own inference.**

### 4.2 What Batch 46 found

Writing `vedanta-desika.md` turned up **Elisa Freschi**, in the peer-reviewed *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, stating the mechanism outright and citing a scholar for it:

> "the **lifespans of the earliest teachers of Viśiṣṭādvaita Vedānta have been prolonged in order to connect them with each other**" — citing **Neevel 1977, pp. 14–16**

**That is M2, named, sourced, and given a purpose: the spans are stretched *so that the teachers can have met*.** A lineage must be continuous — a doctrine transmitted person to person cannot have a gap in it — and if the dates leave a gap, the dates give way.

⚠ And the convention does not stop with the three: Freschi gives **Veṅkaṭanātha himself as c. 1269 – c. 1370**, about **101 years**. She adds the general caution that the biographical material "comes largely from **later hagiographies**" and that these dates are "**traditional rather than definitively established**."

> ⚠ **Neevel was not read.** What the corpus holds is Freschi's report of Neevel's argument, corroborated by its own arithmetic. **That is enough to assert the pattern and not enough to assert any corrected date** — and none is asserted, here or in the node.

### 4.3 Praise mistaken for a date

A cleaner instance, and it cost the corpus something.

`madhusudana-sarasvati.md` (Batch 45) adopted V. Rajagopalan's dating of Madhusūdana Sarasvatī and praised it as **"the only source consulted that argues for a date rather than stating one."** It fixes him by four synchronisms. **The third is: "Gadādhara the logician was his contemporary."**

Writing `gadadhara.md` established that **Gadādhara wrote c. 1640–60**, is 17th-century in every source consulted, and was taught by **Harirāma Tarkavāgīśa, placed c. 1625** — while Madhusūdana is placed at the beginning of the 16th century. **A gap of about 140 years.**

And the synchronism's probable source is identifiable. An Advaita-tradition site quotes a verse to the effect that **when Madhusūdana came to Navadvīpa, Mathurānātha Tarkavāgīśa trembled and Gadādhara became afraid** — and, to its credit, presents it as "quoted **praise poetry rather than historical record**."

> **M3.** A panegyric that a champion frightened the great logicians of the day is a claim about **stature**. Read as a list of contemporaries, it becomes a date. It is the same genre this corpus already discounts in `shankara.md` and `appayya-dikshita.md` — and here it had got inside a dating argument the corpus had **accepted and built on**.

⚠ **What was *not* done.** The other three synchronisms are untouched, and the strongest — Śeṣagovinda studying with Appayya Dīkṣita, whose dates rest on a **dated 1580 inscription** — is external and epigraphic. **The conclusion stands; one of its four legs does not.** An uncomfortable consequence is recorded and left open: the outlier dating that file *argued against* is the one that would make the Navadvīpa links work. **A batch that finds a problem in its own earlier work should say so and stop there.**

## 5. The commentators, who were the best historians and were not trying to be {#commentators}

### 5.1 Two literatures, one shape

**Veṅkaṭanātha** (c. 1269–c. 1370) is the reason five earlier works are not wholly lost. Through his quotations survive fragments of **Nāthamuni's *Nyāyatattva*** (per Freschi, "quoted largely in the *Nyāyasiddhāñjana*"), **Parāśara Bhaṭṭa's *Tattvaratnākara***, **Rāmamiśra's *Ṣaḍarthasaṅkṣepa***, works of the **Vṛttikāra**, and texts of **Yādavaprakāśa** — Rāmānuja's own former teacher.

**Niścalakara** (12th c.) does the same for medicine. Meulenbeld: "**The former's *Ratnaprabhā* is a very rich mine of information on the subject**" — the subject being **what Cakrapāṇidatta read**. The commentary identifies roughly **fifty** works and authors as Cakrapāṇi's sources, including **Kharanāda, Bhaluki, Kṛṣṇātreya, Vṛddhavideha, the *Yogaśata***, and many others that do not survive.

> ⭐ **Two traditions, two centuries apart, no contact — and the same result.** In both, the historical record of *what an earlier author read* is preserved by a later commentator **who was not writing history**. Veṅkaṭanātha quoted his predecessors to argue with them; Niścalakara listed Cakrapāṇi's sources to explain a difficult text. **Neither was making an archive. Both made one.**

### 5.2 And the corpus depends on it, whether it likes it or not

This is not an abstract observation. **Tīsaṭa's terminus ad quem is one line of Niścalakara's source-census** — the fact that Cakrapāṇi (c. 1050) used the *Cikitsākalikā*. Follow the chain:

> a **12th–13th-century** commentator's remark about an **11th-century** author's sources is what supplies the upper limit for a **10th-century** writer.

Ch 35 said citation evidence is ordinal rather than cardinal. **Here it is ordinal at two removes.** The evidence is not Tīsaṭa's, and not Cakrapāṇi's; it is Niścalakara's *about* Cakrapāṇi.

And the tightening is worse. Meulenbeld brackets Tīsaṭa and Candraṭa between **650 and 1050** — four hundred years for a father and son — and narrows to **900–1050** only on a conditional: **if** Candraṭa wrote a commentary on Vṛnda's *Siddhayoga*, "as seems to follow from a remark of Śivadāsasena." That remark is a **16th-century commentator quoting a lost commentary**. So a passing quotation, four centuries later, of a book that no longer exists, tightens a 10th-century author's date by 250 years.

> **This is what the Āyurvedic chronology is made of, and it is worth saying plainly rather than gesturing at.** It is not weak scholarship. It is careful scholarship on evidence of this kind — and Meulenbeld's own hedging (*"as seems to follow"*, *"probably"*, *"do not preclude an earlier date"*) is the sign of it.

### 5.3 Respect read as lineage

Niścalakara names **Vijayarakṣita** as an ***āyurvedaguru*** in his introductory verses and says he wrote because the interpretation of the *Cakradatta* had become obscured **since that teacher's death**.

| reading | who |
|---|---|
| Niścalakara was **Vijayarakṣita's pupil** — "his main argument is the mention of Vijayarakṣita as an *āyurvedaguru*" | **D. Ch. Bhattacharyya** |
| No — the verses mean a **considerable interval** had passed, during which bad explanations accumulated | **P. V. Sharma** |
| Sharma's reading is not what the words say: "Niścala's own words … **do not hint at Vijayarakṣita's death as an event that occurred a long time ago**" | **Meulenbeld** |

⚠ **Meulenbeld corrects Sharma's reading and still does not endorse the discipleship.** *Āyurvedaguru* is a title of respect that a whole region's physicians could use. **M4: an honorific is not a lineage** — though it is very often read as one, because a lineage is a more useful thing to have.

**And the stakes are not small.** Meulenbeld states that **Vijayarakṣita's date "ultimately depends on the period of time that elapsed between his death and the composition of Niścalakara's *Ratnaprabhā*."** The results:

| Vijayarakṣita | proposed by |
|---|---|
| c. 1100 | follows from D. Ch. Bhattacharyya |
| c. 1200, later revised to the Sena kings' reigns | **P. V. Sharma** — *two published positions* |
| c. 1240 | **A. F. R. Hoernle** — "on insufficient grounds," refuted by Bhattacharyya |
| second half of the 13th c. | **G. Halder** |
| 14th or 15th century | **J. Jolly** |

**Five scholars, four centuries, and the hinge is how long after one man's death another man started writing.** ⚠ Note **P. V. Sharma holding two positions at different times** — the corpus's **second** such case, after B. N. K. Sharma in `madhva.md` (1962 and 2000). **A leading Indologist changing a date in print does not remove the earlier figure from circulation.** That is M5 forming.

## 6. Three claims that dissolve on inspection {#dissolving}

### 6.1 A discipleship with nowhere to stand

Batch 45 queued **Vimuktātman** explicitly as a *test*: "Ānandabodha's alleged teacher — **single-sourced**, so writing him is also the test." The test ran, and the claim failed **on every available dating**:

| Vimuktātman | Ānandabodha | result |
|---|---|---|
| 10th c. | 11th–12th c. (Dasgupta) | one to two centuries apart |
| 10th c. | **13th c.** | **three centuries apart** — and *both these datings are on the same page of the same site* |
| c. 1200 | 11th–12th c. | **the relation inverts** — the alleged teacher is the later man |

⚠ **A negative result, stated as one**: the claim is unsupported and chronologically obstructed by every source consulted, **not** refuted by any of them.

> **And the method note is the transferable part.** The claim reached the corpus through *search-level aggregation* that **named** advaita-vedanta.org as a source. **Read directly, that site's own page makes no such claim** — it gives Vimuktātman no *guru* and no pupils. **An aggregator's attribution to a site is not the site's claim.** Checking the named source was enough to dissolve it, and it cost one fetch.

### 6.2 A source that contradicts itself in one article

Wikipedia's *History of Advaita Vedanta* gives Vimuktātman **"c. 1200 CE"** in its prose and lists him under **"Prakāśātman, Vimuktātman, Sarvajñātman (10th century)"** in its own chronology. **A two-century contradiction inside one article.**

With Ch 38 §5.1 (Gaṅgeśa at four ages across three Wikipedia strata) and §6.4 below, that is **three** cases in one batch. The conclusion is not that these sources are unreliable in detail — they are mostly fine — but that they are unreliable **as a set of witnesses**:

> **They do not check themselves across articles. So sampling several of them is not corroboration.** That is M5, and it is the single most practically useful thing in this chapter.

### 6.3 The external anchor that was about a different book

**M6, and it is the most poignant case in the chapter**, because of what it would have been worth.

Ch 35's central finding is that every absolute year in the Āyurvedic chronology **entered from outside the medical literature**. An **external, non-Indian, independently dated** witness is therefore the strongest evidence obtainable anywhere in this field.

**M. Z. Siddiqi** reported that the *Siddhayoga* is referred to by the Arab author **Ibn Wāḍiḥ al-Yaʿqūbī**, who lived in the second half of the ninth century. That would have anchored **Vṛnda** absolutely.

Meulenbeld dismisses it: **"the work mentioned by this Arab author proves to be Ravigupta's *Siddhasāra*."**

> **The one chance to date this author from outside his own tradition turns out to be about a different book.** Even the outside anchors have to be **identified** first — and identification is the hard part.

### 6.4 And a misattribution with a one-clause cause

**A. F. R. Hoernle** held that the *Siddhayoga* was written by **Mādhavakara**, and that the *Rogaviniścaya* and *Siddhayoga* were "clearly the two parts of a single work by one author." Meulenbeld's explanation:

> "**Hoernle was unaware of the existence of the *Mādhavacikitsā*.**"

And the error had a handle: the *Siddhayoga* **is also known as the *Vṛndamādhava***. **A title containing another author's name, plus one text the scholar had not seen, produced an attribution that had to be undone.** ⚠ Note this is not a *tradition* filling a blank but a *modern scholar* doing it, on incomplete evidence, in good faith. **M1–M6 are not failings of premodern minds.**

## 7. The counter-case: when a split is the error {#counter-case}

A chapter like this is dangerous. Having watched six mechanisms manufacture false identities, a reader will start dividing everyone who shares a name — and Batch 46 gave that reader plenty of encouragement. It logged **four** name-collisions:

- **Vācaspati Miśra I / II** (`vacaspati-mishra.md`) — confirmed **twice**, from a list of Śrīharṣa's opponents and, independently, from a census of Gaṅgeśa's commentators;
- **two Harirāma Tarkavāgīśas**, implied by `gadadhara.md`;
- **two Vyāsatīrthas** — because Jayatīrtha's hagiographies are attributed to "his **direct disciple** Vyāsatīrtha," and the *munitraya* Vyāsatīrtha was born some sixty years after Jayatīrtha died (§8);
- the open **Jñānottama** question (`vimuktatman.md`).

**From which the corpus drew the rule: in a tradition of inherited monastic titles and repeated Brahmin family names, "same name" is weak evidence of "same person."**

Now the counterweight. **P. V. Sharma** proposed that there were **two** medical authors called Vṛnda — the author of the *Siddhayoga*, and a later **Vṛndakuṇḍa** who wrote a *Vṛndaṭīkā* on the *Carakasaṃhitā*. **G. Halder** elaborated. Meulenbeld declines it:

> "The evidence available is **not strongly in favour of this hypothesis**. Niścalakara and Śivadāsasena seem to refer **indiscriminately** to Vṛnda and Vṛndakuṇḍa, and Adhamalla mentions an author of glosses, who **may well be the author of the *Siddhayoga* himself**."

> **⭐ The rule, corrected.** "Different works under one name" is **weak evidence of different persons**, exactly as "same name" is weak evidence of the same person. **The pattern this chapter documents is a reason to *check*, not a licence to *split*.** Vṛnda is the case that keeps §7 honest, and it is why he is in this chapter.

## 8. A school partly authored by its systematiser {#retrospect}

The last mechanism is the largest, and it does not fill a blank — it **reshapes what is already there**.

**Veṅkaṭanātha** wrote more than a hundred works in **three languages** (Sanskrit, Maṇipravāḷa, Tamil), and Freschi's assessment of what that did is the sentence this chapter ends on:

> "what seems *a posteriori* to be a Vedāntic school would probably **not have appeared such to the school's contemporaries**"

He "**reconfigured his predecessor's contributions by giving them a new meaning**," and in particular **fixed Rāmānuja as the definitive reference point**. `vishishtadvaita.md` now carries the caution that follows: **it is describing his systematisation, to an unknown degree, rather than a pre-existing consensus.**

Two further turns of the same screw:

- **He criticised his own lineage.** Freschi (citing Clooney 2000): Yāmuna "accepted an **anti-Vedāntic proof for the existence of God, namely the inference**," which Veṅkaṭanātha criticised. **The smooth succession Nāthamuni → Yāmuna → Rāmānuja → Veṅkaṭanātha contains a methodological reversal at its third joint** — and it is the later man who names it. A lineage that looks continuous from outside was arguing with itself.
- **He was then claimed retrospectively himself.** The **Vaṭakalai** current "**retrospectively identified Veṅkaṭanātha as its founder** and adopted *en bloc* his theology," although he had *attempted a synthesis* on the very grace/free-will question that later split the school. The traces are concrete: Vaṭakalai authors began invoking **Hayagrīva rather than Gaṇeśa** on his precedent, and in **1676** dedicated a temple to Hayagrīva. **A man who tried to hold a synthesis together became the founder of one of the halves.**

And the same shape in Dvaita. **Jayatīrtha** is the second of the *munitraya*, and what he did was **structure the philosophical aspects of Dvaita** — Madhva's own works being elliptical. The *arguable* form of the system is his, not the founder's. His nonexistence-doctrine is the sharpest single instance in this batch of how far a school's vocabulary can travel:

> Jayatīrtha defines nonexistence as "**being the counterpositive of a constant absence**" — *pratiyogin* and *atyantābhāva*, **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika's own technical apparatus for negative entities** (Ch 38 §3.2–3.3). **A 14th-century Dvaita Vedāntin uses the Naiyāyika's machinery to defeat an Advaitin.**

Which is Ingalls's observation from the other end — "one finds Mīmāṃsakas and **Vedāntins** coming to use Navya-nyāya logic even in their diatribes against the Nyāya school" — reached by the corpus from a Harvard preface and a 2020 journal article **in the same batch, from sources with no connection to each other.**

⚠ **And even here, a check landed.** **Michael Williams** — the non-partisan specialist Batch 45 had named as the corrective to `vyasatirtha.md`'s reliance on a committed Dvaita partisan — went to verify **B. N. K. Sharma's** claim that Jayatīrtha already knew Gaṅgeśa, and reported: *"**I have not been able to locate this verse in any modern edition of the text.**"* An unlocated verse is not a fabricated one, and Williams claims only what he checked. **But that is what an independent check looks like when it lands.**

## 9. What this chapter leaves open {#open}

1. **Neevel 1977, pp. 14–16** — the actual argument that the ācāryas' lifespans were stretched. Wanted now by two nodes. The corpus has the conclusion at second hand and its own arithmetic; it does not have the argument.
2. **The *Siddhayoga* with Śrīkaṇṭhadatta's *Vyākhyākusumāvalī***, and the ***Ratnaprabhā*** — the corpus depends on these texts from **five** files and has never seen a line of either. **The most-requested unread texts in the Āyurveda layer.**
3. **Mādhava's *Mādhavanidāna*** — a structural gap. The *Siddhayoga* is organised on it, Tīsaṭa is partly dated by *not* following it, Vijayarakṣita's *Madhukośa* comments on it, and **the corpus has no node for any of the three.**
4. **P. V. Sharma**, whom this chapter meets **four times and always in rebuttal** — Meulenbeld rejects his positions on Tīsaṭa's sources twice, on Vṛnda's date, on the two Vṛndas, and on Nāradatta. **The corpus has never read him directly, and a file that only ever records the rebuttal side of a dispute is not neutral.**
5. **Hiriyanna's 1933 *Iṣṭasiddhi***, whose introduction would very likely settle Vimuktātman's date *and* whether the commentator Jñānottama is Citsukha's teacher. **Pursued this batch and failed**: the scan's OCR is unusable garbled Devanāgarī with no Latin at all. **Twice in one batch a located critical edition proved unreadable** (see also the 1888–1901 *Tattvacintāmaṇi*). For pre-war Indological editions, archive.org reliably supplies the **provenance** and reliably fails to supply the **text**.
6. **Is Meulenbeld's "D. Ch. Bhattacharyya" the "D. C. Bhattacharya" of `udayana.md` and `gangesha.md`?** If one man, the corpus is leaning on the same philologist in **two unrelated literatures**. ⚠ Flagged and **not** asserted — §7's discipline applies to modern scholars too.
