## 1. The three-tradition map {#overview}

Three of the world's most sophisticated philosophical traditions all grew up in the same geographic and cultural space (South Asia, roughly 800 BCE – 1200 CE) and all answered the same root question: **why do conscious beings suffer, and how is suffering ended?** They gave incompatible answers.

A single overview table before going into depth:

| question | Advaita Vedānta | Buddhism | Jainism |
|---|---|---|---|
| What ultimately exists? | One: Brahman (Sat-Cit-Ānanda) | Conditioned processes; no permanent substance | Six real substances (dravyas), all co-equal |
| What am I? | Ātman, which is identical to Brahman | A stream of arising/ceasing processes; no self | A distinct individual jīva — one of infinitely many |
| What is the problem? | Ignorance (*avidyā*) of ātman-Brahman identity | Craving (*taṇhā*) + ignorance (*avidyā*) of impermanence / no-self | Karma-matter binding the jīva via passion (*kaṣāya*) |
| What is karma? | Moral law / intentional-action residue (*saṃskāra*) | Mental formations (*saṃskāra*) — intentional, not material | Fine physical matter — literal particles |
| How does one get free? | *Jñāna* (direct knowledge of identity) | Eightfold path; insight (*vipassanā*) reverses dependent arising | Physical removal of karma (nirjarā) + stoppage (saṃvara) |
| What survives liberation? | Nothing individual — ātman was always Brahman | Nothing — there was no permanent self to survive | The distinct individual siddha, omniscient at lokāgra |
| Is there a creator God? | Not in the Advaita sense; Brahman = impersonal absolute | No | No |

The three traditions have different answers to every question in this table. The differences are not cultural flavour — they are structural incompatibilities. Calling them "three paths up the same mountain" requires claiming that these conflicts don't matter. They do.

## 2. The Advaita Vedānta system {#advaita}

*→ concept files: [brahman.md](../../concepts/brahman.md) · [atman-vedanta.md](../../concepts/atman-vedanta.md) · [karma-vedic.md](../../concepts/karma-vedic.md) · [moksha-advaita.md](../../concepts/moksha-advaita.md)*

### 2.1 Brahman — the one reality {#brahman}

Advaita Vedānta's starting point is radical: **only one thing ultimately exists.** That one thing is Brahman.

Brahman's essential nature is described in three terms — not its attributes but its very being:

| term | Sanskrit | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| Pure being | sat | Brahman simply is; it cannot not-be; it is the ground of all existence |
| Pure consciousness | cit | Brahman is awareness itself — not a thing that is aware, but awareness as such |
| Pure bliss | ānanda | Brahman's nature is uncaused, unconditional bliss |

*Sat-cit-ānanda* is not a list of three properties; it is a three-angle description of one thing.

**Nirguṇa vs. Saguṇa Brahman:** Brahman as-it-ultimately-is (*nirguṇa* — without qualities) is formless, impersonal, beyond time and space. Brahman as-perceived-from-a-devotional-standpoint (*saguṇa* = Īśvara) is personal God who creates, sustains, and destroys. Śaṅkara holds: same Brahman, two levels of description. Nirguṇa is the higher truth; saguṇa is a pragmatic concession to those not yet ready for full non-dual realisation.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Brahman ≠ Jain *sat* ≠ Buddhist *śūnyatā***
>
> Three concepts all concern "ultimate reality," but they are structurally opposite:
>
> | | what the ultimate IS | what it does with plurality |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Advaita Brahman** | positive, eternal, conscious absolute (Sat-Cit-Ānanda) | plurality is *māyā* — not ultimately real |
> | **Jain *sat*** | the triadic nature of existence that every dravya satisfies (utpāda+vyaya+dhrauvya) | plurality is real — six co-equal dravyas all exist |
> | **Buddhist *śūnyatā*** | emptiness of inherent existence — all dharmas lack svabhāva | plurality of processes is real; no substance behind them |
>
> Brahman fills the ultimate positively with consciousness. Buddhist śūnyatā empties it of inherent existence. Jain ontology refuses both moves — it distributes reality across six irreducible substances. None of these three positions is compatible with the others.

### 2.2 Ātman — the self that is Brahman {#atman}

The individual self in Advaita is called *ātman* (or *jīvātman* — the "living self"). The Advaita move is to identify it with Brahman. The Upaniṣads give this as a series of *mahāvākyas* (great sayings):

| mahāvākya | Upaniṣad | translation |
|---|---|---|
| *tat tvam asi* | Chāndogya Up. 6.8.7 | "That art thou" — the individual self (tvam) = the ultimate (tat = Brahman) |
| *ahaṃ brahmāsmi* | Bṛhadāraṇyaka Up. 1.4.10 | "I am Brahman" |
| *prajñānam brahma* | Aitareya Up. 3.3 | "Consciousness is Brahman" |
| *ayam ātmā brahma* | Māṇḍūkya Up. 1.2 | "This self is Brahman" |

The apparent individuality of persons — the experience of being a separate self — is a product of *avidyā* (ignorance) operating through *māyā* (cosmic illusion). Neither is ultimately real. This is a pre-existing identity that ignorance obscures, not an identity that liberation creates.

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Advaita *ātman* ≠ Jain *jīva***
>
> Both words are translated "soul." The structural difference is complete:
>
> | | Advaita ātman | Jain jīva |
> |---|---|---|
> | How many? | One only — ātman IS Brahman; plurality is māyā | Infinitely many distinct individuals, all permanently real |
> | After liberation? | Dissolves into Brahman (individual was never real) | Remains a distinct individual at siddha-śilā — permanently |
> | Relation to matter? | Physical world ultimately illusory (māyā) | Pudgala (matter) is co-equally real with jīva — six dravyas |
> | Path? | Jñāna (knowledge); no karma removal needed | Physical karma removal (nirjarā); knowledge alone insufficient |
>
> Calling Jain jīva and Advaita ātman "the same concept" imports Advaita's monism into a tradition that is explicitly and systematically pluralist.

### 2.3 Karma-vedic: the moral law account {#karma-vedic}

Karma in Hindu/Vedic tradition evolved across three periods:

| period | dominant meaning | primary texts |
|---|---|---|
| Early Vedic (~1500–700 BCE) | ritual action (*karman* = the sacrifice and its cosmic fruit) | Ṛg Veda; Brāhmaṇas |
| Upaniṣadic (~700–300 BCE) | any action and its moral consequence across lives | Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya Upaniṣads |
| Bhagavad Gītā (~3rd–2nd c. BCE or later) | dharmic action without attachment; karma-yoga as liberation path | Bhagavad Gītā, Chapters 3–5 |

**Key mechanism — niṣkāma karma:** Kṛṣṇa's teaching to Arjuna in the Gītā: act according to *dharma* (duty/right order) without attachment to results (*niṣkāma* = desireless). Acting with ego-attachment generates binding karma. Acting desirelessly, karma does not bind. The mechanism is *psychological*, not physical.

**Saṃskāra (residual impression):** Past karma leaves *saṃskāras* — subtle impressions on the soul/subtle body that condition future tendencies and experiences. These are latent dispositions, not physical particles. This is the closest Hindu analogue to Jain karma-matter — and the difference between them is the key structural divide.

| dimension | Hindu/Vedic karma | Jain karma |
|---|---|---|
| Ontological status | moral law / residual impression (*saṃskāra*) | fine physical matter (*sūkṣma pudgala*) |
| Role of intention | central — intentional action generates binding karma | secondary — *yoga* causes influx regardless; *kaṣāya* determines how much sticks |
| Can jñāna remove it? | yes — Advaita: realising ātman=Brahman dissolves karma | no — karma must be physically shed via tapas/nirjarā |
| Liberation mechanism | realisation (jñāna) OR desireless action (niṣkāma karma-yoga) | complete physical removal of all karma matter |

### 2.4 Mokṣa-advaita: liberation as recognition {#moksha-advaita}

Advaita's liberation is conceptually unlike anything in Jainism or Buddhism. It is not an event that happens in time; it is the direct recognition of what was always the case.

*Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* (traditionally ascribed to Śaṅkara, ~8th c., cited via Wikipedia — but modern scholarship, e.g. Michael Comans, judges the attribution "most probably erroneous" on stylistic grounds; treat it as a representative Advaita text, not securely Śaṅkara's own): "By reflection, reasoning and instructions of teachers, the truth is known — not by ablutions, not by donations, nor by breath control exercises." Jñāna is the sole proximate cause.

**Two forms:**

| form | Sanskrit | condition |
|---|---|---|
| Liberation while alive | *jīvanmukti* | ātman-Brahman identity recognised; body continues until natural death; no bondage despite embodiment |
| Liberation at death | *videhamukti* | the body drops; liberation is total |

**Jīvanmukti** is Advaita's most distinctive claim: you can be liberated right now, while alive, while the body continues. There is no waiting for karma to exhaust, no rising to a cosmic location. The Jain equivalent is impossible — Jain liberation requires the complete physical removal of all karma matter, which only occurs at stage 14 when the body finally drops.

### 2.5 Intra-Vedanta split: three very different Brahman-and-self relationships {#vedanta-split}

All three Vedānta schools use the same Upaniṣadic texts but read them differently — producing three mutually incompatible accounts of liberation:

| school | key thinker | position | what survives liberation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advaita | Śaṅkara (~8th c.) | ātman = Brahman; individuality is māyā | nothing individual — plurality was never real |
| Viśiṣṭādvaita | Rāmānuja (~11th c.) | souls are real, but are modes/attributes *of* Brahman | soul retains individuality in loving union with personal God |
| Dvaita | Madhva (~13th c.) | soul and Brahman are eternally, absolutely distinct | soul in eternal blissful proximity to God — never merged |

Advaita is the most radical position in all of Indian philosophy on the self-question: individuality is not transcended — it is recognised as never having existed. Critics within Vedānta (Rāmānuja, Madhva) rejected this as going too far.

## 3. The Buddhist system {#buddhist}

*→ concept files: [pratityasamutpada.md](../../concepts/pratityasamutpada.md) · [nirvana-buddhist.md](../../concepts/nirvana-buddhist.md)*

### 3.1 Pratītyasamutpāda — why things arise {#paticca}

The Buddhist diagnosis of suffering begins with a formula — the most compact expression of dependent origination (*idappaccayatā*, Samyutta Nikāya 12):

> **"This existing, that exists; this arising, that arises. This not existing, that does not exist; this ceasing, that ceases."**

Nothing arises independently. Every conditioned phenomenon exists in dependence on conditions and ceases when conditions cease. There is no permanent substance underneath — not a soul, not an absolute, not physical atoms with inherent existence. Only the web of mutual dependence is real.

**Why this matters for the self:** If everything arises dependently, then the "self" is also a dependently arisen process — not a thing. The felt sense of "I" arises dependent on the five aggregates (*khandhas*: body, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness). Remove the aggregates, and there is no "I" left over. This is the *anattā* (no-self) doctrine.

### 3.2 The twelve nidānas — the chain of suffering {#nidanas}

Dependent origination is spelled out in twelve links (*nidānas*) showing how suffering perpetuates itself across three lifetimes:

| # | Pali/Sanskrit | English | when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | avijjā / avidyā | **ignorance** (of impermanence, no-self, suffering) | past |
| 2 | saṅkhāra / saṃskāra | volitional/karmic formations | past |
| 3 | viññāṇa / vijñāna | consciousness | present |
| 4 | nāmarūpa | name-and-form (psycho-physical organism) | present |
| 5 | saḷāyatana | six sense bases (5 senses + mind) | present |
| 6 | phassa / sparśa | contact (sense + object + consciousness) | present |
| 7 | vedanā | feeling-tone (pleasant / painful / neutral) | present |
| 8 | taṇhā / tṛṣṇā | **craving** | present |
| 9 | upādāna | grasping / clinging | present |
| 10 | bhava | becoming | present |
| 11 | jāti | birth | future |
| 12 | jarāmaraṇa | aging-and-death + grief/dukkha | future |

The root is link 1: **ignorance**. Remove ignorance through insight and the chain reverses — "avidyā ceasing, saṃskāra ceases... jarāmaraṇa ceases." That cessation is nirvāṇa.

**The Buddhist vs. Jain diagnosis compared:**

| | Buddhist | Jain |
|---|---|---|
| Root cause of suffering | ignorance (*avidyā*) — cognitive | activity + passion — behavioural/physical |
| What causes bondage | ignorance → craving → grasping → rebirth | karma-matter flowing in via yoga, adhering via kaṣāya |
| Is there an enduring self? | no — anattā | yes — jīva is real and permanent |
| Liberation mechanism | insight reverses the chain; avidyā ceases | physical karma removal (nirjarā + saṃvara) |

### 3.3 Nirvāṇa — what cessation means {#nirvana}

The Pali Canon's *Udāna* 8.1–4 defines nibbāna negatively — by what it is *not*:

> "There is, monks, an unborn, unmade, unconditioned, unbecome. If there were not that unborn, unmade, unconditioned, unbecome, there would be no escape from the born, made, conditioned, become."

Positive language is avoided because positive language can only describe conditioned, relational things. Nirvāṇa is not conditioned, so it can only be approached by negation.

**Two forms:**

| form | Pali | state |
|---|---|---|
| Nirvāṇa with remainder | *sopadisesa-nibbāna* | defilements extinguished; physical body remains until natural death |
| Nirvāṇa without remainder | *anupādisesa-nibbāna* / parinibbāna | at death of an arhat/Buddha; the five aggregates no longer arise |

**What ceases in nirvāṇa:** The *arising* of the conditioned aggregates. Since there was never a permanent self, nothing "persists" in liberation. There is no individual consciousness waiting at a location.

**Internal Buddhist debate (Theravāda vs. Mahāyāna):**
- **Theravāda:** saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are genuinely distinct; nirvāṇa is the real cessation of suffering
- **Mahāyāna** (Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*): the saṃsāra/nirvāṇa dichotomy is itself a product of conceptual grasping; from the perspective of śūnyatā, no ultimate difference — "form is emptiness, emptiness is form"

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Buddhist nirvāṇa ≠ Jain mokṣa ≠ Advaita mokṣa**
>
> This was previewed in Ch 03 §10. Here it is stated in full:
>
> | | Buddhist nirvāṇa | Jain mokṣa | Advaita mokṣa |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Does a self persist? | no — anattā; "self" was never real | yes — distinct individual siddha at lokāgra | no — ātman merges into Brahman (was never separate) |
> | What is extinguished? | arising of conditioned aggregates | all physical karma matter | avidyā (ignorance of ātman-Brahman identity) |
> | Is liberation spatial? | no fixed location | yes — lokāgra, universe summit | no — Brahman is all-pervasive |
> | Liberation while living? | yes — arhatship (*sopadisesa-nibbāna*) | no — requires body-shedding at guṇasthāna 14 | yes — *jīvanmukti* |
> | Positive content? | deliberately left inexpressible | infinite knowledge/bliss/perception/energy | infinite being/consciousness/bliss (Sat-Cit-Ānanda) |
> | Karma mechanism | mental formations; intention-based | fine physical matter; consequence-based regardless of intent | moral law; dissolved by jñāna, not physically removed |
>
> All three use "liberation" as the goal-word. The answers they give are mutually exclusive on the most fundamental question: does any individual self survive liberation?

## 4. Ethics: non-violence across the three traditions {#ahimsa}

*→ concept files: [ahimsa.md](../../concepts/ahimsa.md) · [ahimsa-buddhist.md](../../concepts/ahimsa-buddhist.md)*

### 4.1 Jain ahiṃsā — ontological, absolute {#jain-ahimsa}

Jain non-violence is grounded in the jīva doctrine (Ch 03 §2): every living being has a jīva — a conscious soul with upayoga. Because every jīva can experience suffering and has the capacity for liberation, harming any jīva generates karma for the harmer. The scope of ahiṃsā is exactly coextensive with the scope of jīva.

**Six categories of jīvas — graduated scope:**

| category | Sanskrit | examples | senses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earth-bodied | pṛthvīkāya | soil, minerals | touch only (1 sense) |
| Water-bodied | jalakāya | water in natural form | touch only |
| Fire-bodied | agnikāya | fire, lightning | touch only |
| Air-bodied | vāyukāya | wind | touch only |
| Plant-bodied | vanaspatikāya | plants, trees | touch only |
| Mobile | trasa | animals, humans, insects | 2–5 senses |

One-sensed beings cannot defend themselves — harming them generates karma, though less than harming a five-sensed being. This is why Jain monks sweep the path before walking (to avoid harming insects), filter water before drinking, and avoid root vegetables (whose harvest kills the whole plant).

**The scope is absolute:** Even *unintentional* harm generates karma. The mechanism is physical — karma-matter flows in whenever violence causes yoga + kaṣāya, regardless of intent. There is no mental-state exemption.

**Three dimensions of the vow:** Non-harm extends through thought (*manas*), speech (*vāc*), and body (*kāya*) — the same three yoga-channels that cause āsrava. Violence in any dimension opens the karma-influx gate.

### 4.2 Buddhist ahiṃsā — intentional, relational {#buddhist-ahimsa}

Buddhist non-harm is grounded in *cetanā* (intention/volition) — the first precept of the *pañcasīla*: *pāṇātipātā veramaṇī* (abstaining from taking life). The textual anchor is **Dhammapada 10.129** (Arihanta Institute citing Pali Canon):

> "All tremble at violence; all fear death. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should neither slay nor cause to slay."

The basis is **empathic identification** — not an ontological claim about which beings have jīvas. Buddhist non-harm is *motivated by compassion* rather than mandated by a cosmic-harm-tracking mechanism.

**The four brahma-vihāras** — the positive psycho-ethical basis:

| brahma-vihāra | Pali | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Loving-kindness | mettā | universal goodwill to all beings |
| Compassion | karuṇā | empathy with others' suffering; desire to alleviate it |
| Sympathetic joy | muditā | rejoicing in others' well-being |
| Equanimity | upekkhā | non-reactive, non-partial balance |

Mettā and karuṇā together constitute the positive Buddhist ethic of non-harm. The precept against killing follows naturally from cultivating these states — it is not primarily a rule but a psychological orientation.

### 4.3 The intentionality gap — the key structural difference {#intentionality-gap}

The most important difference is not the level of care but the *reason* for the level of care:

| | Jain | Buddhist |
|---|---|---|
| Ground of non-harm | every jīva exists and can suffer → karma mechanism | beings suffer and I can alleviate it → compassion |
| Unintentional harm | **generates karma** — hence sweeping path, filtering water | **does not bind karma** if no harmful intent |
| Meat-eating | prohibited — killing for another's food = karma | allowed if three-pure (not seen/heard/suspected killed for you) |
| Scope | all beings with jīva, including 1-sensed (plants, fire, water) | sentient beings (conscious experience) |
| Mahāyāna override | no override possible — vows are absolute | bodhisattva compassion may override precepts in extreme cases |

> ⚠️ **Conflation alert — Jain *ahiṃsā* ≠ Buddhist *ahiṃsā***
>
> Both traditions are famous for non-violence. The surface resemblance is genuine — both produce extreme care for life, both have monastic traditions with elaborate rules protecting small beings. But the *mechanism* is completely different:
>
> Jain ahiṃsā is **ontological**: harm is harm regardless of intent because it flows karma into the harmer via the physical karma-mechanism. A Jain monk who unknowingly steps on an insect has accrued karma.
>
> Buddhist ahiṃsā is **intentional**: harm is defined by *cetanā* (volition). A Theravāda monk who unknowingly steps on an insect has not accrued karma — there was no harmful intention. What generates bad karma is the *mental act* of intending to harm.
>
> This difference is philosophical, not just practical. It follows from the two traditions' deepest disagreements: Jainism has a physical karma-mechanism operating regardless of mind; Buddhism has a cetanā-based karma mechanism where mind is primary. The ethics reflects the metaphysics.

## 5. The complete three-tradition comparison {#full-comparison}

### 5.1 Seven-dimension comparison table {#seven-dimensions}

| dimension | Jainism | Advaita Vedānta | Buddhism (Theravāda / early) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ultimate reality** | six co-equal dravyas (no single absolute) | Brahman — the one, non-dual consciousness | conditioned arising; nirvāṇa as unconditioned |
| **The self** | infinite many jīvas, permanently real and distinct | one ātman = Brahman; individuality is māyā | no self (anattā); stream of arising/ceasing processes |
| **Cause of bondage** | karma-matter (physical) via passion (kaṣāya) | ignorance (avidyā) of ātman-Brahman identity | ignorance (avidyā) + craving (taṇhā) |
| **Karma** | fine physical particles that accrete to jīva | moral law / intentional residue (saṃskāra) | mental formations (saṃskāra); cetanā-based |
| **Liberation means** | physical removal of karma (nirjarā + tapas) | jñāna (direct recognition); jīvanmukti possible | eightfold path; insight (vipassanā); arhatship |
| **Liberation content** | siddha: distinct, omniscient, blissful at lokāgra | identity with Brahman: Sat-Cit-Ānanda | cessation; "unborn, unmade, unconditioned" |
| **What the world is** | fully real; six dravyas co-equal | appearance (māyā / vivartavāda) on Brahman | conditioned processes; empty of inherent existence |

### 5.2 The one point all three agree on {#agreement}

Despite disagreeing on everything else, all three traditions agree on one premise:

**Ordinary unexamined existence is not the best that consciousness can achieve — and there is a way out.**

All three posit a *diagnostic* (here is the problem), a *prognosis* (here is what happens if untreated), and a *therapy* (here is the practice). The disagreement is not about whether liberation is possible but about what liberation *is*, what the *obstacle* is, and what kind of *effort* is needed to remove it.

This shared structure — diagnosis / obstacle / path / liberation — is what makes comparison productive. It is not evidence that the three paths lead to the same place. They don't.
