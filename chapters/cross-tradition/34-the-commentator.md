## How to use this chapter

Almost nothing in this corpus reaches us directly.

The *Caraka Saṃhitā* reaches us **inside Cakrapāṇidatta's gloss** — every modern edition prints them together. The *Suśruta Saṃhitā* reaches us inside Ḍalhaṇa's, which is the only one of its several commentaries to survive complete. Śaṅkara's Vedānta reaches us through two rival schools each named not for him but for a **sub-commentary on a sub-commentary**. The alchemical Raseśvaras reach us through **one chapter of one 14th-century doxography**, and everything anyone says about them descends from it.

Two earlier chapters stand next to this one and ask different questions of the same material. **[Ch 25](../hindu/darsana/25-commentators-and-authors.md)** asks *who* the commentators were and how an author can dissolve under scrutiny — its §1 is titled “Why the commentator is the philosopher,” and this chapter assumes that point rather than re-arguing it. **[Ch 29](29-transmission.md)** asks how texts survive at all: what the channels are, and what each one loses.

This chapter asks the third question. **What is a commentary as a *form* — what does writing one commit you to, and what happens to a doctrine when it passes through one?** Ch 25 is about authors, Ch 29 about attrition; this is about architecture. It is also the first of the three to work **across traditions**: Advaita, Āyurveda, Jainism and a Śaiva alchemical school, which is what lets §5 compare dating methods that no single tradition supplies on its own.

The seven concepts here were written as one batch and turned out to answer each other. Three are Advaitin, two are Āyurvedic, one is Jain, one is a school known only through a doxographer. They are not usually read together, and reading them together is what makes the pattern visible.

## 1. The stack, and why the school is named after storey four {#stack}

Start with a fact that looks like an accident and is not.

The two great schools of post-Śaṅkara Advaita are called **Vivaraṇa** and **Bhāmatī**. Neither is named after Śaṅkara. Neither is named after a work of Śaṅkara's. Both are named after a **commentary several removes down** from him.

Take the Vivaraṇa side, recorded in [Ch 30](../hindu/darsana/30-locus-of-ignorance.md):

1. Bādarāyaṇa's ***Brahma-sūtra***
2. Śaṅkara's ***bhāṣya*** on it
3. Padmapāda's ***Pañcapādikā*** — a gloss on that bhāṣya, covering the **first four sūtras only**
4. Prakāśātman's ***Pañcapādikā-vivaraṇa*** — a gloss on the gloss

The school is **Vivaraṇa**: storey four. Not the sūtras, not the bhāṣya, not even Padmapāda. And the reason is not deference gone wrong. It is that storey four is where the school's **operative doctrine** was actually fixed — *mūlāvidyā* as a positive (*bhāvarūpa*), beginningless root ignorance functioning as the material cause of the world. That is Prakāśātman's, not Śaṅkara's. A school names itself after the layer at which it became a school.

This has a consequence worth stating plainly, because it governs the rest of the chapter: **in this literature, the interesting move is usually several storeys above the famous name.** A reader who goes to Śaṅkara to find out what Advaita holds about the locus of ignorance will not find it, because Śaṅkara did not settle it. The commentators did, and they settled it differently.

## 2. Five texts, one name {#five}

The Bhāmatī side has the same architecture, one storey taller, and — this is the find — **the tradition counts the storeys and names the set.**

> "**Vedānta Śāstra** means 'the five texts — *brahmasūtra*, the commentary on it by Śrī Śaṅkara, the further commentary on it by Vācaspati Miśra, the commentary on the latter by Amalānanda named *Kalpataru*, and the commentary on that by Appayya Dīkṣita, named *Parimala*'."

| storey | text | author | when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Brahma-sūtra* | Bādarāyaṇa | — |
| 2 | *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* | Śaṅkara | 8th c. |
| 3 | *Bhāmatī* | Vācaspati Miśra | 9th–10th c. |
| 4 | ***Vedāntakalpataru*** | **Amalānanda** | 13th c. |
| 5 | *Kalpataru-parimala* | **Appayya Dīkṣita** | 16th c. |

Sit with what that definition does. Asked what *Vedānta Śāstra* **is**, this tradition does not answer with a doctrine, or a body of revelation, or a list of topics. It answers with a **five-member commentarial series**. In that usage the discipline *is* the stack.

### 2.1 Why storey five exists

Not for completeness. The *Kalpataru* is "renowned for its depth and complexity, often considered **too abstruse for most students**," and Appayya's *Parimala* was written — at the request of the Advaitin Nṛsiṃhāśrama — to make it approachable.

So: Amalānanda's continuation of the *Bhāmatī* required its own continuation in order to be readable. That is a compact description of what happened to Indian scholasticism between the 13th and 16th centuries, and it is not a complaint. A tradition that keeps arguing accumulates distinctions; distinctions accumulate technical vocabulary; technical vocabulary eventually needs a translator, and the translator is another commentator. Storey five is a **maintenance operation on storey four**.

### 2.2 What storey four was for

The *Kalpataru*'s job, on the reporting source's account, was "refining arguments, stabilizing definitions, and responding to objections drawn from contemporaneous schools such as Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and **Dvaita**."

That last word is datable, and it does real work. Dvaita is Madhva's, and Madhva is later than Vācaspati. So the *Kalpataru* is not merely explaining the *Bhāmatī*; it is **defending it against objections that did not exist when it was written**. This is the standing situation of a commentator in a live tradition: the text is fixed, the opposition is not, and the commentary is where a school does its fighting without admitting it has changed.

### 2.3 A commentator who worked both sides

Amalānanda also wrote a ***Pañcapādikā-darpaṇa*** — on the **Vivaraṇa** school's foundation text. Four independent reference sources report both works, and several describe him as "an early link between the Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa schools."

The corpus records the fact and **declines the inference.** Glossing a rival school's foundation text is not adopting its position, and not one source consulted says what the *Darpaṇa* argues. What can be said is narrower and still useful: the two "schools" were **close enough to share a commentator**. That alone is a corrective — it makes the Bhāmatī/Vivaraṇa split a disagreement inside one scholarly world rather than a partition between two. It is not a synthesis, and the node says so.

## 3. The same building in medicine {#medicine}

Now the same architecture where nobody expects it.

| root text | its commentary | the commentator | when |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Caraka Saṃhitā* | ***Āyurvedadīpikā*** | **Cakrapāṇidatta** | fl. 1040–1070 |
| *Suśruta Saṃhitā* | ***Nibandhasaṅgraha*** | **Ḍalhaṇa** | 12th c. |
| *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* | *Sarvāṅgasundarā* | Aruṇadatta | (see §4.4) |

Cakrapāṇi's is "the most famous among the commentators of Caraka Saṃhitā," and the *Caraka Saṃhitā* is printed with it as a matter of course — Jādavjī Trikamjī Ācārya's standard edition, the Sengupta five-volume edition, all of them. Ḍalhaṇa's case is starker: of the several commentaries the *Suśruta Saṃhitā* attracted, **"only Ḍalhaṇa's *Nibandhasaṅgraha* is completely available today."** The others survive in fragments or not at all.

Which means the surgical tradition of classical India reaches the present **through one 12th-century reader's decisions about what Suśruta meant.** Not because he was appointed to the role, but because his manuscript kept being copied and the others stopped. Ch 29 §1.4 calls this the material channel; here we can see what it selects *for*: a commentary that was useful enough to be worth the scribe's labour, over and over, for eight centuries.

And Ḍalhaṇa's title says what his book is. A ***nibandha-saṅgraha*** is a **collection of compilations** — it "compiles the views of a large number of authors and commentators… who lived before Ḍalhaṇa." The complete survivor is the one that had **absorbed the others**. That is not a coincidence; it is close to a mechanism. ⚠️ The corpus flags an unfilled hole here: **no source consulted says which earlier commentators the *Nibandhasaṅgraha* preserves**, which is the single most valuable thing about a compilation of lost works.

## 4. Four things a commentator does {#operations}

"Commentary" sounds like paraphrase. It is not, and the cases collected in this batch let us name four distinct operations — none of which is explaining what a word means.

### 4.1 Supply a mechanism the root text left out

Classical Āyurveda holds that each tissue turns into the next: *rasa* → *rakta* → *māṃsa* → and so on to *śukra*, as milk turns into curd. This is the ***kṣīra-dadhi*** model, and it has an obvious problem, which the tradition raises against itself:

> If conversion were **total**, then a month's fasting would leave the body in *śukrakṣaya* with **no intermediate tissues at all** — every stage having emptied into the next, with nothing held back.

Ḍalhaṇa's answer divides each tissue's *pariṇāma* three ways:

| portion | what it becomes |
|---|---|
| ***sūkṣmabhāga*** | the *upadhātu*, and the **next** dhātu |
| ***malabhāga*** | that tissue's own waste — the *dhātu-mala* of [Ch 31 §5](../hindu/shastra/31-the-ayurvedic-body.md) |
| ***sthūlabhāga*** | the **same** dhātu: its own upkeep |

Transformation is therefore **partial** (*krama-pariṇāma*), and the objection dissolves. Notice the shape of this. The objection is not a straw man — it is a genuine consequence of the doctrine as stated. And the answer is not a re-assertion; it is a **refinement that changes the doctrine's content**. Ch 31 described the Āyurvedic body as a set of rates; Ḍalhaṇa is the man who says what fraction of each rate goes where.

### 4.2 Extend a category

Suśruta defines *śalyatantra* — the surgical branch — as the removal of foreign objects: grass, wood, stone, metal, bone, hair, nails, pus. **Ḍalhaṇa adds *mala***, bodily waste, to the list of *śalya*s requiring extraction.

This is small and it is not trivial. It converts the surgical branch's object from *"things that came in from outside"* to *"things that must not be where they are."* And it moves *mala* across a line the system had drawn: what Ch 31 treats as **physiology** becomes, for the surgeon, a **foreign body**. One word added to a list, and a discipline's scope has changed.

### 4.3 Make a doctrine relative to the discipline asking

Suśruta counts **blood (*rakta*) as a fourth doṣa**, alongside vāta, pitta and kapha — which appears to contradict the tridoṣa framework the whole tradition rests on. Ḍalhaṇa supplies the justification, and the justification is the interesting part: *śalyatantra* is the branch concerned with ***vraṇa*** (wounds), and in wound pathogenesis *rakta* is the principal constituent. So blood is a fourth doṣa **for surgery**.

Stop and take that seriously rather than filing it as fudge. It means the doṣa-count in classical Āyurveda is **not one fact about the body but a function of which branch is asking.** A general physician works with three; a surgeon works with four; both are right, because a *doṣa* is being defined by what it explains in a given clinical domain rather than by what exists. That is a real methodological position — closer to Jain *naya*-relativity ([Ch 02 §4](../jain/02-jain-epistemology.md)) than to inconsistency — and the corpus declines to flatten it into an error.

⚠️ **And a source-handling note, because it applies to this whole section.** The paper that supplies §§4.2–4.3 reads Suśruta and Ḍalhaṇa "in parallel to western surgical knowledge" throughout: *mala*-as-*śalya* is glossed by faecal impaction in obstructive appendicitis, *rakta*-as-doṣa by haemoglobin and serum bilirubin in wound healing. **The corpus takes the philology and leaves the correlations.** Ḍalhaṇa's reason for admitting *rakta* is that wound pathogenesis is blood-centred — a statement inside the Āyurvedic system, which neither needs nor gains from a modern warrant. This is §4's "take the term, leave the editorialising," and it is the same handling Ch 31 §7 applied to the Caraka-Saṃhitā-Online gloss.

### 4.4 Split an axis the school had collapsed

The best case is Advaitin, and it corrects something [Ch 30](../hindu/darsana/30-locus-of-ignorance.md) presented as a single question.

Ch 30 sorts post-Śaṅkara Advaita on one axis: **where does avidyā live?** Brahman (Padmapāda, Prakāśātman) or the jīva (Vācaspati)? **Sarvajñātman** — the third holder of *pratibimbavāda*, and on that axis firmly on the Brahman side — turns out to take the opposite side of a **second, independent** question the one-axis picture hides:

| | Sarvajñātman | Prakāśātman | Vācaspati |
|---|---|---|---|
| **locus (*āśraya*) of avidyā** | pure consciousness | Brahman | the jīva, and **plural** |
| **material cause of the world** | **Brahman alone** — ajñāna is only a ***dvāra*** | ***mūlāvidyā* is** the material cause | māyā in the jīva, an **accessory** cause |
| **is avidyā *bhāva-rūpa*?** | yes | yes | yes, but plural |

Dasgupta's phrasing is exact: "pure Brahman is the ultimate cause of everything **through the instrumentality (*dvāra*) of ajñāna**," and Sarvajñātman "strongly holds that Brahman in association and jointly with ajñāna **cannot** be regarded as the material cause."

*Dvāra* is a doorway. A doorway is the **means** by which something passes, not an **ingredient** of what passes. So you can hold every Vivaraṇa-side doctrine — positive beginningless ajñāna, Brahman as its locus, reflection-theory for the jīva — and still refuse to let ignorance be the stuff the world is made of.

This matters beyond Advaita, because Ch 30 and the `prakāśātman` node both draw a comparison with Sāṃkhya: *mūlāvidyā* occupying [prakṛti's](../hindu/darsana/15-samkhya-yoga.md) job-slot as a single positive beginningless substrate that the world is made from. **Sarvajñātman is the counter-example.** For him prakṛti's slot stays empty and Brahman keeps it — and blocking exactly that inference was the point. The corpus's own comparison now has a documented internal objector, which is a better position than having it unopposed.

**The general lesson:** "the Vivaraṇa view" is a phrase that flattens two independent axes into one. Commentators are where you find out that a school's "position" was several positions.

## 5. Three ways to date a commentator {#dating}

[Ch 29 §5](29-transmission.md) asked what a name in a genealogy is worth, and answered: often very little. This batch supplies the comparison that section could not make, because it produced three figures dated by **three different methods**.

| figure | method | the evidence | result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cakrapāṇidatta** | **regnal anchor** | his father Nārāyaṇa and brother Bhānudatta served the Gauḍa king **Nayapāla**, whose reign is fixed at 1040–1070 from dynastic records | a **30-year window**, independent of medicine entirely |
| **Ḍalhaṇa** | **citation-bracketing** | he **quotes Cakrapāṇi** (fl. 1060); **Hemādri** (fl. 1260) **quotes him** | a **150-year bracket**, from nothing but texts |
| **Sarvajñātman** | **lineage arithmetic** | Śaṅkara d. c. 820 + a long pontificate + a claimed discipleship under Sureśvara | "c. 900… **may not be very far wrong**" (Dasgupta's own hedge) |

### 5.1 What the third one costs

The lineage method is the fragile one, and this batch caught it failing. A fourth source — found while researching Amalānanda — records that Sarvajñātman **salutes his guru as Deveśvara** in his own works, and that "some confusion exists regarding his direct lineage to Sureśvara."

That is the strongest kind of evidence available: what the author says about himself, in his own text. And it does not name Sureśvara. It does not refute the discipleship — Deveśvara may be an ordination name, and the corpus does not assume otherwise. But it means **Dasgupta's date is computed from a premise the text itself does not supply.**

### 5.2 The point none of the three states

Rank the three by precision and the medical men win easily. Rank them by *independence* and the order changes: citation-bracketing needs no patron, no maṭha and no hagiography — only two other datable authors and the fact that quotation runs one way in time. It is the only method a purely textual tradition can generate **about itself**.

But the real observation is about neither precision nor independence:

> **How well a pre-modern Indian figure can be dated is mostly a fact about what kind of institution they stood next to — not about how much was written about them.**

Cakrapāṇi is well dated because his family worked at a **court**, and courts are documented **by others**. Sarvajñātman is badly dated because his tradition is a **maṭha**, and a maṭha documents **itself**. The Advaita literature is vastly larger than the Āyurvedic commentarial literature and vastly less useful for chronology, because volume of self-description is not evidence. Ch 29 §5.4 stated the pattern for `govinda-bhagavatpāda`; here it has a control case in the same period, and the control case is a doctor.

### 5.3 And a case where the best edition does not help

Sarvajñātman's date has a third reading: that he was a younger contemporary of **Śaṅkara himself**, "ordained by the Bhagavatpāda himself and nominated to succeed him and Sureśvara" at the **Kāmakoṭi-pīṭha at Kāñcī** — which the Kāñchī tradition makes consistent by dating Śaṅkara to **500 BCE**. Total spread across the three readings: roughly **fourteen centuries**.

Here is the uncomfortable part. That claim's most prominent carrier is **N. Veezhinathan** — and Veezhinathan is the editor of the *Saṃkṣepaśārīraka*'s **critical edition** (University of Madras, 1985, collated manuscripts, introduction, notes, four indexes). The claim appears in the edition's own front matter.

§4 signal 5 says weight sources by provenance. This is the case that shows what that does **not** mean:

> **Provenance quality on the *text* does not transfer to provenance quality on the *biography*.**

The corpus takes Veezhinathan's edition as the best available witness to what the *Saṃkṣepaśārīraka* says, and declines its blurb as settling when its author lived. Those are two different kinds of claim, established by two different kinds of work, and a title page does not merge them.

## 6. The doxographic turn {#doxography}

Four of this batch's nodes involve books **about** the field rather than only **in** it. They are not the same genre, and the chapter's job is to keep them apart.

| author | work | when | what it does | genre |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ḍalhaṇa** | *Nibandhasaṅgraha* | 12th c. | absorbs the commentators before him into one surviving text | **compilation** |
| **Hemacandra** | *Anyayogavyavacchedadvātriṃśikā* | 12th c. | 32 verses turning *anekāntavāda* against **six named schools** — Vaiśeṣika, Nyāya, Vedānta, Sāṃkhya, Buddhism, Cārvāka | **refutation-doxography** |
| **Mādhava** | *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* | 14th c. | arranges every system in order, the Raseśvaras included | **ordering doxography** |
| **Appayya Dīkṣita** | *Siddhāntaleśasaṃgraha* | 16th c. | surveys **one school's internal divergences** — *ekajīva-vāda*, *nānājīva-vāda*, *bimba-pratibimba-vāda*, *sākṣitva-vāda* | **reconciling doxography** |

These commit their authors to different things. A compiler owes his predecessors preservation. A refuter owes his opponents accurate statement, and gets his force from having stated them fairly. An orderer owes the map completeness — which is why Mādhava has a mercury chapter at all. A reconciler owes his own school a demonstration that its quarrels are superficial.

What they share is a **stance**: from roughly the 12th century, a scholar's characteristic move is increasingly to write about the **map of positions** rather than only from a position. Four nodes, four traditions, one batch. The corpus offers this as a pattern it noticed, **not as a thesis it has argued** — four cases across four centuries is a suggestion, and the honest label for it is "worth testing."

### 6.1 Where Appayya parts from this corpus, in exactly one place

The *Siddhāntaleśasaṃgraha* is a 16th-century doxography of Advaita's internal disagreements: it collects earlier Advaitins' positions on the number of jīvas, on reflection-theory, on the witness-consciousness, and sets out the contrary views properly rather than only the author's own. Structurally that is very close to what a `contested` node in this repository is, and closest of all to `prakāśātman`, `sarvajñātman` and `vācaspati-miśra`.

It parts company at the last step. Appayya holds the divergent sub-schools to **converge** — "they all ultimately converge on the same fundamental insight."

§4 of this project's charter does not have that move available. **Convergence is an output**, which the signals either produce or fail to produce; a survey that guarantees agreement in advance has decided the question before collecting the evidence. The comparison earns its place precisely because the two projects look so alike and differ on **one** commitment — and the difference is not that Appayya was careless. He was writing *for* a school and needed it to be one school. This corpus is writing for nobody and does not.

Which is also why the *Siddhāntaleśasaṃgraha* is named in three nodes as an **upgrade path**. Whatever its framing, it is a pre-modern witness to what those men were taken to have held, by someone with access to texts that are now lost.

## 7. Two hard cases {#hard}

### 7.1 When the doxography is the only witness

The **Raseśvara** or mercurial school appears as chapter IX of the *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha*, and its argument deserves to be stated on its own terms before anything else, because it is a *philosophical* objection and not a recipe:

> "**Liberation is declared in the six systems to follow the death of the body… Therefore a man should preserve that body by means of mercury and of medicaments.**"

That is a charge of **deferral**. The orthodox darśanas put release after death, which puts it beyond reach while you live and beyond report afterwards. Accept instead that release must be *had*, and you notice what the premise costs: if liberation is to be enjoyed, someone must be left to enjoy it. The body stops being an obstacle and becomes a **requirement**. Everything alchemical follows — the eighteen operations on quicksilver, the three states (*swooning*, *dead*, *bound*), mercury as *pārada* "because it is a means of conveyance **beyond** the series of transmigratory states."

It is the mirror-image of *jīvanmukti* ([Ch 19](../hindu/scripture/19-hindu-scripture.md), and the `jīvanmukti` node): the same demand for release-while-alive, and the opposite conclusion about the body. For Advaita the *jīvanmukta*'s body persists incidentally as spent karma runs out, and its death changes nothing. For the Raseśvaras a durable body is a **necessary condition**. Same demand, inverted consequence — which is why those two nodes carry both a parallel edge and a NOT-equivalent edge.

**Now the problem.** Cowell and Gough's 1882 translation dates the school to "about the commencement of the Christian era," and Wikipedia repeats it — **with attribution to them**. So what looks like two sources is one 1882 source and its echo. Against that, three independent literatures date the root texts to the **10th–12th centuries**: Biswas (1990) gives the *Rasahṛdaya* to the 11th c. with P. C. Ray's 9th–10th as an alternative, and the *Rasārṇava* to the 12th; David Gordon White treats the *Rasārṇava* as 11th-century, drawing on a 10th-century *Rasahṛdayatantra*.

A school roughly a thousand years older than its own root texts is not a school. So the corpus splits the object: there is certainly a **raseśvara literature**, continuous and medieval; whether there was ever a self-identifying *darśana* rests on **one chapter in a doxography** — a genre whose business is arranging rivals in order. Mādhava is not thereby wrong; doxographies name real things. But the school's **unity is attested by the same source that supplies its doctrine**, and that caps what any node about it can claim.

This also closes a loop. Ch 29 §5.1 left `govinda-bhagavatpāda` with an alchemical second career that "fights the dates." The dates are now in, and they block the identification from **both** ends: Cowell and Gough's 1st century is too early for an 8th-century guru, and the 10th–12th-century *Rasahṛdaya* is too late. Two men, one name — the ordinary answer, reached by dating rather than by argument. And the pattern repeats: the *Rasaratnākara* is credited to a "**Nāgārjuna**," the 14th-century *Rasaratnasamuccaya* to a "**Vāgbhaṭācārya**" — whose homonym problem Ch 23 already documents from the medical side. **Rasaśāstra attracts prestigious authorship**, which is what one expects of a literature simultaneously technical, disreputable and enormously promising.

⚠️ **On mercury, in both directions.** Mercury compounds are toxic; chronic exposure causes hydrargyria. That is not in dispute and the corpus records it. What the corpus does not do is adopt the framing that this "contradicts the tradition's immortality claims" — a rebuttal aimed at raw quicksilver is not addressed to a claim about *bound* mercury after eighteen operations. **This is not a defence of the claim**; no source consulted analyses any preparation chemically, and the corpus takes no position on efficacy. §0 requires the same care in the debunking direction as in the inflating one, and the honest statement is that the argument and its refutation are about different objects. What can be said without any of that: the Raseśvaras made a **falsifiable-in-principle promise** of a kind the six darśanas were careful never to make.

### 7.2 When the commentator's own allegiance is the question

**Appayya Dīkṣita** wrote roughly a hundred works. Among them:

- the ***Kalpataruparimala***, which completes the five-text Bhāmatī series and won him the title ***Advaita-Sthāpanācārya***, "the teacher who established Advaita";
- the ***Nyāyarakṣāmaṇi***, his own gloss on Śaṅkara's bhāṣya;
- the ***Siddhāntaleśasaṃgraha***, the Advaita survey of §6.1;
- the ***Śivārkamaṇidīpikā***, on **Śrīkaṇṭha's** *Brahmamīmāṃsābhāṣya* — reconstructing an entire **Śaiva** theology from an obscure commentary;
- and titled demolitions of Madhva: *Madhvamata**vidhvaṃsana***, *Madhvatantra-mukha-**mardana***.

A 1580 inscription calls him "the sole emperor of **Śaiva** Advaita." Wikipedia holds the tension open: "widely remembered as an influential preceptor of the Advaita tradition," yet "historically rooted in the Śivādvaita school as its most prominent representative." **Jonathan Duquette**'s monograph — the first full study of the Śaiva oeuvre — goes further: Appayya "**single-handedly established**" Śivādvaita Vedānta, and the *Śivārkamaṇidīpikā* is its magnum opus.

The corpus does not settle this, and notes that the source most displacing the received picture is the one with the best provenance — whose thesis it knows only from a publisher's description, two scholarly fetches having been blocked.

But one result transfers, and it is the most portable thing in this chapter. Duquette's reading is that the Śaiva oeuvre was "mainly directed against **Viśiṣṭādvaita** Vedānta, the dominant Vaiṣṇava school of philosophical theology in his time and place." And Wikipedia notes that Appayya composed **no work explicitly directed against Rāmānuja's philosophy**.

Both can be true, and their compatibility is the point:

> **The opponent you title a book after and the opponent you build a whole theology against need not be the same opponent.**

Against Madhva — refutable in the ordinary polemical register — he wrote refutations, and named them. Against the school that actually held the ground he wanted, he built a **rival system**, which is the more serious form of attack and the one **invisible to a bibliography**. Anyone reconstructing a scholar's controversies from his tables of contents will get Appayya exactly backwards.

### 8.1 A dispute this chapter opened rather than closed

[Ch 31 §4](../hindu/shastra/31-the-ayurvedic-body.md) presented the tissue-nourishment *nyāya*s as **three incompatible mechanisms transmitted simultaneously** — contested *by design*, not by scholarly disagreement. Reading the Āyurvedic literature properly for `cakrapāṇidatta` complicated that in three ways at once:

1. **There are four, not three.** The fourth, ***eka-kāla dhātu-poṣaṇa***, is credited to **Aruṇadatta** on the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*.
2. **They are credited to different authorities.** Cakrapāṇi on Caraka for *kṣīra-dadhi* and *khale-kapota*; **Suśruta himself** for *kedārī-kulyā*; Aruṇadatta for *eka-kāla*. The scheme is assembled across the commentarial traditions of all three saṃhitās — so "credited to Cakrapāṇidatta," which is what Ch 31 §4.2 flagged as thinly attested, turns out to be a **compression that drops two of them**.
3. **The enumeration is modern.** The review's footnote for "there are four theories" cites not a classical text but a **2017 *Kriyā Śārīra* textbook**. The images and their classical loci are ancient; the numbered list of rival theories is modern pedagogy laid over them.

And the same review states the opposite relation between them: they "**function as a cohesive group… do not conflict or interfere with one another**," with *eka-kāla* as the frame in which the other three run at once.

The corpus retires neither reading. The disagreement turns on a question **neither source asks** — whether the classical texts ever offered these as *alternatives* at all, or whether the rivalry is an artefact of the same modern scheme that numbered them. That is exactly what upgrade path #2 would settle.

Point 3 deserves its own line, because it names a failure mode this project usually meets in the other direction:

> §0 warns against reading a modern concept **into** an old text. This is the same error running backwards: a **modern organising scheme mistaken for an old one**. Same mechanism, opposite direction, equally invisible if you only read the summaries.

## 9. Six questions {#questions}

1. The Bhāmatī school is named after storey 3, the Vivaraṇa school after storey 4, and *Vedānta Śāstra* is defined as all five. **What would have to be true of a tradition for its schools to be named after root texts instead?**
2. Sarvajñātman holds Brahman is the locus of avidyā **and** that avidyā is not the material cause. Ch 30 sorted the field on the first question alone. **What else might a one-axis map of a debate be hiding — and how would you notice?**
3. Cakrapāṇi is dated to a 30-year window because his brother had a job. **Name a figure in this corpus whose date would collapse to a decade if one non-textual record survived.**
4. Ḍalhaṇa says *rakta* is a fourth doṣa **for surgery**. Is that a doctrine about the body, a doctrine about medicine, or a doctrine about definitions? **Does the answer change what you would count as refuting it?**
5. Appayya's survey ends by declaring Advaita's sub-schools to converge; §4 of this project forbids assuming that. **Is there any case in which assuming convergence is the more honest starting point?**
6. The Raseśvara school is known through one chapter of one doxography. **At what point does "a school we know about only from its cataloguer" stop being a school and become a catalogue entry — and does the answer depend on anything except the number of sources?**

## 10. Check yourself {#check}

You have this chapter if you can say, without looking:

- **which storey** of the Vedānta commentarial stack each of Padmapāda, Prakāśātman, Vācaspati, Amalānanda and Appayya occupies — and why the schools are named where they are;
- what ***dvāra*** does in Sarvajñātman's account, and why it makes him a counter-example to a comparison **this corpus itself** draws between *mūlāvidyā* and prakṛti;
- Ḍalhaṇa's **three-part *pariṇāma***, and the objection it answers;
- the **three dating methods** of §5, which is most precise, which most independent, and the observation about institutions that neither of those rankings is really about;
- why **"provenance quality on the text does not transfer to provenance quality on the biography"** — and the specific case that forced the distinction;
- the difference between **compilation, refutation-doxography, ordering doxography and reconciling doxography**, and one author of each;
- what the Raseśvaras' argument **is** (the charge of deferral), why its date is contested, and why the corpus splits "raseśvara literature" from "Raseśvara darśana";
- why a scholar's real opponents are **not readable off his book titles**;
- and, for §8: the **four** *nyāya*s, who each is credited to, and which part of the scheme turns out to be from 2017.

If you can also say **how many verses** stand between this corpus and an upgrade to two of its Jain nodes, you have the most actionable thing in the chapter.
