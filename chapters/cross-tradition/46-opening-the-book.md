# Chapter 46 — Opening the Book: What Changed When the Corpus Finally Read Its Sources

> **Primary concepts:** `bhāmaha` · `śaurasenī` · `durgasiṃha`
> **Folder:** `cross-tradition/` — continuing [Ch 29](29-transmission.md), [Ch 34](34-the-commentator.md), [Ch 39](39-filling-in-the-blanks.md) and [Ch 45](45-the-witness-problem.md). A Prākṛt commentator, a **language** that is simultaneously a stage register and a **Jain doctrinal medium**, and a Kātantra commentator who may be three other people: the subject is transmission, not grammar.
> **Reads after:** [Ch 44](../hindu/shastra/44-the-grammars-that-lost.md) and [Ch 45](45-the-witness-problem.md) — this chapter **corrects both**, and the corrections are its point.

---

## How to use this chapter

Every previous grammar chapter in this corpus was written **about** books rather than **from** them. Chapter 44 said so itself: it "could describe six grammars and could not establish what any of them originally contained." Batch 49 closed by naming three free, located, unread primary sources and arguing that the next unit of work should be to go and read them.

**Batch 50 went and read them.** Two arrived; one turned out not to exist in a form this corpus is permitted to use. This chapter is what changed.

Four things to carry out, and three of them are corrections to chapters you have already read:

1. **The corpus's most interesting unsourced claim was true — and it meant something completely different from what was recorded.** A text growing from nine chapters to twelve was filed as book-architecture. It was **ontology**: the addition demoted the book's own subject from a genus to a species. (§2)
2. **"Free on archive.org" is not the same as "readable."** Two of the three target texts were free, located, and downloaded. One of them the corpus is **forbidden by its own charter** to use. (§5)
3. **The same gap can be read as a loss or as a terminus, and first-rate editors get it wrong in both directions.** (§3)
4. ⚠ **A §0 failure mode this charter has no name for**: inflation not into modern physics but into **modern politics** — and the specialist who names it aims it at the tradition this repository is anchored on. (§6)

## §1 What "reading the source" actually bought

Two books, both free, both open access, both read at first hand this batch:

- **E. B. Cowell, *The Prākṛta-prakāśa… with the Commentary (Manoramā) of Bhāmaha*, 1854** — a critical edition from six collated manuscripts, **with an English translation**. That last clause is why it is usable: §1 of this charter forbids original translation, and Cowell did the translating 172 years ago.
- **Andrew Ollett, *Language of the Snakes*, 2017** — the standing modern authority on Prākṛt. ⚠ **Batch 49 recorded this book as "located and NOT retrievable."** It is retrievable. Project MUSE serves no individual chapters and the author's own page returns 403 — both still true — but the Directory of Open Access Books copy sits on archive.org under a CC licence. **The corpus had recorded a dead end as a property of the book rather than of the route it tried.**

### §1.1 The first thing a first-hand reading gives you is a shape

Nobody had counted. Section by section, from Cowell's translation:

| § | subject | sūtras | | § | subject | sūtras |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | vowels | 44 | | VII | conjugation | 34 |
| II | single consonants | 82 | | VIII | verbal roots | 71 |
| III | conjunct consonants | 66 | | IX | particles | 18 |
| IV | "mixed" rules | 33 | | **X** | **Paiśācī** | **13** |
| V | declension | 47 | | **XI** | **Māgadhī** | **17** |
| VI | pronouns | 64 | | **XII** | **Śaurasenī** | **32** |

**521 sūtras. The three dialect chapters are 62 of them — 11.9%.** The entire grammar of three languages is shorter than the chapter on single consonants.

And the method is Pāṇini's, visibly: **I.1 and II.1 are *adhikāra*s**, **IV.1 turns on a *pratyāhāra***, and Cowell has to gloss I.1 by citing **Pāṇini I.1.70**. Batch 49's fourth test — is *paribhāṣā* Pāṇinian property or shared technology? — is confirmed here in a stronger form than that batch could reach: **the shared metalanguage crosses the language boundary.** A grammar of a *different language* is unreadable without Pāṇini's apparatus.

### §1.2 And the answer to the question the node had said it could not answer

`prakrta-prakasha` closed Batch 49 admitting it knew the text's "shape, attribution, dialect coverage and editorial history" and "**nothing about its contents** — not one rule, not its method, not how it states the relation between Prākṛt and Sanskrit, **which is the question a reader would most want answered**."

The relation to Sanskrit is **one sūtra**: `śeṣaṃ saṃskṛtāt` — *the rest, from Sanskrit*.

⭐ **The whole grammar is a diff.** It describes only what changes; everything unmentioned is imported wholesale. That is how 521 sūtras can constitute a language — the book never has to say what Prākṛt *is*, only how it differs.

## §2 The correction that matters most: a fact kept, a finding replaced

Batch 49 recorded that the *Prākṛtaprakāśa* "was originally composed in eight chapters and later expanded to 12," called it "the node's most interesting claim and its least well sourced," and filed it as the third instance of a **terminal-module** pattern shared with the Cāndra grammar and Hemacandra — an architectural habit.

### §2.1 The claim is confirmed, and the evidence is a single word

> "What had earlier been 'Prakrit' was reconfigured… as the species **'Māhārāṣṭrī': crucially, the word appears in the expanded version of Vararuci's *Light*, but not the older version.**"

**The core text never names its own subject, because it had nothing to contrast it with.** It was simply *Prakrit*. The label *Māhārāṣṭrī* appears exactly when the three dialect chapters do.

⭐ **This is the kind of argument worth holding up as a standard**: falsifiable, resting on the text's own vocabulary rather than on a colophon, and explaining *why* rather than merely *when*.

### §2.2 But the finding was the wrong size

> "These additional chapters represent a **pluralization of the category of 'Prakrit'**… The redactors clearly considered it a **genus**… Prakrit, now Māhārāṣṭrī, no longer stood **above** the other languages, but **alongside** them."

**Adding three chapters demoted the book's own subject from a genus to a species.** The text did not merely grow; **its object changed category.** And Ollett draws the consequence that makes it philosophy rather than book-history: "**genera do not have specific characteristics, and in this case, they do not have grammars.**"

⚠ **The corpus had the shape of the event and none of its content**, because it had read *about* the text and not *in* it. That is `DRIFT.md` D1 — distance from primary sources — as a worked example instead of a metric.

### §2.3 And the mechanism was not the one this corpus proposed

Chapter 44 offered: *the organisation that makes a grammar teachable is the organisation that makes its parts detachable* — "a pattern with three instances, not a law." Ollett supplies a **documented** mechanism for two of the three, and it is not that one:

- The threefold schema (Sanskrit / Prakrit / Apabhraṃśa) was replaced by a **sixfold** one adding **Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, Paiśācī**, first attested in **Rudraṭa, Kashmir, early ninth century**.
- The "expanded" *Light on Prakrit* is listed among the **multilingual grammars** of that moment; **Hemacandra's famous eighth book adopts the six languages as its organizing principle.**
- Behind that: a shift of critical focus from "**literature heard**" to "**literature seen**" — the theatre — traceable to Bhaṭṭa Udbhaṭa's Kashmiri *Nāṭyaśāstra* commentary under **Jayāpīḍa (779–813)**.

⭐ **The terminal module is not a pedagogical habit. It is a new genre appearing, and going at the end of existing books because it was added to them.** The corpus's synthesis is **superseded** for the two dialect cases and **untested** for the third (the Cāndra's Vedic chapters, which were *lost* rather than added). Recorded as supersession, not refutation — the earlier reasoning was wrong for an instructive reason: **it generalised from architecture when the evidence was about audience.**

## §3 The same gap, read twice — and both readings are made by good editors

`sauraseni` exists because two first-hand sources contradict each other about one fact.

**Cowell, 1854**, finds no Bhāmaha commentary on Section XII and reads it as **loss**: "In consequence of the **loss** of Bhāmaha's commentary on this Section, many of Vararuci's Sūtras are obscure and corrupt… **I have not attempted a translation of this Section.**" The judgement is not idle — on its strength he leaves untranslated the only section of a book he otherwise translated entire.

**Ollett, 2017**, finds the identical absence and reads it as a **terminus**: the chapter was not there to be commented on. And he does not argue from the absence. **Abhinavagupta, in the eleventh century**, describes a Vararuci "**excluding regional languages such as Śaurasenī**."

⭐ **This is not opinion against opinion. It is a dated external witness against an inference from a gap.** And it explains an anomaly found at first hand: **why the section that both other dialect sections declare as their base is printed last.** Because it was added last.

### §3.1 The error has a shape, and this corpus has met its mirror — and then made it

Chapter 44 caught Abhyankar reading **a transmission accident as an authorial choice** (the Cāndra's missing Vedic chapters).

This is the inverse: **a redactional terminus read as a transmission accident.**

⚠⚠ **And the corpus made it too, in this same batch.** The `bhamaha` node was written and committed stating the commentary was "lost," and built a paragraph on that loss as a demonstration of [Ch 45 §1.2](45-the-witness-problem.md). **That paragraph inherited Cowell's inference without testing it — hours after this same batch had caught Cowell's contemporaries doing the same kind of thing.** It was corrected from `sauraseni`, and the correction is left visible in the file.

⭐ **Both errors sit at the end of a text.** That is where "absent" and "lost" are hardest to tell apart, and — per §2 — where material is most likely to have been added. **A book's last chapter is the least trustworthy thing in it.**

### §3.2 What the correction cost and what it bought

Lost: the claim that Bhāmaha's work is incomplete, and the neat Ch 45 illustration built on it. **Cowell's failure to translate Section XII shows the sūtras were hard, not that a commentary had perished.**

Gained, and better: **Bhāmaha's stopping-point is a *terminus ante quem* for the expansion of the text.** He becomes a witness in the argument rather than a casualty of it. **The node gained a fact by losing a finding.**

## §4 Names, again — and one place where the corpus's own rule paid out in advance

### §4.1 The rule predicted the specialist

Batch 49 closed by sharpening the collision rule to: **distrust identifications that rest on the name *alone*.**

Applied to Bhāmaha *before* Ollett was read, it says: the *Manoramā*'s grammarian and the *Kāvyālaṅkāra*'s poetician share the word *Bhāmaha* and nothing any source argues. Ollett, reading a literature this corpus has not: "**I doubt very much that Bhāmaha, the author of the popular *Manoramā* commentary on *Light*, is identical to the scholar who wrote *Ornament of Literature*.**"

⭐ **A rule formulated from grammatical evidence in one batch anticipated a Prākṛtist's judgement in the next.** That is the strongest validation the method in §4/§5 of the charter has had.

### §4.2 And then had to be aimed more carefully

The corpus declined Ollett's supporting argument — that Abhinavagupta seems not to know the *Manoramā* — on the ground Batch 49 established: **an argument from a source's silence is worth close to zero.**

⚠ **But Ollett uses Abhinavagupta for two different things**, and only one is an argument from silence. The other is a **positive description** of the text's contents — and it is exactly what overturns Cowell in §3. **The node's first draft lumped them together and discounted both.**

⭐ **The rule against arguments from silence is a scalpel, not a broom.** Using it as a broom cost this corpus a real fact for several hours.

### §4.3 A generator that is not confusion but warrant

Every collision generator the corpus had catalogued — personal name, place-name, doctrine-name, short form, genre label, title in another śāstra — is a **mechanism of accident**. Ollett supplies one that is not:

> "**Vararuci-Kātyāyana was the go-to sage for authorizing additions and interventions in these new non-Pāṇinian systems.**"

**The attributions are not errors. They are claims.** A text departing from Pāṇini needs a warrant, and Vararuci is the sage on record departing from Pāṇini. ⭐ The corpus now separates two questions it had been running together: *is this the same man?* (usually no) and *why was he named?* (usually a reason).

### §4.4 Durgasiṃha: four identity questions on one name, and an attribution caught happening

Abhyankar raises three doubts about his own subject and resolves none: is Durgasiṃha **Durgācārya**, Yāska's commentator? Is "Durgasiṃha" a **title**, the man being **Amarasiṃha** of the *Amarakośa*? Is the sub-commentator the same man? Plus a legend making him **brother of Vikramāditya**.

But the best thing in the entry is not a doubt. It is a bare fact about two shelves:

> The Kātantra *Paribhāṣāvṛtti*, "explaining 65 Paribhāṣās. **No name of the author is found in the Poona manuscript. The India Office Library copy has given Durgasiṃha as the author's name; but it is doubtful whether Durgasiṃha was the author of it.**"

⭐⭐ **One text, two manuscripts, and an author appears in one of them.** §4.3's mechanism is a historian's generalisation; **this is the same thing visible in a catalogue entry, with no inference required.** It is the cleanest evidence this corpus holds for how the attributions it keeps distrusting actually get made.

⚠ **And it flags a finding.** Chapter 44 §7's census — T4, "confirmed in its strongest form" — counts "gloss by Durgasiṃha, 65 maxims, Kātantra" among fifteen collections. **Abhyankar doubts that attribution on the next page.** The *count* is untouched, and T4 was about the **distribution of a technique**, which survives an anonymous author perfectly well. The *attribution* does not.

### §4.5 Royal patrons do the same work as divine ones

Sarvavarman is fastened to the **Sātavāhana** court; Durgasiṃha to **Vikramāditya**'s; Vararuci appears both among Vikramāditya's "nine gems" and at the **Nanda** court. Three grammarians of three rival systems, three founding kings.

⭐ Set beside Chapter 44's divine warrants — the Kātantra from Kārttikeya, Pāṇini's sūtras from Maheśvara — **both are answers to one question: by what authority does this book depart from Pāṇini?** §4.3's generator, showing up as biography instead of as attribution.

## §5 The follow-up that was mis-specified — and how the corpus found out

Batch 49 carried **T3** (does Haradatta's non-hierarchical reading of the three sages survive first-hand checking?) as "**not blocked; not done**," and called it "the cheapest high-value item this node opens," because the Kāśikāvṛtti *with* the *Padamañjarī* is free on archive.org.

**This batch went and got it.** 3.8 MB, and the OCR is garbled Devanagari.

⚠ **But the OCR is not the real obstacle**, and saying so would let the corpus off too lightly. Even with perfect OCR, **the *Padamañjarī* is an untranslated Sanskrit commentary, and §1 of this charter forbids this corpus from translating it.** What T3 requires is a published **translation or study**. A bounded search found none.

⭐⭐ **"The text is free on archive.org" was never a reason to think T3 was cheap.** Free access to a primary text the corpus may not translate buys nothing on its own. **T3 remains not discharged, and the reason has changed from logistical to structural** — which is a more useful thing to know than another deferral.

⚠ **The general lesson for the queue, and it applies to `DRIFT.md` D1 directly**: a *located* source is not a *read* one, and an **untranslated** located source may not be a readable one at all. **A follow-up list that does not distinguish these makes the backlog look healthier than it is.**

### §5.1 What the attempt did produce

Going to the **Dharmaśāstra** side of collision #12 — is the grammarian Haradatta the Āpastamba commentator? — found a Haradatta placed in **South India**, "**A.D. 1200?**", with four Dharmaśāstra commentaries, **no mention of the *Padamañjarī*** — and singled out for commentaries "**meticulous in grammar**."

⭐ A Dharmaśāstra reference work, apparently unaware of the *Padamañjarī*, independently notes its Haradatta's **grammatical precision**. **That is style-and-substance evidence rather than name-evidence** — precisely what Ch 45 concluded carried the load in the Jinendrabuddhi case. It is the first such evidence here.

⚠ **Still not diagnosed.** The omission cuts both ways (§4.2). "Śaivite" vs "advaitin" diverges. The source is one the corpus has already caught misattaching an appositive. And **Olivelle** — who wrote a dedicated article on precisely this question — is **paywalled**: Springer redirects to an authorization endpoint, academia.edu returns 403, the DOI lookup 404s. ⭐ **The sharpest single D2 item the corpus holds: a specialist has answered the question and the corpus cannot read the answer.**

## §6 A §0 failure mode the charter has no name for

`sauraseni` connects this material to the deepest layer of this repository. Pischel's classification of the Jain Prākṛts:

| language | where |
|---|---|
| **Ardhamāgadhī** | the Śvetāmbara canon |
| **Jain Śaurasenī** | ⭐ the **Digambara doctrinal literature** — so `kundakunda` and `samayasāra` are in it |
| **Jain Māhārāṣṭrī** | commentary and narrative, both sects |

Nothing in this repository previously said what language its Jain sources were in.

⚠⚠ **And the warning travels with the fact.** Ollett, immediately:

> the proposition "that the use of Prakrit is part of what characterizes Jainism as an **inclusive and egalitarian religion** in contrast to the Brahmanical traditions, which insisted on using the obscure and exclusive Sanskrit language" rests on "a **threefold continuity**" that is assumed rather than argued — and Alsdorf's version of it ("an uninterrupted tradition on the soil of the motherland," "anti-brahmanic, popular linguistic origins") is "plausible, if **sentimental and indigenist**."

⭐⭐ **§0 of this charter guards against inflation into modern physics. This is inflation into modern politics, by an identical mechanism**: a real structural fact (Jains wrote in Prākṛts) quietly mapped onto a modern category (popular, egalitarian, anti-elite), with the mapping never stated as a mapping. **"If a reading only works by quietly mapping a Sanskrit/Prākṛt term onto a modern concept, that reading is wrong"** — §0 already covers it in principle, and the corpus had only ever applied it to physics.

⚠ **And it is aimed at the tradition this repository is anchored on, by the specialist the repository has just started reading.**

## §7 What to carry out of this chapter

1. **Read the book.** Two of Batch 49's three named sources were free and unread; reading them **confirmed the corpus's headline claim, replaced the finding built on it, superseded a mechanism the corpus had proposed, and corrected three committed files.** None of that was available from summaries.
2. **A dated external witness beats an inference from a gap** — even when the inference is made by the editor holding the manuscripts.
3. **The rule against arguments from silence is a scalpel.** It killed a real fact in this batch before being narrowed.
4. **Distinguish *located* from *readable*.** A free primary text this corpus may not translate is not a discharged follow-up, and a queue that does not say so overstates its own health.
5. ⚠ **The §0 discipline is not only about physics.** The same move — a structural fact mapped onto a flattering modern category — is available for politics, and here a specialist catches it being made about Jainism.
