# Chapter 22 — Four and Four: Equanimity, Compassion, and the Same Words in Three Soteriologies

**Concepts covered:** mettā · karuṇā · muditā · upekkhā (the four *brahmavihāra*) · maitrī-jain · pramoda · kāruṇya · mādhyasthya (the four Jain *bhāvanā* of TS 7.11) · ahiṃsā-vedic · with samatva · dāna · ahiṃsā · ahiṃsā-buddhist cross-referenced
**Confidence:** medium throughout, and **evenly so** — an unusual property in this corpus. The Jain half rests on **Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.11 directly fetched**, with Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi* gloss read through secondary quotation; the Buddhist half rests on an encyclopedia article plus **Nyanaponika Thera's "The Four Sublime States"** (a Theravāda monastic exposition), converging on definitions, canonical loci, and the near/far-enemy diagnostic. **Neither the *Visuddhimagga* nor the Pāli suttas were directly fetched**, and the Sanskrit of *Sarvārthasiddhi* was not read from a critical edition.
**Nothing here is `contested`.** This is the corpus's cleanest cross-tradition cluster: every node converged, and the interest lies entirely in **how precisely the parallel holds and exactly where it breaks**.

---

## How to use this chapter

The earlier cross-tradition chapters took **metaphysics** (Ch. 04: brahman, ātman, karma, mokṣa across three traditions) and **epistemology** (Ch. 05: what counts as a valid way of knowing). This one takes **ethics** — specifically the affective ethics: not what you must not do, but what you are to *cultivate*.

**Remit note.** *Ahiṃsā* (Jain) and *ahiṃsā-buddhist* are primary-covered in **Ch. 04**; the Jain vow-system is **Ch. 08**; *samatva* and *dāna* are **Ch. 19 §6.3.1** and **§8.2**. This chapter takes the **two quartets** and the **Hindu ahiṃsā** node that had no home, and cross-references the rest.

**The organising question, and it is unusually sharp.** Three Indian traditions each hold a set of **four** cultivated attitudes toward other beings. Two of the sets use **the identical four Sanskrit words**. The third shares two of the four and swaps the others. They are obviously related. So:

> **Is this one doctrine that three traditions inherited — or three doctrines that happen to have collided in vocabulary?**

The answer is neither, and getting to it is what this chapter is for. §4 shows that the two sets **partition different things**: the Buddhist four are four *attitudes* radiated to *all beings*, while the Jain four are four attitudes each aimed at *its own specified class of person* — and the class-names are written into the sūtra itself. Same words, same count, and a different logical shape underneath.

## 1. The three quartets, side by side {#quartets}

| # | **Buddhist** — *brahmavihāra* / *appamaññā* | **Yoga-Sūtra 1.33** | **Jain** — the four *bhāvanā*, TS 7.11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **mettā** / maitrī — loving-kindness | **maitrī** | **maitrī** — benevolence to all beings |
| 2 | **karuṇā** — compassion | **karuṇā** | **pramoda** — joy at the virtuous |
| 3 | **muditā** — sympathetic joy | **muditā** | **kāruṇya** — compassion for the afflicted |
| 4 | **upekkhā** / upekṣā — equanimity | **upekṣā** | **mādhyasthya** — equanimity toward the ill-behaved |

Three things fall out of the table immediately, and each is a finding.

**First: Patañjali's list is not a parallel — it is the same list.** Yoga-Sūtra 1.33 gives *maitrī, karuṇā, muditā, upekṣā*: **the Buddhist four in their Sanskrit forms, in the Buddhist order.** The corpus records this in four separate concept files without editorialising, and this chapter will not either — a shared list of four technical terms in the same sequence is a strong indication of contact between the Yoga and Buddhist traditions, but **which direction, and through what channel, is not something these sources establish**, and the corpus does not assert it. (Ch. 15 records the broader Sāṃkhya-Yoga/Buddhist entanglement; Ch. 19 §6.3.1 records the *other* collision between these two traditions — that "yoga" itself means one thing in the Gītā and another in Patañjali.)

**Second: the Jain set shares positions 1 and 3 and replaces 2 and 4.** *Maitrī* is literally the same word. *Kāruṇya* is the same root as *karuṇā*. But position 2 is **pramoda**, not *muditā*, and position 4 is **mādhyasthya**, not *upekṣā* — two different Sanskrit words for what look like the same slots.

**Third — and this is easy to miss — the order differs.** Read the two outer columns down:

- **Buddhist:** loving-kindness → **compassion** → **joy** → equanimity
- **Jain:** benevolence → **joy** → **compassion** → equanimity

**Positions 2 and 3 are swapped.** In the Buddhist sequence compassion precedes joy; in the Jain sequence joy precedes compassion. That is not a scribal accident — §4 shows it follows from what each set is a partition *of*.

## 2. The Buddhist four — attitudes without an object-restriction {#brahmavihara}

*→ concept files: [metta.md](../../concepts/metta.md) · [karuna.md](../../concepts/karuna.md) · [mudita.md](../../concepts/mudita.md) · [upekkha.md](../../concepts/upekkha.md)*

The four are called ***brahmavihāra*** ("divine abidings") and, equivalently, ***appamaññā*** — **"the immeasurables."** That second name is the load-bearing one: *appamaññā* means they are **radiated without limit or partiality**, which is a claim about their *object*, not their intensity. Canonical loci: the **Tevijja Sutta** (DN 13) and the *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* (Sn 1.8 / Khp 9), with Buddhaghosa's 5th-c. ***Visuddhimagga*** as the commentarial anchor.

**Mettā** (from *mitra*, "friend" — **universalised friendliness**) is boundless impartial goodwill: *"may all beings be happy."* First brahmavihāra, first immeasurable, and in Theravāda the first of the ten *pāramī*. Its canonical simile, from the Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta: *"Just as a mother would protect her only child at the risk of her own life, even so let one cultivate a boundless heart toward all beings."* The practice — ***mettā-bhāvanā*** — radiates well-wishing in **widening circles: oneself → loved ones → neutral persons → difficult persons → all beings in all directions.**

**Karuṇā** is **mettā meeting suffering**: the wish that beings be *free from* suffering. It "takes away from the heart the inert weight, the paralyzing heaviness" (Nyanaponika).

**Muditā** is **mettā meeting good fortune**: gladness at others' happiness and success, "undiminished by envy or comparison."

**Upekkhā** is **equanimity — "a perfect, unshakable balance of mind, rooted in insight."** It holds the other three in balance: neither the warmth of mettā nor the ache of karuṇā nor the gladness of muditā, but even regard toward all beings — pleasant, unpleasant, neutral — recognising that beings are heirs to their own *kamma*.

**Why exactly four, and why these four.** The concept file for muditā gives the cleanest statement of the set's logic, and it is worth quoting the reasoning: mettā and karuṇā orient the mind toward beings who **lack** well-being or who **suffer** — but **without muditā the practitioner has no trained response to beings who are flourishing, and untrained, that response defaults to envy.** So the four together cover the complete field of another's fortune:

| the other's condition | the trained response |
|---|---|
| any / all conditions | **mettā** — wish them well |
| suffering | **karuṇā** — relieve it |
| flourishing | **muditā** — rejoice in it |
| any, sustained over time | **upekkhā** — remain balanced |

The concept file also notes that **muditā is the least-discussed brahmavihāra in popular practice yet structurally essential** — which is a fair diagnosis of a set that is usually reduced, in Western presentation, to "loving-kindness meditation."

### 2.1 Near and far enemies — a diagnostic with no Jain counterpart {#enemies}

The *Visuddhimagga* defines each brahmavihāra **by what it is not** — and by what *counterfeits* it, which is the more useful half:

| virtue | **far enemy** (its opposite) | **near enemy** (its counterfeit) |
|---|---|---|
| **mettā** | ill-will, hatred (*vyāpāda*, *dosa*) | **selfish or preferential affection** (*rāga*) — partial and possessive |
| **karuṇā** | cruelty; callous indifference | **sentimental pity** — grief that sinks into the sufferer's distress and weakens the capacity to help |
| **muditā** | envy, resentment, jealousy | **frivolous or conditional pleasure** — worldly elation dependent on personal benefit |
| **upekkhā** | agitation; craving-and-aversion (reactivity) | **indifference, apathy, callous disengagement** |

**The near-enemy column is the whole point of the diagnostic**, and it is a genuinely sophisticated piece of moral psychology. A far enemy is easy: nobody mistakes hatred for love. **A near enemy is dangerous precisely because it feels like the virtue** — and in each case it is the *partial*, *self-referential* version of it:

- Affection that clings looks like mettā. It is not, because it is **preferential**: *"Love, without desire to possess … this is the highest love"* (Nyanaponika). Mettā is explicitly **not** romantic love, attachment, or fondness for those who please us.
- Pity looks like karuṇā. It is not, because being overwhelmed by another's suffering **disables** the response that compassion exists to enable. **True karuṇā remains clear and effective; its counterfeit is to be overwhelmed.**
- Conditional delight looks like muditā. It is not, because it is gladness at *my* benefit wearing another's face.
- And **indifference looks like equanimity** — the most consequential of the four. Genuine upekkhā is **"rooted in insight"** (into impermanence and not-self); **"its counterfeit is rooted in not bothering."** That single contrast is the most useful sentence in this chapter.

**No corresponding diagnostic exists in the Jain set.** The Jain files gloss each *bhāvanā* by its object and its function, not by its counterfeit. This is a real asymmetry in the two traditions' **method** — and it is worth registering as such rather than as a deficiency: the near/far-enemy scheme is a *commentarial* achievement (Buddhaghosa, 5th c.), not a canonical one, and the Jain tradition put its commentarial energy elsewhere (Ch. 09, Ch. 10, and the Tattvārtha reading-room show where).

**One further point about upekkhā that distinguishes it from the other three.** It is not only a social attitude — it is also a ***bojjhaṅga***, a **factor of awakening** (*upekkhā-bojjhaṅga*), the mature non-reactive equanimity the path develops toward liberation. And its grounding is doctrinally specific: **the realisation of *anattā*.** When the "I" that would cling or recoil is seen through, balance follows. Equanimity in this system is not willpower applied to the emotions; it is what remains when a particular metaphysical error is corrected. Hold onto that — §4.3 shows the Jain equanimity resting on something else entirely.

## 3. The Jain four — one verse, four objects {#bhavana}

*→ concept files: [maitri-jain.md](../../concepts/maitri-jain.md) · [pramoda.md](../../concepts/pramoda.md) · [karunya.md](../../concepts/karunya.md) · [madhyasthya.md](../../concepts/madhyasthya.md)*

The Jain set has a single canonical source, and **the corpus fetched it directly** — **Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.11**:

> *maitrī-pramoda-kāruṇya-mādhyasthāni ca sattva-guṇādhika-kliśyamānāvineyeṣu*
>
> "Benevolence toward all living beings (*maitrī*), joy at the sight of the virtuous (*pramoda*), compassion for the afflicted (*kāruṇya*), and tolerance toward the insolent and ill-behaved (*mādhyasthya*) are the right sentiments."

**Read the sūtra's own structure.** It is a *dvandva* compound of four sentiments followed by a locative compound of **four matching recipient-classes**, in order:

| sentiment | its object, **named in the verse** |
|---|---|
| **maitrī** | ***sattva*** — all living beings |
| **pramoda** | ***guṇādhika*** — those **superior in virtue** |
| **kāruṇya** | ***kliśyamāna*** — those who are **suffering** |
| **mādhyasthya** | ***avineya*** — the **unteachable**, the undisciplined |

**This is the structural fact the rest of the chapter turns on.** The Jain verse does not simply list four attitudes; it **pairs each attitude with its own class of person.** Three of the four objects are *restricted*.

The glosses, from Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi* (read through secondary quotation, not from a critical edition):

- **maitrī** — "the desire that all beings be free from suffering and pain," directed without limit at every *jīva*. **This is the positive affective ground beneath the ahiṃsā vow:** where ahiṃsā is the restraint (*do no injury*), maitrī is the cultivated goodwill from which the restraint flows. *To be a friend to all is to be unable to wish anyone harm.*
- **pramoda** — "fervent affection together with veneration in the presence of the virtuous." The antidote to envy, and specifically to ***māna*** (pride) — one of the *kaṣāyas* — which resents another's superiority.
- **kāruṇya** — "**the disposition to render assistance to the afflicted**." Not mere feeling but the **active will to relieve suffering**.
- **mādhyasthya** — "freedom from attachment and repulsion based on desire and aversion." Its object is the person who is uncivil, cruel, or beyond correction: rather than meeting them with anger (which would bind karma through the *kaṣāya*), the practitioner holds an even mind. **Internal non-attachment in its hardest case.**

> **A distinction the concept file insists on, and it removes the obvious objection.** Mādhyasthya is **not** approval of bad conduct, and it is **not** callous indifference to suffering — **the afflicted are the object of *kāruṇya*, not *mādhyasthya***. The verse assigns them to different sentiments precisely so that equanimity toward the incorrigible cannot be mistaken for coldness toward the suffering. The object-restrictions are doing protective work.

**And the purpose of the whole set is specific.** These are the attitudinal supports a Jain cultivates **to strengthen the vows and stop karmic influx** — oriented to ***stilling the kaṣāyas*** (the passions: anger, pride, deceit, greed) that bind karmic matter to the *jīva* (Ch. 03). That is a mechanical goal in a system where karma is literally particulate. The bhāvanās are not cultivated for their own sake, nor primarily as a meditative attainment; they are **cultivated because passion is what makes karma stick.**

## 4. Where the parallel breaks — three answers {#break}

Now the comparison can be made properly. The two sets differ on **object**, on **order**, and on **mechanism** — and the three differences are one difference seen three ways.

### 4.1 The sets partition different things {#partition}

> **The Buddhist four are four attitudes, each radiated to *all beings without limit*. The Jain four are four attitudes, each directed at *its own class of person*.**

That is why the Buddhist set is called ***appamaññā*, "the immeasurables"** — the name is a claim that **no object-restriction applies**. Mettā is radiated to all beings; so is karuṇā; so is muditā; so is upekkhā. The practice explicitly widens *"to all beings in all directions."* The four divide **the practitioner's stance**, not the population.

And TS 7.11 does the opposite: it names *sattva*, *guṇādhika*, *kliśyamāna*, *avineya* — **one universal class and three restricted ones.** The four divide **the population**, assigning to each kind of person the appropriate response.

Two consequences follow, and they are not small:

**(a) The Jain set is a scheme of *social discrimination* in the neutral sense** — a rule for what is owed to whom. The Buddhist set is a scheme of *undifferentiated radiation*. One tells you to treat the virtuous, the suffering, and the incorrigible **differently**; the other tells you to extend the same four attitudes to **everyone**, the incorrigible included.

**(b) The order-swap is explained.** Buddhist order runs mettā → karuṇā → muditā → upekkhā because it moves through **the practitioner's states** in a natural sequence of expansion: goodwill, then its response to pain, then its response to joy, then its stabilisation. The Jain order runs maitrī → pramoda → kāruṇya → mādhyasthya because it moves through **classes of person, ranked**: all beings, then those **above** you in virtue, then those **below** you in fortune, then those **outside** correction altogether. **One list is ordered by inner development; the other by social position.** Positions 2 and 3 are swapped because they are answering different questions.

### 4.2 *Muditā* and *pramoda* are not the same attitude {#mudita-pramoda}

The corpus draws both `structurally-parallel-to` **and** `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` between them — the sanctioned two-edge pattern — and the reason is the object-restriction again:

| | **muditā** | **pramoda** |
|---|---|---|
| object | **another's good fortune of any kind** | specifically the ***guṇādhika*** — those **superior in spiritual virtue** |
| what it opposes | envy at another's happiness | envy, and specifically ***māna*** (pride) resenting another's superiority |
| what it serves | the brahmavihāra path toward awakening | **stilling the kaṣāyas** that bind karmic matter |

So muditā is gladness at *someone else's luck*; pramoda is **veneration of someone else's spiritual attainment.** A Buddhist practitioner is to rejoice when a stranger prospers. A Jain practitioner is to rejoice at the sight of a person **further along the path than they are** — which is a narrower and more specifically religious emotion, and one aimed at a specific passion (pride) in a system that enumerates the passions.

### 4.3 The two equanimities rest on different foundations {#two-equanimities}

This is the deepest of the three breaks, and it is not visible in the words at all.

| | **upekkhā** | **mādhyasthya** |
|---|---|---|
| object | all beings, without restriction | the ***avineya*** — the insolent and ill-behaved |
| grounded in | **insight into *anattā*** — no self that clings or recoils | ***kaṣāya*-restraint** — refusing the passions that bind karma to an **eternal, real** *jīva* |
| also functions as | a **factor of awakening** (*upekkhā-bojjhaṅga*) | a guard on the vow; the affective side of *aparigraha* (non-possession) |
| near enemy | **indifference** — explicitly excluded | **not** approval or callousness — explicitly excluded |

**The two are grounded in opposite metaphysics of the self.** Buddhist equanimity follows from there being **no self** to be disturbed — the grasping "I" is seen through, and balance is what remains. Jain equanimity is a discipline imposed **by** a self, **on** a self, **for** the sake of that self: the *jīva* is real, permanent, individual, and it holds an even mind because reacting with anger would cause **karmic matter to adhere to it** (Ch. 03).

**Same posture, opposite reason.** One is the absence of a reactor; the other is a reactor exercising restraint. And the corpus's `structurally-parallel-to` edge between them is exactly right: **the structure is genuinely parallel, and the grounding is genuinely incompatible.**

**The one striking convergence, though, deserves equal weight:** *both traditions go out of their way to exclude indifference.* Upekkhā's near enemy is apathy; mādhyasthya is explicitly not callousness toward the suffering. Two incompatible metaphysics, arriving independently at the same worry — **that a discipline of non-reactivity will be counterfeited by simply not caring** — and both building a guard against it into the doctrine. When two systems with nothing metaphysical in common defend against the same failure, the failure is probably real.

### 4.4 And a third equanimity, from Ch. 19 {#samatva}

*→ concept file: [samatva.md](../../concepts/samatva.md) (primary in Ch. 19 §6.3.1)*

The Gītā adds a **third** occupant of the same slot: ***samatva***, even-mindedness toward success and failure — and BG 2.48 makes it *the definition of yoga itself*, *samatvaṁ yoga ucyate*. Its grounding is different again: **neither no-self insight nor karma-restraint, but detached action** — the inner steadiness that makes *desireless action within the world* possible. Kṛṣṇa explicitly commands continued action (*kuru karmāṇi*).

Set all four equanimities in a row and the structural slot is unmistakable while the content refuses to merge:

| equanimity | grounded in | aimed at |
|---|---|---|
| **upekkhā** (Buddhist) | *anattā* insight | liberation; a factor of awakening |
| **mādhyasthya** (Jain) | *kaṣāya*-restraint | stopping karmic influx to an eternal jīva |
| **samatva** (Gītā) | detachment from fruits | **acting fully** without attachment |
| ***upekṣā*** (Yoga-Sūtra 1.33) | the citta-purification programme | *kaivalya* — isolation of an eternal *puruṣa* |

Four traditions, one posture, four incompatible reasons for holding it. **The corpus's consistent verdict — `structurally-parallel-to`, never identity — is the finding, not a hedge.**

## 5. The negative side: ahiṃsā in three versions {#ahimsa}

*→ concept file: [ahimsa-vedic.md](../../concepts/ahimsa-vedic.md); Jain [ahimsa.md](../../concepts/ahimsa.md) and [ahimsa-buddhist.md](../../concepts/ahimsa-buddhist.md) are primary in Ch. 04*

The quartets are the **positive** ethics — what to cultivate. Their negative counterpart is **ahiṃsā**, non-injury, and the corpus splits it into **three tradition-suffixed nodes** precisely because the shared word conceals three different doctrines (charter §8's typed-split rule in action).

**Hindu ahiṃsā** is the node this chapter gives a home to, and its defining feature is that it is **not absolute**. It is *"one virtue among a network of dharmic obligations,"* and dharma permits exceptions:

| period | text | development |
|---|---|---|
| early Vedic (~1500 BCE) | Ṛgveda; Kapiṣṭhala Kaṭha Saṃhitā | the Ṛgveda records ritual animal sacrifice; the Kapiṣṭhala Kaṭha Saṃhitā holds the **earliest reference to *paśu-ahiṃsā*** in a moral sense |
| Upaniṣadic (~800–600 BCE) | Chāndogya Upaniṣad | lists ahiṃsā among **five essential virtues** with *satya*, *ārjava*, *dāna*, *tapas*; links it to escaping rebirth |
| epic (~400 BCE–400 CE) | Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parva | ***"ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ"*** — "non-violence is the highest moral virtue" — **yet warriors may kill in battle without sin** |
| classical Yoga (~400 CE) | Yoga-Sūtra II.30 | ahiṃsā is the **first of five *yamas***, foundational to the rest; applies to thought, word, and deed |
| medical (~600 CE) | Suśruta Saṃhitā | **recommends various meats** for specific ailments and for pregnant women — medical necessity overrides the general principle |

Note the shape of that table. **The maxim and its exception sit in the same text.** The Mahābhārata says non-violence is the highest dharma *and* that a kṣatriya who kills in a just war incurs no sin. This is not incoherence; it is what "one virtue among networked duties" means in practice — and the corpus records the realistic Hindu standard under the term ***alpadroha***, "minimum violence," rather than total abstention.

**The three-way comparison:**

| | **Hindu** | **Jain** | **Buddhist** |
|---|---|---|---|
| metaphysical ground | all life is sacred (ātman/brahman); souls interconnected | **every *jīva* has a soul; harm = karma bondage** | all sentient beings suffer; **intentional** harm generates negative karma |
| scope | graduated — exceptions for dharma-roles | **near-absolute** for monks; no dharmic exceptions | **intention-based** — unintentional harm less grave |
| exceptions | **warrior duty, punishment, medicine, ritual** | essentially none for ascetics | no formal exceptions |
| obligation type | one virtue among networked duties | the **first and supreme *mahāvrata*** | first precept; also grounded in mettā |

> ⚠️ **Hindu ahiṃsā ≠ Jain ahiṃsā, despite sharing the maxim.** Both traditions quote *ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ*. They mean different things by it. **The Jain position explicitly rejects every one of the Hindu exceptions** — Jain ascetics may not farm (soil-bodied *jīvas*), may not eat after dark, must sweep the ground before sitting. **No dharmic role overrides the soul-protection principle.** The corpus types this as an explicit non-equivalence, and this is the case where the shared maxim makes the conflation almost irresistible.

**And here the two halves of the chapter join up.** The positive quartets are the affective **ground** of the negative restraint, in both traditions, and both say so:

- **Jain:** *maitrī* is "the positive affective ground of the ahiṃsā vow — goodwill from which non-injury flows," and *kāruṇya* is "a primary motive behind the ahiṃsā vow."
- **Buddhist:** mettā is "the positive, cultivated ground from which Buddhist non-harm (*avihiṃsā*) flows — the active goodwill behind the restraint," with karuṇā as its co-formulation.

**Both traditions independently locate the same relation: a rule against harming is unstable unless it rests on a trained disposition to wish well.** That is a convergence at the level of *moral architecture* rather than of doctrine — and it is more interesting than the vocabulary overlap that started the chapter, because the vocabulary might be borrowed and this cannot easily be.

## 6. Compassion, and where the two traditions send it {#karuna-divergence}

One last divergence, because it is the one with the largest downstream consequences.

**Karuṇā is shared across all Buddhism — and Mahāyāna makes it the engine of everything.** ***Mahākaruṇā*** ("great compassion"), paired with the wisdom of *śūnyatā*, is what **generates *bodhicitta***, the vow to attain Buddhahood for the sake of all beings (Ch. 12 §5; Ch. 20 §3, where Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* makes the case at length). So:

- in **Theravāda**, karuṇā is **one of four** sublime abidings cultivated toward liberation;
- in **Mahāyāna**, it expands into **the defining orientation of the entire vehicle**.

One member of a four-set becomes the organising principle of a whole tradition. Nothing comparable happens to Jain *kāruṇya*, which stays a *bhāvanā* among *bhāvanā*s — because the Jain path has no analogue of the bodhisattva vow, and could not: a liberated Jain **cannot** act in the world at all (Ch. 11 §7.2, Ch. 18 §9). **The structural reason Mahāyāna could promote compassion to its centre is that its liberated beings remain able to help.** The Jain siddha cannot, so compassion in that system has to remain a discipline for the unliberated rather than a program for the liberated.

> ⚠️ **Jain *kāruṇya* ≠ Buddhist *karuṇā*** — the same word, different metaphysics. Jain compassion motivates non-injury grounded in **the ontology of harm to a *jīva***: harming any living being **binds karmic matter**. Buddhist karuṇā is **intention- and empathy-based within a no-self framework**. One is a fact about what happens to your soul; the other is a fact about your mind and another's suffering.

## 7. What the cluster shows {#synthesis}

**First: this is what a real parallel looks like when it is neither identity nor coincidence.** The two quartets share a word (*maitrī*), a root (*karuṇā*/*kāruṇya*), a count (four), a structural slot for each member, and a function (cultivated attitudes toward others, supporting a non-harm ethic). They differ in **object-restriction**, **order**, **the metaphysics grounding the fourth member**, and **what cultivating them is for**. Neither "these are the same doctrine" nor "these are unrelated" survives contact with the detail — which is exactly the outcome the charter's typed-link vocabulary exists to record, and why `structurally-parallel-to` paired with `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` is the right pair of edges rather than a fudge.

**Second: the near/far-enemy diagnostic deserves to be better known than it is.** Defining a virtue by its **counterfeit** rather than its opposite is a genuinely powerful method, and it produces the sharpest single line in this cluster: equanimity is *"rooted in insight"*; **indifference is "rooted in not bothering."** The method generalises well beyond Buddhism — every virtue in this corpus has a near enemy, and most traditions never name theirs.

**Third: two incompatible systems independently built a guard against the same failure.** Both traditions could have let "equanimity" slide into "not caring," and both explicitly refused. That convergence is not vocabulary and cannot be borrowed; it is two traditions noticing the same thing about how the discipline goes wrong.

**Fourth: the Jain verse tells you what it is doing, and reading it closely is worth more than the comparison.** TS 7.11 names its four recipient-classes *in the sūtra itself* — *sattva*, *guṇādhika*, *kliśyamāna*, *avineya*. Every structural difference in §4 falls out of that one grammatical fact. **The corpus fetched the verse directly, and the whole chapter's central finding comes from reading the compound rather than from any secondary source's comparison.** That is the method the charter asks for, doing what it is supposed to do.

## 8. Every flag in this chapter, in one place {#flags}

| flag | why the resemblance is tempting | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Buddhist quartet ≠ Jain quartet** | four cultivated attitudes; *maitrī* identical, *karuṇā*/*kāruṇya* cognate | **immeasurable radiation to all** vs **four named recipient-classes**; positions 2 and 3 **swapped** |
| **muditā ≠ pramoda** | both are trained gladness at another's good | muditā: **any** good fortune, opposing envy; pramoda: the ***guṇādhika*** specifically, opposing *māna* |
| **upekkhā ≠ mādhyasthya** | both are equanimity in the fourth slot | grounded in ***anattā*-insight** vs ***kaṣāya*-restraint on an eternal *jīva*** — no-self vs real-self |
| **upekkhā ≠ samatva ≠ upekṣā (Yoga)** | one posture across four traditions | liberation-insight vs karma-restraint vs **detached action** vs *kaivalya* |
| **kāruṇya ≠ karuṇā** | same word, same slot | **jīva-harm ontology** vs **intention within no-self**; and only karuṇā scales into *mahākaruṇā* |
| **equanimity ≠ indifference** *(both traditions agree)* | calm looks like calm | insight-rooted vs **"rooted in not bothering"** — the near enemy, explicitly excluded on both sides |
| **mettā ≠ affection** | warmth toward others, in both | affection is **preferential and possessive** — the near enemy that masquerades as the virtue |
| **karuṇā ≠ pity** | both respond to suffering | pity **sinks into** the distress and disables help; karuṇā stays clear and effective |
| **Hindu ahiṃsā ≠ Jain ahiṃsā** | **both quote *ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ*** | Hindu permits **warrior duty, punishment, medicine, ritual**; Jain rejects every exception |
| **Yoga-Sūtra 1.33 ∥ the brahmavihāras** *(not a flag — the same list)* | — | *maitrī, karuṇā, muditā, upekṣā*, in that order; contact is likely, **direction not established by these sources** |

## 9. Check yourself {#check}

1. Lay out the three quartets. Which two are word-for-word identical, and which two positions does the Jain set replace?
2. The Jain order puts joy **before** compassion; the Buddhist order reverses that. Explain why, using what each list is a partition *of*.
3. What does ***appamaññā*** mean, and why is that name a claim about the **object** of the four rather than their intensity?
4. Read TS 7.11's compound. Name the four recipient-classes it specifies, and say what follows from three of them being **restricted**.
5. Why is muditā "structurally essential" even though it is the least practised brahmavihāra? What does the practitioner default to without it?
6. Give the near enemy of each of the four brahmavihāras. What makes a near enemy more dangerous than a far one?
7. "Genuine upekkhā is rooted in insight; its counterfeit is rooted in not bothering." Unpack both halves — what insight, and why does the counterfeit pass so easily?
8. Distinguish muditā from pramoda on **object** and on **which passion each opposes**. Why does the corpus draw two edge-types between them rather than one?
9. Upekkhā and mādhyasthya are the same posture. State the opposite metaphysics of the self on which each rests.
10. Both traditions explicitly exclude indifference from equanimity. Why is that convergence more evidentially interesting than the shared vocabulary that opens the chapter?
11. Set the four equanimities (upekkhā, mādhyasthya, samatva, Yoga's upekṣā) against their four groundings. What is the corpus's verdict, and why is it a finding rather than a hedge?
12. Why is mādhyasthya **not** indifference to the suffering? Point to the feature of TS 7.11 that rules that reading out.
13. Reconstruct the Hindu ahiṃsā exceptions. How can the Mahābhārata hold both *"ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ"* and the warrior's exemption without incoherence?
14. Both traditions place a positive attitude *beneath* the negative rule of non-harm. State the relation in each, and say why this convergence is harder to explain by borrowing than the vocabulary overlap is.
15. Karuṇā becomes *mahākaruṇā* and reorganises an entire vehicle; kāruṇya stays one *bhāvanā* among four. Give the **structural** reason the Jain tradition could not have made the same move.
16. In one paragraph: "The brahmavihāras and the Jain bhāvanās are the same teaching in two traditions." What is right about that, and what does it flatten?
