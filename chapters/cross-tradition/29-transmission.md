## How to use this chapter

Every other chapter in this corpus asks *what did they argue?* This one asks a question that logically comes first and is almost always skipped: **how do we know what they argued?**

That is not a philosophical question. It is a question about manuscripts, quotations, translations, hagiographies and losses — about the physical and social channels through which an argument made in Kashmir in the tenth century, or Constantinople in the eleventh, arrives in front of a reader now. The channels are not transparent. Every one of them **selects**, and most of them select for reasons that have nothing to do with what the original author thought important.

The corpus has been accumulating this material for many batches without a chapter to hold it. Simplicius preserving the Presocratics; Vindhyavāsin surviving through the Buddhists who beat him in debate; Cārvāka known only through people ranking it last. Batch 42 added six more cases at once, and they turned out to sort into a small number of recognisable **kinds**. This chapter sets out the kinds.

## 1. Four channels {#channels}

A doctrine can reach the present by more than one route, and the routes have different failure modes. Four recur across this corpus.

### 1.1 The interested channel

Someone with their own purposes preserved the material. They are not neutral, they were never trying to be, and their interest determined **what got kept**.

| channel | what survives through it | the interest | where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buddhist opponents (Paramārtha's *Life of Vasubandhu*) | **Vindhyavāsin**'s Sāṃkhya | recording a debate their side eventually answered | Ch 25 |
| hostile doxography, esp. the *Sarva-darśana-saṅgraha* | **Cārvāka** | ranking it lowest in a hierarchy designed to end at Advaita | Ch 17, Ch 25 |
| **Simplicius**'s commentaries | ~two-thirds of the verbatim Presocratic fragments | philosophical commentary with its own agenda | Ch 24, Ch 14 |
| **Vācaspati Miśra**'s pan-darśana works | **Vārṣagaṇya**'s Sāṃkhya | doxographic completeness | §4, Ch 30 |
| **Psellos**'s exegesis | the **Chaldean Oracles**, and Proclus's lost treatise on them | a Christian revival of Platonism | §2 |

The failure mode is **silent selection**. Nothing is falsified; things are simply not mentioned, and there is no way to see the gap from inside the channel. The right question is never "is this source reliable?" but **"what would this source have had no reason to keep?"**

### 1.2 The genealogical channel

The material is attested by a lineage that needs it to exist. Guru-paramparās, *Śaṅkara-vijaya*s, colophons that name a grandfather.

The failure mode is **circularity**: the genealogy is both the claim and the evidence for it. Unanimity across many texts of one *genre* is not independence (charter §4, signal 1), and this is the case where that rule bites hardest. See §5.

### 1.3 The inheritance channel

Someone acquired a system without access to its sources — through translation, through a disciple, through a Christianising intermediary — and rebuilt it downstream.

The failure mode is not distortion but **invisible mediation**: the receiver honestly believes they have the thing, and the thing they have has already been shaped by whoever handed it over. Eriugena (§3) is the corpus's cleanest instance.

### 1.4 The material channel

Manuscripts. Palm leaf, birch bark, parchment; damp, insects, fire, disinterest. The failure mode is **loss without pattern** — which is in one way the most honest channel, because it is not selecting *for* anything, and in another the worst, because the losses are unrecoverable and you cannot even estimate what they were. §6 is the case where this can be watched happening, because the recovery is still under way.

## 2. Psellos, and what two filters do {#psellos}

### 2.1 The situation

The **Chaldean Oracles** are ~227 fragmentary hexameter verses, composed in the 2nd century CE, which acquired quasi-scriptural authority among later Neoplatonists — "the Bible of the Neoplatonists." Ch 24 already records the difficulty: **no fragment survives independently of a later Neoplatonist quoting it.**

Ch 24 names three quoters and adds a parenthesis about the third: **Proclus, Damascius, and (via a different transmission line) Michael Psellos.** This chapter is what the parenthesis means.

**Michael Psellos** (c. 1018 – c. 1096) was a Byzantine courtier, adviser to a run of emperors, "the leading professor at the University of Constantinople," and holder of the title *hypatos tōn philosophōn*, **Chief of the Philosophers**. He led a revival of classical study whose influence reaches the Italian Renaissance. And:

> "The Chaldean Oracles were lost, but many verses have been gathered from the writings of Michael Psellus, whose commentary survives."
> "**The most extensive surviving commentary** was written by the Christian philosopher Michael Psellus in the eleventh century."

His commentary is also the main route back to **Proclus's own lost treatise** on Chaldean philosophy. So he is not one link but two.

### 2.2 Why two filters is different from one filter twice

Here is the point that makes this case worth a section rather than a footnote.

- Proclus and Damascius quote the Oracles as **pagan scripture they believe**, inside a living theurgic tradition, in the 5th–6th centuries.
- Psellos quotes them as a **Christian describing a pagan system**, six centuries later, with no living practice behind him and under suspicion for his interest.

These two filters distort in **different directions**, and that has a consequence in each direction:

**The gain.** An item present in both lines is unlikely to be an artefact of either interest. Two biased instruments that are biased *differently* triangulate better than one unbiased instrument you cannot check.

**The loss.** A doctrine that Proclus found essential *and* Psellos found unremarkable — or dangerous — leaves no trace at all. The overlap is the reliable part; it is not the whole part, and the difference between them is invisible.

The corpus records both halves. That is the entire methodological content of "provenance," stated once and applied everywhere.

### 2.3 It does not stop at antiquity

The modern editions inherit the problem. The chain is **Kroll's *De oraculis Chaldaicis* (1894)** → **Hans Lewy (1956)** → **Édouard des Places's Greek–French edition (1971)**, and des Places's edition, on one reviewer's assessment, "has not published **all** Chaldean fragments quoted by Neo-Platonists or later interpreters like the Byzantine intellectual Michael Psellus."

So the standard critical text is **not a complete record of what the Byzantine channel preserves.** Anyone working from des Places alone has a filter they did not know about, applied in 1971.

This generalises, and it is why the charter's §4 signal 5 (provenance) is not a note about ancient manuscripts. **Every layer between you and the text is a layer**, including the one with an ISBN.

### 2.4 The man himself is contested — and the charge is contemporary

Psellos's own position is disputed, and it bears on how to weight him.

| reading | evidence |
|---|---|
| sincere Christian Platonist | he read "Homeric literature and Platonic philosophy as **precursors to and integral components of Christian doctrine**" — a standard Byzantine harmonisation. On this reading he preserves the pagan material *because he is explaining it* |
| pagan sympathiser under cover | in **1054** he "was accused by his erstwhile friend, the future Patriarch **John Xiphilinos**, of **forsaking Christ to follow Plato**"; some readers find irony in his work "transmitting highly critical and subversive messages… even about Byzantine Christian beliefs" |

The corpus does not adjudicate. It records that the charge is **contemporary, and from a friend who became Patriarch** — not a modern suspicion projected backwards. And it records where the institutional line actually fell: his pupil **John Italos** succeeded him as Chief of the Philosophers, was formally accused of "Hellenizing" — teaching metempsychosis and the eternity of the world — and "confessed and recanted" **twice**.

**The Byzantine church would tolerate the study of Plato and not the adoption of him.** The line ran somewhere between master and pupil, and Psellos stayed on the right side of it — which is itself information about what he would and would not have written down.

## 3. Eriugena, or a system without its sources {#eriugena}

### 3.1 The fact

**Johannes Scottus Eriugena** (c. 800 – c. 877) built the one complete Neoplatonic system in the Latin West between Augustine and the twelfth century. And, per the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*:

> **Eriugena "did not have direct knowledge" of pagan Neoplatonists like Plotinus or Proclus. His Neoplatonism derives entirely from Christian Eastern sources** — Pseudo-Dionysius and Maximus Confessor.

He is a Neoplatonist by **inheritance rather than by reading**. And the mechanism is his own hand: around **860** he produced a complete Latin *Corpus Dionysii*, plus Maximus's *Ambigua ad Ioannem* and *Quaestiones ad Thalassium*, Gregory of Nyssa's *De hominis opificio*, and possibly Epiphanius's *Anchoratus*. He translated his way into a tradition he could not read at first hand.

### 3.2 What he built

The *Periphyseon* (c. 862–867) takes ***natura*** as "the general term for all things that are and all things that are not" — a category wider than being — and divides it by two predicates, *creating* and *created*:

| # | species | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | creates, is not created | God as source |
| 2 | creates, is created | the Primary Causes |
| 3 | is created, does not create | temporal effects — the world |
| 4 | neither creates nor is created | God as end; non-being |

**Species 1 and 4 are the same God**, as origin and as terminus. So this is not a hierarchy of four things but a **circuit** — procession out, return back — which is the Plotinian shape exactly, reached without Plotinus.

And the apophatic method comes with it: "denials concerning God… are '**more true**'" than affirmations, and God is ***nihil per excellentiam***, "**nothingness through excellence**," with creation proceeding from his "superabundant nothingness."

### 3.3 The comparison the corpus will not make

*Nihil per excellentiam* looks like an invitation to compare with **śūnyatā**. The corpus draws the edge and labels it a **conflation to avoid**:

- Eriugena's "nothing" is a **superlative predicated of a plenum**: God is called nothing because *no* category of being reaches something that exceeds them all, and the world genuinely proceeds from him.
- Madhyamaka's emptiness is the **absence of *svabhāva*** in everything, including itself, asserted precisely to block any such source.

**The same negation, doing opposite structural jobs — one to protect a plenum, one to refuse one.** This is Ch 28's Move 1 (surface-form match) waiting to happen, and it is worth naming here rather than there because the Eriugena case adds a wrinkle Ch 28 does not have: the *reason* the comparison is tempting is that Eriugena, like a Madhyamika, is using negation systematically. Method-similarity is even more seductive than content-similarity, and even less probative.

### 3.4 What the corpus will compare, with the disanalogy attached

| | Eriugena | Advaita |
|---|---|---|
| ultimate | God, *nihil per excellentiam*, beyond being | Brahman, *nirguṇa*, beyond predication |
| method | apophatic; denials "more true" | *neti neti* |
| **world** | **theophany — genuinely created, and real** | ***vivarta* — apparent; its reality is the question** |
| goal | *reditus*, the return of all things | *mokṣa*, recognition of an identity never lost |
| status at home | **condemned twice** (1210; Honorius III, 1225) | the orthodox reading of the *Brahma Sūtra* |

**Row three is load-bearing.** A parallel drawn without it turns two different systems into one. And row five is a fact about institutions rather than arguments: **the same structural move is heresy in one tradition and orthodoxy in the other.**

### 3.5 The pantheism charge, dated

The *Periphyseon* was condemned in **1210** and by **Honorius III in 1225**, alongside David of Dinant and Amaury of Bène — Amaury having been condemned for saying God is the formal principle of all things, which "recalled Eriugena's statement that God is the '***forma omnium***.'" It stayed on the *Index* until the **1960s**.

Two observations about the shape of that evidence:

1. The condemnation is **350 years posthumous** and arrives bundled with two contemporaries *of the condemners*. It is evidence about 13th-century Paris at least as much as about a 9th-century text.
2. Eriugena "explicitly rejects identifying God as the '**genus**' of which creatures are '**parts**'" — precisely the move a pantheist would need. Whether that disclaimer does real work in his metaphysics is a question about the *Periphyseon*, **which was not read for this corpus**, and the node says so.

### 3.6 Psellos and Eriugena as a pair

Put the two together and almost the whole problem of how late-antique philosophy crossed into the Middle Ages is visible in two men:

- **Psellos read the pagan texts and preserved them without adopting them.**
- **Eriugena adopted the structure without access to the texts.**

Neither is the normal picture of "transmission." Neither is a copyist.

## 4. Vārṣagaṇya, or a philosopher made of quotations {#varsaganya}

### 4.1 What the claim actually is

**Vārṣagaṇya** is named in the standard Sāṃkhya teacher-lists with Kapila, Āsuri, Pañcaśikha, Vindhyavāsin, Jaigīṣavya and Īśvarakṛṣṇa. Everything else about him is one sentence:

> **Frauwallner suggested, based primarily on citations of Vārṣagaṇya's views in the works of Vācaspati Miśra, that Vārṣagaṇya was the author of a *revised version* of the *Ṣaṣṭitantra*, older versions of which had been attributed to Kapila or Pañcaśikha.**

Unpack what that costs:

1. **The evidence is quotation in a source seven-or-more centuries later.** Vācaspati Miśra (9th–10th c.) is a compulsive citer of positions he does not hold — which is exactly what makes him usable, and exactly what makes this a reconstruction.
2. **The claim is about a *revision*.** So the reconstruction must separate his layer from the older layers it displaced, in a text nobody has.
3. **The *Ṣaṣṭitantra* is contested in kind.** It may be "either itself a text or groups of texts, or **simply an enumerated list of a system for oral teaching**." If the third, "authorship" is not quite the right question.

### 4.2 Two literatures that do not meet

| reading | source |
|---|---|
| **Pañcaśikha** wrote it — "the revered Vedic sage," also called *Kāpileya*, whence *Kāpilīya-śāstra*; lost "before Ādi Śaṅkarācārya" | Wikipedia's *Shashti Tantra* article, which **does not mention Vārṣagaṇya at all** |
| **Vārṣagaṇya** revised an older Kapila/Pañcaśikha version | Frauwallner, via the *Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies* vol. 4 |

This is not two scholars arguing. It is a **traditional-attribution account and a philological-reconstruction account**, each internally coherent, neither engaging the other. Recognising that shape matters: a reader who meets only one will not know there is a question.

The dates split the same way — **early 4th century CE** (Frauwallner) against **1st–2nd century CE** — and the two answer different questions: Frauwallner's is derived from where the *epistemology* must sit relative to its Buddhist interlocutors, the earlier one from where the *text* must sit relative to its predecessors.

### 4.3 What he is said to have held, and why it matters

Three positions, reported consistently:

- **The guṇas in their ultimate nature cannot be perceived and are known only by inference from their manifestations.** That is an epistemological move: it puts the whole system's foundation on *anumāna*.
- **"There is neither production of something new nor extinction of something existent. What exists is always existent; what does not exist will never be existent."** *Satkāryavāda* stated as a conservation principle.
- Considerable attention to **the evolution of the *tattva*s from *pradhāna***.

If the reconstruction holds, **Īśvarakṛṣṇa's seventy verses are a "schoolbook" abridgement of a fuller system**, and the *Sāṃkhyakārikā*'s famous compression stops looking like originality and starts looking like summary. That is a large consequence resting on a thin base, and the node is `confidence: low` for exactly that reason.

### 4.4 The instrument becomes visible

There is a neat reflexive point here. The corpus wrote [Vācaspati Miśra](../../concepts/vacaspati-mishra.md) earlier in the same batch, from an entirely unrelated source-set, and independently established that he was a **prolific quoter of positions across five darśanas** he did not all hold. That is what makes Frauwallner's citation-basis plausible.

So the corpus has, in effect, characterised the instrument and the reading it produces separately. That is the good case. The general lesson: **when a figure is reconstructed from quotations, the quoter is part of the evidence, and should be assessed on their own.**

## 5. What a name in a genealogy is worth {#genealogy}

### 5.1 Govinda Bhagavatpāda: the load-bearing joint

The Advaita lineage runs **Gauḍapāda → Govinda Bhagavatpāda → Śaṅkara**. The middle link is required — it is what makes Gauḍapāda Śaṅkara's *paramaguru* — and it is the least attested figure in the chain:

> "**Little is known of his life and works, except that he is mentioned in all the traditional accounts (Śaṅkara-vijayams) as the teacher of Ādi Śaṅkara.**"

Four things are attributed to him. Each fails to settle much, and they fail in **four different ways**, which is why the case is worth teaching:

| attribution | why it does not settle much |
|---|---|
| guru of Śaṅkara, disciple of Gauḍapāda | attested by the *Śaṅkara-vijaya*s — **one genre**. Unanimity across texts of a genre is not independence, and Ch 25 established the genre's late and constructed character |
| author of the ***Rasahṛdaya-tantra***, an alchemical work of the **Raseśvara** school | the identification is **disputed by name in the scholarly literature**, and the dates fight: Cowell & Gough place the Raseśvara school "**about the commencement of the Christian era**" against a 9th-c. author |
| identical with **Patañjali** | generated by a shared motif — **both are called incarnations of Śeṣa** — not by evidence. The motif read as a fact |
| named in the **first verse of the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi*** | ⚠️ the corpus has already established (Ch 25 §3.1) that the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi*'s attribution to Śaṅkara is **rejected**. A pseudepigraphic text naming him tells you the name was current in the tradition that produced the text, and nothing about the 8th century |

### 5.2 The Narmada episode, read for what it does

The *Mādhavīya Śaṅkaravijaya* has them meet at Omkareshwar. Govinda asks the young Śaṅkara who he is; **Śaṅkara answers with an extempore verse stating Advaita, and is then initiated.** In the best-known version Śaṅkara stops a Narmada flood with his *kamaṇḍalu*, saving the guru who lies in samādhi in a cave.

Notice the order. **The disciple arrives already possessing the doctrine.** The guru's role is to *recognise*, not to transmit. A hagiography that needed to prove doctrinal **descent** would have written the scene the other way round.

So the episode does double duty: it **authenticates Śaṅkara's originality** while formally preserving the lineage. That is exactly the pair of things the Advaita tradition needed done — which is a reason to read the whole scene as **constructed to a purpose** rather than remembered.

This is the general method for genealogical evidence: **do not ask whether the story is true; ask what the story is for.** If the answer is "it does precisely the job the tradition needed doing," the story's existence is evidence about the need, not about the event.

### 5.3 Vāgbhaṭa: two claims usually run together

**Vāgbhaṭa** is the third member of the *bṛhat-trayī* with Caraka and Suśruta. Two large works bear his name:

| | *Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha* | *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdayasaṃhitā* |
|---|---|---|
| form | prose, "longer and less concise" | verse, **7120 ślokas** |
| scale | **150 chapters** | **120 chapters** |
| afterlife | less transmitted | translated into **Tibetan, Arabic, Persian** and modern languages |

**The AH is the one that travelled** — and its travel is the historically significant fact. A Tibetan and an Arabic *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* means Indian medicine entering the Tibetan *gso ba rig pa* and the Islamicate medical literature through *this* text rather than through Caraka or Suśruta. **Concision travels.** (It is also the text this corpus leans on: the tissue-fire relation cited in Ch 31 is A. Hṛ. Sū. 11/24.)

Now the authorship question, in the two forms that are usually conflated:

| claim | what it says |
|---|---|
| **traditional** | "some old commentators have referred to two different Vāgbhaṭas, one the **Elder** and the other the **Junior**… scholars have suggested existence of a duo of the grandfather and his grandson" |
| **philological** | "modern philological research… argues that these two texts are **unlikely to be the work of a single author**," and the relationship "has not been conclusively resolved" |

**The philological claim is negative; the traditional claim is positive and specific.** Accepting the first does not give you the second. And the AS colophon shows the ambiguity is already inside the primary source:

> "There was a great physician by name Vāgbhaṭa, who was my grandfather, **I bear his name**; from him was born Siṃhagupta and I am from him; I was born in the **Sindhu country**."

That is *already* a grandson explaining a homonym. The confusion is not a later reader's mistake; it is a feature of the evidence.

### 5.4 The pattern, stated once

All three great authorities of classical Indian medicine — **Caraka, Suśruta, Vāgbhaṭa** — are `status: contested` **as persons**, while the texts under their names are perfectly definite. The same holds for **Vyāsa**, **Vālmīki**, **Kapila** and **Govinda Bhagavatpāda**.

> **In this literature, a name is a title on a compendium before it is a biography.**

That is not scepticism and not debunking. It is a statement about what kind of object an "author" is in a tradition where texts are compiled, redacted and re-redacted over centuries, and attribution marks a *lineage of authority* rather than a claim about who held the pen.

## 6. Utpaladeva, and loss you can watch {#utpaladeva}

### 6.1 The author behind a doctrine the corpus already had

The corpus has had **pratyabhijñā** — the Kashmiri Śaiva doctrine of Recognition — since Ch 18. It did not have the man who named it.

**Utpaladeva** turned his teacher Somānanda's visionary monism into an argued system, and the datum that makes him more than a link in a chain is this: **the term *pratyabhijñā* does not occur in Somānanda's *Śivadṛṣṭi* at all.** His *Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā* is "regarded as the definitive formulation of the school's philosophy," and he "deviates in style and substance" from his teacher.

That reverses the usual Indian pattern. Normally the founder states and the commentator elaborates. **Here the second man supplies the thesis.**

(The dating is contested by a consistent ~25-year shift across all three generations — Wikipedia against the academic literature — which is two schemes rather than a dispute about a person, and the node records it as such.)

### 6.2 The *Vivṛti*

Utpaladeva wrote two commentaries on his own verses: a short *Vṛtti*, which survives, and a long ***Vivṛti***, which is **almost entirely lost**. What is known about the loss is unusually specific, and reads as a case study in how a philosophical text degrades:

- only "a small part of it has been preserved in a **single, very incomplete manuscript**," edited and translated by **Raffaele Torella**;
- the largest fragment recovered since was found "**in the margins of a manuscript containing Abhinavagupta's *Vivṛtivimarśinī***" — the root text surviving inside its own commentary's commentary;
- and the decay is **datable**: "it is certain that the *Vivṛti* was already fragmentary when **Śaṅkarakaṇṭha** made marginal annotations… in the **seventeenth century**."

### 6.3 Two consequences

**First: dominance can be an artefact of survival.** Abhinavagupta's works "eventually overshadowed" Utpaladeva's own — and the overshadowing is literal. What we read of Utpaladeva's self-interpretation, we read *through* Abhinavagupta's manuscripts. Before concluding that the grand-disciple was the greater philosopher, one has to subtract the fact that his books are the ones that survived.

**Second: this is the material channel (§1.4) caught in the act.** Unlike Vindhyavāsin or Cārvāka, there is no hostile filter here — the channel is a **devoted follower** — and the material was lost anyway, to damp and time and disinterest. Recovery is ongoing and published. This is the mildest and best-documented instance of the problem in the whole corpus, and it is still severe.

### 6.4 The manoeuvre worth learning

One substantive point, because it is easy to garble and the node states it exactly. Utpaladeva's Buddhist opponent is also his toolkit — Dharmakīrti's philosophy "pervasively influenced" him, and the engagement "resulted in… absorption of their doctrines and terminology," including *apoha* and *anupalabdhi* outright. Then:

> The Pratyabhijñā philosophers **used Dharmakīrti's own arguments for Vijñānavāda against the Sautrāntikas' inference** — and **then used that same discussion to argue that their idealism is superior to the Vijñānavādins' own.**

The Buddhist idealist is first enlisted against the Buddhist realist, then defeated by the position he was enlisted to defend. What the Śaiva has that the Yogācārin lacks is a **persisting, self-aware, free subject** — consciousness that is *ajaḍa* (non-inert) and possessed of ***svātantrya***. Momentary consciousness-events cannot **recognise**, because recognition just *is* the identification of a present cognition with a past one by one subject.

## 7. The control case {#control}

### 7.1 Prajñākaragupta

**Prajñākaragupta** (mid-8th to 9th c.) wrote the ***Pramāṇavārttikālaṅkāra***, a commentary on Dharmakīrti's *Pramāṇavārttika* running to **over 16,200 Sanskrit ślokas**, and — "unlike other commentaries" — one that "involves the author's **independent arguments**."

Here is what exists for him:

- a **full Sanskrit edition**: Rāhula Sāṅkṛtyāyana, 1953, Tibetan Sanskrit Works Series 1, Patna;
- a **manuscript facsimile**: Shigeaki Watanabe, 1998;
- a **Tibetan translation**;
- **two Sanskrit commentaries on the commentary**: Jayanta's *ṭīkā*, and Yamāri's *Supariśuddhā*.

Original text, manuscripts, a second-language witness, and its own commentarial layer. Set that beside Vārṣagaṇya (no text at all) and the contrast is the chapter's whole point in one comparison.

### 7.2 Why the node is still only `medium`

Because **the primary text was not read**, in any of the three forms in which it survives. And there is a visible content consequence: the corpus can say what he **rejected** — Dharmottara's *niścaya-pratyaya*, the ascertaining cognition inserted between raw perception and action — and **cannot say what he proposed instead**, because no source consulted states it. The node names the hole and names the item that would fill it (Miyo on *vyavasthā*).

**That is the distinction the chapter is built to make visible.** For Vārṣagaṇya, `low` reflects *the evidence that exists*. For Prajñākaragupta, `medium` reflects *the reading that was done*. Both are honest; they are not the same kind of limitation, and a confidence level that did not distinguish them would be less informative than no confidence level at all.

### 7.3 And one thing the rejection does establish

Even without his positive account, the exchange establishes something structural: **the Buddhist epistemological tradition was still repairing its own foundations two generations after Dharmakīrti.** Ch 20 collects the pattern — Nāgārjuna against Abhidharma, Vasubandhu against his own root text, Prāsaṅgika against Svātantrika, Śāntarakṣita adjudicating Yogācāra against Madhyamaka. Prajñākaragupta against Dharmottara is the epistemological instance. **The sharpest arguments are between Buddhists.**

## 8. The reader's error: dating a hymn that carries no date {#atri}

Every channel above is a **producer**'s filter. The last one is the **reader**'s.

### 8.1 The hymn

**Atri** is one of the Saptarṣi and the seer-family behind **Maṇḍala 5 of the Ṛgveda**, 87 hymns to Agni and Indra. **RV 5.40** is nine verses: four in praise of Indra, then

> "the fifth verse informs us that a demon named **Svarbhānu** pierced the sun and plunged a bewildered world into darkness. The next four verses are in praise of Atri, who is said to have discovered the sun concealed in gloom and, **with the power of his prayers**, restored it back in the sky."

It is widely repeated that this is the earliest reference to a total solar eclipse in any historical astronomy, and that the eclipse is **datable**.

### 8.2 What the corpus asserts, and what it declines

**Asserted.** RV 5.40.5–9 describes the sun obscured and restored, attributes the obscuring to Svarbhānu, and is old. Svarbhānu is the figure who later splits into **Rāhu and Ketu** (Ch 21). The hymn heads a genuine long line of eclipse-thinking.

**Declined.** That it is a datable observation; that it is demonstrably the world's earliest such record; that it can date the Ṛgveda.

### 8.3 The evidence for declining

| proposed date | proposer | basis |
|---|---|---|
| **22 October 4202 BCE** | Vahia (TIFR) & Soma (NAOJ), on **Tilak**'s interpretation | not the hymn — references in the ***Śāṅkhāyana Brāhmaṇa*** to where the sun rises at the spring equinox, Orion or the Pleiades |
| **19 October 3811 BCE** | **the same two authors** | the same method |
| **25 July 3928 BCE** | a separate treatment | a different reconstruction |

**Three dates, 391 years apart, from one hymn — two of them from the same pair of authors.** A method that does not select a unique solution in its own proponents' hands is not selecting anything.

Add: the hymn contains **no date, no year, no location**; the numbers come from a *different text's* equinox description filtered through a **19th-century** reading; and Wikipedia's own article on the episode is flagged by its maintainers for "unreliable citations" while conceding that the Ṛgveda's language is "very symbolic" and that astronomers have "**interpreted**" the disappearance as an eclipse.

### 8.4 The shape of the error

A hymn in which a demon swallows the sun and a sage restores it **by the power of his prayers** is, on its face, a ritual and mythological text. **Reading it as an observation log is a decision, not a finding.**

This is a variant of the failure Ch 21 §4 already documents at Rāhu. There the error is reading the swallowing-demon myth *as* an eclipse theory, when the relation between the myth and India's real computational astronomy is "**naming and correlation, not identity**." Here it runs one step further: the myth is read as an observation, and the observation is used to **date the text**.

Note how the corpus's negative claim is phrased. It says these datings are **not established by anything it has seen** — not that they are false. That asymmetry is deliberate and is the §0 discipline working in the modest direction: **declining to assert is not the same as asserting the negation**, and a corpus that overshot here would be committing the mirror-image of the error it is refusing.

## 9. Six questions {#questions}

Generalised from §§2–8. Ask them of any claim that has travelled.

1. **Who kept this, and why?** Not "is the source reliable" but *what interest selected it*. (§1.1)
2. **What would this channel have had no reason to keep?** The gap is invisible from inside; you have to reason about it from outside. (§2.2)
3. **Is the attestation independent, or is it one genre repeated?** Many *Śaṅkara-vijaya*s are not many sources. (§5.1)
4. **What job does this story do for the people who told it?** If it does exactly the job they needed doing, its existence is evidence about the need. (§5.2)
5. **Is the negative claim being read as a positive one?** "Unlikely to be one author" is not "grandfather and grandson." (§5.3)
6. **Does the method select a unique answer?** If it yields three, it is producing outputs, not findings. (§8.3)

And one that sits under all six: **is the modern edition a layer too?** (§2.3)
