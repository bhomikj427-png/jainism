# Chapter 45 — The Witness Problem: When the Commentary Outlives the Manuscript

> **Primary concepts:** `jinendrabuddhi` · `nyāsa (kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā)` · `haradatta` · `puṇyarāja`
> **Folder:** `cross-tradition/` — continuing [Ch 29](29-transmission.md) (*How Things Reach Us*), [Ch 34](34-the-commentator.md) and [Ch 39](39-filling-in-the-blanks.md) (*How the Record Gets Made*). The four figures here are a **Buddhist**, a **Śaiva**, a **Kashmiri** and a text with **five homonyms across five grammatical systems**; the chapter's subject is transmission, not grammar. The **grammars** from the same batch are in [Ch 44](../hindu/shastra/44-the-grammars-that-lost.md), in `hindu/shastra/`.
> **Reads after:** [Ch 44](../hindu/shastra/44-the-grammars-that-lost.md), whose §11 hands this chapter its question — and [Ch 42](../hindu/shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md), one of whose central claims this chapter half-repays.

---

## How to use this chapter

Chapter 44 kept failing at the same point. It could describe six grammars and could not establish what any of them *originally contained*. The Cāndra's chapters 7 and 8. Whether the *Prākṛtaprakāśa*'s twelve chapters are its author's. Whether a lexicographer's "six chapters" described a book or a bookshelf.

This chapter is about that failure, and it opens with the sentence that explains it. It comes not from a historian but from three working editors building a digital critical edition of the Kāśikāvṛtti:

> ### **"Manuscripts in India last no more than about five hundred years."**

Everything else follows. Four things to carry out:

1. **For most Sanskrit texts, the commentary is a better witness than any surviving manuscript** — not a distortion layer over the text, but the **oldest evidence of it there is.** (§1)
2. **And a commentary is a partial witness with no valid argument from silence** — a technical asymmetry this corpus had been circling loosely for three batches and can now state exactly. (§2)
3. **Pāṇinian grammar had regions** — eastern, southern, Benares — which the corpus did not know, and which decided **which commentary a school read**. (§4)
4. **Names in this literature are unreliable in both directions**, and this chapter has instances of each: one man carrying two careers the corpus had filed separately, and one man carrying two names on his own authority. (§3, §6, §7)

⚠ **This chapter half-repays a debt to Chapter 42 and half-corrects it.** Batch 48 attached a caveat to its own work — *we have never seen these verses apart from the commentary* — and treated it as a **risk**. Working editors of exactly such a text say the same fact and call it **invaluable**. §1.2 sets out why both are right and what the corpus does with that.

## §1 The witness problem

### §1.1 The four sentences

Ajotikar, Ajotikar and Scharf, "Enriching the digital edition of the Kāśikāvṛtti by adding variants from the Nyāsa and Padamañjarī":

> "**Manuscripts in India last no more than about five hundred years. The oldest readable manuscript of the KV. dates only to the early fifteenth century.** Yet several centuries earlier, the KV. was commented upon in the *Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā* by **Jinendrabuddhi in the eighth or ninth century** and then in the *Padamañjarī* by **Haradatta in the thirteenth century**. These commentators provide information about the constitution of the text of KV. in several ways: **by direct citation and incipits, as well as less directly by discussion on the text**. The information provided by commentators **hundreds of years prior to the oldest manuscript is invaluable to reliably establishing the text** of the KV."

Read the arithmetic. The Kāśikāvṛtti was written in the **7th century**. The oldest legible copy of it is from the **early 15th**. Its first commentator wrote in the **8th or 9th** — **six centuries before any surviving manuscript of the text he was commenting on.** Its second wrote in the **13th**, two centuries before.

⭐ **The commentaries are not a later layer on the text. They are the older evidence.**

### §1.2 ⚠ Repaying Chapter 42, and correcting its emphasis

Chapter 42, and the concept files behind it, flagged something as a hazard: the corpus's claims about Bhartṛhari's *Vākyapadīya* III reach it **through Helārāja's gloss**, and it "has never seen those verses apart from the commentary." That was recorded as a caveat — a reason for caution.

Scharf and the Ajotikars state the identical situation and call it **invaluable**.

⭐⭐ **Both are right, and holding both at once is the whole discipline in miniature.** The sub-commentary is simultaneously the **only long-range witness** to its base text and an **interested** one, and there is no vantage outside that pair from which to check.

⚠ So the Batch 48 caveat is **not withdrawn and not weakened. It is relocated.** The risk is not that commentarial mediation might be avoidable and the corpus failed to avoid it. **The risk is that it is universal** — and therefore the honest discipline is not to seek an unmediated text but to **say which commentary you are standing on, every time.** That is now a standing rule, and it came from a source that had no interest in the corpus's methodology at all.

### §1.3 And the Kāśikā's own state

The same paper reports something Chapter 44 would have wanted to know:

- The standard critical edition (**Sharma, Deshpande & Padhye, 1969–70**) rests on "**only nine manuscripts** as well as four previous editions."
- The *New Catalogus Catalogorum* notices "**more than two hundred**."

⭐ **The only extant full-length commentary on Pāṇini's sūtras has been critically edited from under 5% of its known witnesses** — and an effort "begun nearly thirty years ago" has so far produced an edition of one section (**Haag and Vergiani, 2009**, on the pratyāhāras), with a successor now funded under **Malhar Kulkarni at IIT Bombay** aiming at the first pāda of the first adhyāya.

## §2 ⭐ The argument from silence, stated exactly

This is the section that pays for the chapter. The same editors set out, technically, an asymmetry this corpus had been gesturing at loosely since Batch 47:

> "When a critical edition is prepared, the assumption is that each manuscript covers the entire span of text edited… Hence only variants are reported in the critical apparatus and **it is assumed that silence regarding a witness reports its support for the adopted text**… In contrast, commentaries on scientific texts, and on grammatical texts in particular, **generally do not mention or comment upon every word of their base text**… **Subcommentaries as a rule specifically mention only a small proportion of the words in the base text. Since the full text is not always cited, one cannot assume that silence regarding a reading in the base text indicates support.**"

⭐ **A manuscript's silence is evidence. A commentary's silence is nothing.**

⚠ **And there is a direct consequence for this corpus's own habits.** Wherever a concept file says *the commentator does not dispute X*, or *no commentator objects*, that is worth **zero**. The corpus has not systematically audited itself for that move, and this chapter records the exposure rather than claiming to have cleared it.

### §2.1 What the editors do instead — and why the corpus recognises the instrument

Their remedy is to record **positive readings as well as variants**, so that a commentary's coverage can be *measured* rather than assumed. That forces a unit problem, which they state: neither a word nor a reading works, so they will count **characters** in a phonetic encoding, noting that "traditional accounting of the extent of text in India used the orthographic syllable (*akṣara*)."

⭐ **That is the same instrument that produces this corpus's two best statistics.** Ch 42 §4 turns on the Mahābhāṣya covering **1,228 of 3,981 sūtras** — 31% — against the Kāśikā's **all 3,981**. Those are coverage figures. **The corpus was already doing, crudely, what these editors are now doing properly**, and it did not know that was what it was doing.

## §3 Jinendrabuddhi, and a collision inside this corpus

The Kāśikā's first great commentator wrote the ***Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā*** — universally called the ***Nyāsa***. Abhyankar: "written in the **eighth century** by the **Buddhist grammarian** Jinendrabuddhi, who belonged to the **eastern school of Pāṇini's Grammar**." The specialist database EAST dates him **c. 710–770 CE**.

Now the other half. The corpus's `pramana-samuccaya.md` — written batches ago, in a completely different literature — records that Dignāga's foundational text of Buddhist logic is **largely lost in Sanskrit**, and "is reconstructed from two Tibetan translations and from **Jinendrabuddhi's commentary (Ṭīkā)**, an ongoing scholarly project."

**The corpus held both men, in separate files, and had never noticed they share a name.**

### §3.1 The scholarship, which had noticed

| position | scholar |
|---|---|
| **two different men** | **Kobayashi (1977)** |
| **one man** | **Hayes (1983)** |
| **one man** — with the observation that Jinendrabuddhi "seems to use the *Tattvasaṅgraha*" | **Funayama (1999)**, p. 92 |
| **one man**, on "identical author-name in the colophons of the two works" | **Steinkellner (2005)**, pp. xl–xlii |

And the state of the question: "there are several **remarkable similarities in style and tone**" between the grammarian's and the Buddhist commentator's works, and "there are **no pieces of evidence that weigh strongly against** the hypothesis."

⭐⭐ **On the majority reading, the principal Sanskrit witness to the foundational text of Buddhist logic is a commentary by the man who wrote the standard commentary on the standard commentary on Pāṇini.** Note that this is **not** a doctrinal bridge — nobody claims the *Nyāsa* argues epistemology. It is a **bibliographical** one, and it is stronger for that: a fact about who was in the room, not an analogy between ideas.

### §3.2 ⚠ Why this looked, at first, like a counterexample to the corpus's own rule

Chapters 42 and 44 spent themselves teaching distrust of exactly this inference. Vararuci: four men, one name. Patañjali: three. Candra: three identifications. The corpus's reflex is: *same name, therefore same man* is how false history gets made.

And **Steinkellner's ground, as summarised, is "identical author-name in the colophons."** Applied naively, the corpus's own suspicion would put it on **Kobayashi's** side, against three later specialists.

⭐ **The resolution is that the rule needed sharpening, not abandoning.** The corpus is reading a one-line précis of an argument that runs three pages, and must not reduce a specialist's case to its summary. And the load here is not carried by the name at all: it is carried by **Hayes's and Funayama's stylistic and doctrinal evidence** — similarities of style and tone, and an apparent use of the *Tattvasaṅgraha* that also constrains the date.

> **The name is the occasion for the question. The style and the citations are the evidence.**

⚠ That is exactly what the Vararuci case never had. There, Abhyankar's refutation was that **Patañjali "does not associate Vararuci with Kātyāyana at all"** — an absence of content evidence. ⭐ **So the rule is not "distrust identifications." It is "distrust identifications that rest on the name alone."** A case that looked like a counterexample sharpened the rule instead of breaking it.

### §3.3 The same commentator, preserving and inventing

Two facts about the *Nyāsa*, from Abhyankar, that belong side by side:

- A grammarian is "believed to have written a gloss on the Sūtras of Pāṇini **on the strength of a reference to him in the Nyāsa of Jinendrabuddhi**" (*Nyāsa* on 1.1.1). ⭐ **A lost author whose entire existence in the record is one citation in this commentary.**
- And (Ch 42 §6) Jinendrabuddhi derives **Pāṇini's own name** from Śalātura out of a rule *inside Pāṇini's grammar* — manufacturing a biography.

⭐⭐ **The same apparatus preserves an author who would otherwise be nothing and invents a biography for the one everybody has.** ⚠ The corpus's standing formulation, and this is its cleanest illustration: **the mechanism is not corrupting, it is invisible.** Only checking tells you which of the two you are holding.

## §4 ⭐ Pāṇinian grammar had regions

The corpus did not know this, and it comes from two Abhyankar entries read side by side:

- **Jinendrabuddhi** "belonged to the **eastern school** of Pāṇini's Grammar."
- **Haradatta**, author of the *Padamañjarī*, was "a very learned grammarian of the **Southern School**."
- And: "**the Benares School of Grammarians follow [the *Padamañjarī*] more than the equally learned another commentary *Kāśikāvivaraṇapañjikā* or *Nyāsa*.**"

⭐⭐ **Three named regional schools, and a recorded fact about which text one of them preferred.** And the corpus already knows who the Benares school is — [Ch 42](../hindu/shastra/42-the-ladder-and-the-boundary.md) reconstructed it: **Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita, his nephew Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa, his grandson's pupil Nāgeśa**, a Varanasi household. **The tradition Chapter 42 is about had a choice between two commentaries on the Kāśikā, and took the southern one.**

### §4.1 ⚠ The inference this chapter refuses

Jinendrabuddhi was a **Buddhist**. Haradatta was a **Śaiva**. The Benares school was **Brahminical**. The explanation writes itself, and it should not be written.

**Abhyankar gives no reason.** No source consulted gives one. And there are at least three other candidates on the page:

1. The *Padamañjarī* is **later**, and could simply have superseded.
2. Abhyankar says Haradatta "has **drawn considerably from Nyāsa**" — so a reader of the *Padamañjarī* gets much of the *Nyāsa* anyway.
3. Abhyankar separately reports that "in the Padamañjarī Haradatta is said to have given **everything of importance from the Mahābhāṣya**" — and for a Benares school built on the *Mahābhāṣya*, that is a sufficient reason with no theology in it.

⭐ **This is precisely the shape of reading that only works if you supply the motive**, which is what this project exists to not do. Recorded as an unexplained fact with four candidate explanations and no verdict.

### §4.2 The other half of Chapter 42's sharpest contrast

Ch 42 §2.4 carried Deshpande's finding: **Kaiyaṭa** (Kashmiri, Vaiṣṇava) built a **hierarchy** of the three sages topped by Patañjali, because he knew Patañjali-as-Śeṣa; **Haradatta** (southern, Śaiva) produced a **complementary, non-hierarchical** view, because he knew Pāṇini-taught-by-Maheśvara and not the Śeṣa motif. **Which myth reached which region decided which doctrine of authority each man wrote.**

Chapter 42 held one half of that contrast without a node for the other. It now has both.

⚠ **And T3 — whether Haradatta's side survives first-hand checking — is *not* discharged, and the corpus says so.** Deshpande's account was read at first hand; **Haradatta's own text was not.** The *Padamañjarī* is free on archive.org, bound together with the *Nyāsa*. **The test was not blocked. It was not done.**

### §4.3 And a date that will not settle

| Haradatta's date | who |
|---|---|
| **11th century** | Wikipedia; "most sources" |
| 12th century | one source |
| ⭐ **13th century** | **Ajotikar, Ajotikar & Scharf** — working editors, who need his date *because they use him as a dated witness* |
| "a near-contemporary of Kaiyaṭa" (11th c.) | **Deshpande** |

⭐ **The party with the strongest professional reason to get the date right is the outlier**, which inverts the source-caution Ch 42 §3 relied on. ⚠ The corpus does **not** adjudicate and specifically does not average — and it notes the cost: a 13th-century Haradatta puts two centuries between him and Kaiyaṭa, which weakens, though it does not break, Deshpande's "near-contemporary" framing in §4.2.

## §5 Puṇyarāja, and reading a Kashmiri question through a Kashmiri

**Puṇyarāja**: born in **Kashmir** in the last decades of **800 CE**; wrote the *Ṭīkā* on **kāṇḍas I and II** of Bhartṛhari's *Vākyapadīya*, where Helārāja covered kāṇḍa III.

Now recall an open question the corpus has carried since Batch 47 and narrowed in this one. **Candrācārya** — very likely the Candragomin of Ch 44 §5 — appears in the *Vākyapadīya* either **blamed** for the *Mahābhāṣya* falling into neglect or **credited** with its revival, and the secondary literature flatly contradicted itself. The corpus established two things this batch:

- Abhyankar, at first hand, gives the address: **VP II.489** is where Bhartṛhari refers to Candra.
- The **blame-verse is a different verse with different names in it** — at **VP 2.484** Bhartṛhari criticises **Vaiji, Saubhava and Haryakṣa** for *śuṣka-tarka*, "dry logic," and for ignoring Pāṇini and Patañjali. **Candrācārya is not in that list.** Five verses later he appears in the other connection: recovering Mahābhāṣya study **in Kashmir**, on a tradition **Kalhaṇa** independently records.

⭐ So the contradiction is very probably not a scholarly dispute at all but **one secondary writer attaching Candrācārya to the blame-list five verses too early.** ⚠ **Narrowed, not closed** — neither verse has been read, and the 2.484 content reaches the corpus from **the same weak provenance that generated the problem.** The corpus does not resolve a weak source with itself.

### §5.1 The coincidence that is the chapter's subject

**Both verses are in kāṇḍa II. Puṇyarāja is the commentator on kāṇḍa II.** And:

- the revival in question is a revival **in Kashmir**;
- it is corroborated by **Kalhaṇa**, a Kashmiri chronicler;
- **Puṇyarāja is a Kashmiri**, c. 800 CE;
- and **Kaiyaṭa**, whose ladder Chapter 42 is built on, is **also a Kashmiri**.

⭐⭐ **The verses about a Kashmiri revival of the Mahābhāṣya are read through a Kashmiri commentator writing three centuries later, in a tradition a fourth Kashmiri would go on to systematise.**

⚠ **This does not impugn the reading, and the corpus does not suggest it does.** It is §1.2's situation with the interest **named**. That is the most that can honestly be said, and it is worth saying: the open question is not merely unread — it is unread **through a specific apparatus whose standpoint is known**. It is a reason to read VP II.481–490 with the commentary and the region in view. It is not a conclusion about what is in them. ⚠ **No source connects Puṇyarāja's region to his handling of these verses**; the observation is the corpus's, assembled from three separately sourced facts.

### §5.2 A fourth way a text changes hands

Chapter 44 §4 catalogued three fates for a detachable module: **lost, detached, accreted.** Puṇyarāja supplies a fourth, and it is about authorship rather than contents.

Three separate authorship disputes sit on his work, and the literature keeps them apart:

1. **The *Vṛtti* on kāṇḍas I–II** — Bhartṛhari's or not? **Aklujkar, "The Authorship of the *Vākyapadīya-Vṛtti*" (1972)**.
2. **The *Ṭīkā* on kāṇḍa II** — Puṇyarāja's or not? **Aklujkar, "The Authorship of the *Vākya-kāṇḍa-ṭīkā*" (1974)** — a *separate* article, and new to this corpus.
3. **The commentary on kāṇḍa I** — ⭐ **Peri Sarveswara Sharma** holds that it "is an **abridged version of Bhartṛhari's own *Vṛtti*** and it is **wrongly attributed to Puṇyarāja**."

⭐ **(3) is a possibility neither Abhyankar nor this corpus had.** Not "Puṇyarāja wrote it," not "Bhartṛhari wrote it," but: **someone abridged Bhartṛhari, and the abridgement acquired Puṇyarāja's name.**

⚠ And it is the same move Chapter 44 watched succeed *openly* everywhere else — the Kātantra abridging Pāṇini, the Kāśikā as a beginners' gloss, Varadarāja's *Laghusiddhāntakaumudī* displacing Bhaṭṭoji's full text, Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa's own abridgement outranking his *Bhūṣaṇa*. **Here the abridgement did not merely displace its original. It changed authors.**

## §6 ⚠ *Nyāsa* is a genre, not a title

Abhyankar states this outright, and without it the corpus would have walked into it. **At least five unrelated works, across five grammatical systems, are called *Nyāsa*:**

| whose | on what | system |
|---|---|---|
| **Jinendrabuddhi** | the Kāśikāvṛtti | Pāṇinian |
| **Devanandin** | the Jainendra grammar | Jainendra |
| **Prabhācandra** | the *Amoghavṛtti* on Śākaṭāyana | Śākaṭāyana |
| a *Vṛtti* (and Hemahaṃsagaṇi's) | Hemacandra's *Śabdānuśāsana* | Haima |
| **Ugrabhūti** (11th c.), called *Śiṣyahita* | the Kātantra | Kātantra |

And the adjacent genre does it too: ***Paribhāṣāvṛtti*** is "a **general name**… **irrespective of the system of grammar**, whether it be that of Pāṇini, or of Kātantra, or of Jainendra or of Hemacandra."

The corpus has been keeping a running taxonomy of what generates a name-collision. It now has six kinds:

| generator | instance |
|---|---|
| a **personal name** | Vararuci, Patañjali, Kātyāyana |
| a **place-name** | *Kāśikā* — "as possibly they were written at Kāśī" |
| a **doctrine-name** | *Sphoṭavāda* — Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa's and Nāgeśa's |
| a **short form** | **Candra** — absorbing a Prākṛt grammarian and Candrācārya |
| ⭐ a **genre label** | ***Nyāsa***, ***Paribhāṣāvṛtti*** |
| ⭐ a **title in another śāstra** | **Haradatta**, given the Āpastamba and Gautama *Dharmasūtra* commentaries |

⭐ This is why the corpus's node is keyed **`nyasa-vyakarana`** and not `nyasa` — and the suffix is doing real work, because ***nyāsa* is separately a live technical term** in this corpus, in the Jain and Pāṇinian sense of "placing," at `nikshepa.md`.

### §6.1 Haradatta and the sixth generator, undiagnosed on purpose

Wikipedia's stub states that the *Padamañjarī*'s Haradatta "is also the author of a commentary on the ***Āpastamba Dharmasūtra***, the ***Gautama Dharmasūtra*** and other texts" — **flatly, with no doubt raised and no argument given**, citing **Patrick Olivelle (2005), pp. 301 ff.**

That is the Vararuci shape a fifth time: an identification of a grammarian with a same-named author **in a different śāstra**, asserted in the accessible source without visible argument. ⭐ **The corpus has now met this pattern once in every śāstra it has touched** — grammar/Prākṛt, grammar/yoga/medicine, grammar/poetry, grammar/pramāṇa, and now grammar/dharma.

⚠ **And §3.2's sharpened rule forbids diagnosing it.** The corpus does not know whether Olivelle *argues* the identification or *inherits* it, because Olivelle has not been read. And Jinendrabuddhi is the standing warning in the other direction: there the merge looked like the name-fallacy and turned out to rest on style and citation. ⚠ Note too that for a South Indian *paṇḍita*, writing on both grammar and dharmaśāstra is unremarkable, so the prior here is much friendlier to identity than in the Vararuci case. **Flagged as a candidate. Not diagnosed as a defect.**

## §7 Names fail in both directions

Everything above runs one way: **many men collapsing into one name.** Puṇyarāja supplies the inverse, and on his own authority — he "**identified himself as Rājānakaśūravarma**."

⭐ **One man, two names, self-reported.** ⚠ The corpus draws nothing from it: *Rājānaka* is a Kashmiri title and the second form may be a full formal name rather than an alias, which no source clarifies. But it is a useful corrective to this chapter's own habit. **Two chapters have been spent learning to distrust name-identity. Name-*difference* is equally weak evidence.**

⚠ And Candragomin is the same lesson in its most developed form. **Mark Tatz** separates a **5th-century grammarian** from a **7th-century *upāsaka* poet** who debated Candrakīrti at Nālandā for seven years, and the *Encyclopedia of Buddhism* follows him. **Wikipedia — incomparably the most-read of the three — still merges them, and does not mention the grammar at all.** Asked directly about the Cāndra-vyākaraṇa, its article offers only this: "**Tāranātha** wanted to depict Chandragomin as someone who is learned and cultured while **Butön Rinchen Drub** emphasised his work on grammar."

⭐⭐ **The division of the man is visible inside the Tibetan historiography itself** — two historians of one tradition writing up one name with different halves foregrounded — **and the modern encyclopedia inherited Tāranātha's half and dropped Butön's.** The specialist made the split; the split did not propagate.

## §8 What this chapter corrected in the corpus, and what it did not

Two errors were caught inside the batch, and both are recorded rather than silently fixed.

**1. An OCR gap reattached a relative clause to the wrong noun.** The `jinendrabuddhi` node, as first committed, reported from Abhyankar that the *Nyāsa* "unfortunately is available only in a fragmentary state at present." **It is not.** The scan breaks mid-sentence across two blocks, and the clause belongs to **Maitreya Rakṣita's twelfth-century *Tantrapradīpa***, the commentary *on* the Nyāsa. The *Nyāsa* itself is fully published and is the base of an active critical-edition project.

⭐ **That is precisely the defect Ch 42 §8 documented in a crowd-sourced source** — where dropping a teacher's name let an appositive re-attach and turn a teaching lineage into a bloodline — **arriving by a completely different road.** It was caught not by re-reading but by an independent source mentioning the *Nyāsa*'s printed edition.

**2. And the corpus had held both halves of a collision for several batches without noticing** (§3). Neither error was careless. **Both were caught by reading a second source that had no interest in the first.**

### §8.1 What is still not known

- **Neither the *Nyāsa* nor the *Padamañjarī* has been read**, though the Kāśikāvṛtti with **both** is free on archive.org.
- **No verse of Puṇyarāja's *Ṭīkā*.** Kāṇḍas I–II with it were published in the **Benares Sanskrit Series in 1884** and are out of copyright; **K. A. Subramania Iyer's English kāṇḍa II** exists.
- ⭐ **VP II.481–490 remains unread**, which is the one thing that would close the Candrācārya question outright.
- **None of Kobayashi, Hayes, Funayama or Steinkellner has been read.** ⚠ **Kobayashi is a flagged one-sided case**: the corpus knows the dissenting position only as a negative sign in someone else's bibliography.
- **Neither Aklujkar article.** **Olivelle 2005** unread, which is why §6.1 is undiagnosed.
- The Ajotikar/Scharf paper **was** read at first hand — the fetch tool could not parse the PDF, so it was extracted locally and read directly. ⭐ **It is the best-provenanced source in either of these two chapters, and it is the one that supplied both the framing sentence and the correction in §1.2.**
