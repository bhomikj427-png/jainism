## How to use this chapter

Chapter 04 covered the Buddhist tradition briefly — pratītyasamutpāda, nirvāṇa, ahiṃsā-buddhist — alongside Hindu and Jain comparanda. This chapter goes deep into the Buddhist philosophical architecture, which is far more internally diverse than any introductory comparison can show.

Buddhism contains three irreducibly different philosophical positions under one name: **Theravāda** (the oldest surviving school, grounded in Pali scripture), **Madhyamaka** ("middle-way" emptiness philosophy, Nāgārjuna ~2nd c. CE), and **Yogācāra** ("practice of yoga" / consciousness-only, Asaṅga/Vasubandhu ~4th–5th c. CE). These schools do not simply emphasise different aspects of the same teaching — they reach philosophically incompatible conclusions about what ultimately exists.

The chapter builds from Theravāda upward:

§1 starts with the Four Noble Truths — the Buddhist diagnostic framework  
§2 maps the chain: dukkha → taṇhā → paticcasamuppāda → nibbāna  
§3 covers the two great philosophical pillars: anattā and anicca  
§4 covers the arhat ideal and the Theravāda school  
§5 introduces Mahāyāna's radical revision: śūnyatā and the bodhisattva  
§6 covers the two Mahāyāna schools: Madhyamaka and Yogācāra  
§7 covers Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha)  
§8 draws the cross-tradition structural comparisons

## 1. The diagnostic framework — *catvāry ārya-satyāni* {#four-noble-truths}

*→ concept file: [four-noble-truths.md](../../concepts/four-noble-truths.md)*

The Four Noble Truths (*catvāry ārya-satyāni* / Pali *cattāri ariya-saccāni*) are the organizing framework of the entire Buddhist path. Directly fetched from SN 56.11 (*Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, Access to Insight) and MN 141 (*Saccavibhaṅga Sutta*):

| truth | Pali | content | prescribed action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st: suffering | *dukkha* | birth, aging, death, grief, pain, despair; the five aggregates are suffering | *pariññeyya* — to be fully understood |
| 2nd: origin | *samudaya* | craving (*taṇhā*) is the origin of suffering | *pahātabba* — to be abandoned |
| 3rd: cessation | *nirodha* | cessation of craving = cessation of suffering = nibbāna | *sacchikātabba* — to be directly realised |
| 4th: path | *magga* | the Noble Eightfold Path leads to cessation | *bhāvetabba* — to be developed |

**The action-prescription is distinctive**: each truth not only states a fact but names what the practitioner *does* with it (understand / abandon / realise / develop). No other tradition's equivalent framework (Jain 7 tattvas, Advaita's avidyā-jñāna pair) has this explicit per-truth praxis assignment.

> ⚠️ **"Noble Truths" ≠ truths you believe in**
>
> *Ariya* in Pali means "noble" — the truths are *noble* because they are seen and enacted by *noble ones* (ariyas — those who have entered the path). They are not propositions to hold on faith but *diagnoses to verify in one's own experience*. The First Truth is not "believe that life contains suffering" but "directly understand the nature of the five aggregates." This is a difference in the very category of religious claim.

## 2. The chain of suffering and release {#chain}

*→ concept files: [dukkha.md](../../concepts/dukkha.md) · [tanha.md](../../concepts/tanha.md) · [paticcasamuppada-pali.md](../../concepts/paticcasamuppada-pali.md) · [nibbana-theravada.md](../../concepts/nibbana-theravada.md)*

### 2.1 Dukkha — three types {#dukkha}

SN 45.165 gives three kinds of *dukkha*, not just "pain":

| type | Pali | content |
|---|---|---|
| *dukkha-dukkha* | pain-as-pain | direct physical and mental pain |
| *vipariṇāma-dukkha* | change-suffering | pleasant experiences end; impermanence produces dissatisfaction |
| *saṅkhāra-dukkha* | conditioned-suffering | the deepest: the very fact of having a conditioned existence prone to change |

The Third Noble Truth tells us this is not an existential given — it has a cause.

### 2.2 Taṇhā — craving, the engine of bondage {#tanha}

*Taṇhā* (literally "thirst") is the Second Noble Truth: **craving is the origin of suffering**. SN 56.11 specifies three types:

| type | Pali | what it craves |
|---|---|---|
| Sense craving | *kāma-taṇhā* | sensory pleasure |
| Existence craving | *bhava-taṇhā* | continued existence / eternal life |
| Non-existence craving | *vibhava-taṇhā* | annihilation / non-existence |

Even the craving for *not existing* is a form of taṇhā. Taṇhā occupies the 8th link in the 12-link chain of dependent origination.

> **Taṇhā vs Jain kaṣāya**: The structural parallel is tight — both name the passion-mechanism that drives karmic bondage. But the underlying ontologies differ: taṇhā is an *intentional mental process* (cetanā) that produces karmic results; Jain kaṣāya operates by *physically disturbing* the karmic matter already bound to the soul. One tradition makes karma mental-intentional; the other makes it physical-material. See [kashaya.md](../../concepts/kashaya.md) vs [tanha.md](../../concepts/tanha.md).

### 2.3 Paticcasamuppāda — the causal machinery {#paticcasamuppada}

*→ concept file: [paticcasamuppada-pali.md](../../concepts/paticcasamuppada-pali.md)*

The conditional formula, directly fetched:

> ***imasmiṃ sati idaṃ hoti*** — "this being, that comes to be"
> ***imassuppādā idaṃ uppajjati*** — "from the arising of this, that arises"
> ***imasmiṃ asati idaṃ na hoti*** — "this not being, that does not come to be"
> ***imassa nirodhā idaṃ nirujjhati*** — "from the cessation of this, that ceases"
> — SN 12.21 (*Cetanā Sutta*)

This formula operates in *both directions*: the forward chain (avijjā → … → birth, aging, death, sorrow) describes how suffering arises; the reverse chain (cessation of avijjā → … → cessation of suffering) *is* the path to nibbāna.

**The 12 nidānas (links):**

| # | Pali | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *avijjā* | ignorance |
| 2 | *saṅkhārā* | volitional formations |
| 3 | *viññāṇa* | consciousness |
| 4 | *nāmarūpa* | name-and-form |
| 5 | *saḷāyatana* | six sense bases |
| 6 | *phassa* | contact |
| 7 | *vedanā* | feeling-tone |
| 8 | *taṇhā* | craving |
| 9 | *upādāna* | clinging |
| 10 | *bhava* | becoming |
| 11 | *jāti* | birth |
| 12 | *jarāmaraṇa* | aging and death |

Theravāda orthodoxy reads this as a **three-lifetime structure**: links 1–2 (past life causes) → 3–10 (present life) → 11–12 (future birth). A minority reading maps all 12 onto moment-to-moment mental arising.

### 2.4 Nibbāna — the unconditioned {#nibbana}

*→ concept file: [nibbana-theravada.md](../../concepts/nibbana-theravada.md)*

Nibbāna is the cessation of *taṇhā* and all its chains. Udāna 8.1 (directly fetched):

> ***"atthi, bhikkhave, ajātaṃ abhūtaṃ akataṃ asaṅkhataṃ"***
> "There is, monks, that which is unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned."
> "If there were not that unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, there would be no escape for the born, become, made, conditioned."

This verse logically implies nibbāna is *not* mere extinction — it is a *positive unconditioned reality* that makes escape from conditioned existence possible. Two forms:

| type | Pali | content |
|---|---|---|
| *sa-upādisesa-nibbāna* | nibbāna with remainder | while still living; the five aggregates continue but the fires of passion are extinguished (the arhat's experience) |
| *anupādisesa-nibbāna* | nibbāna without remainder | at death; no further arising — *parinibbāna* |

## 3. The two philosophical pillars — *anattā* and *anicca* {#pillars}

*→ concept files: [anatta-buddhist.md](../../concepts/anatta-buddhist.md) · [anicca.md](../../concepts/anicca.md)*

### 3.0 The word underneath both pillars — *dhamma* {#dhamma}

*→ concept file: [dhamma.md](../../concepts/dhamma.md)*

Before either pillar can stand, one word has to be unpacked — and it is **the most polysemous term in the entire Indian philosophical lexicon**. Pali ***dhamma*** and Sanskrit ***dharma*** are etymologically identical (√*dhṛ*, "to hold, support") and their semantic fields then diverge radically.

Pali *dhamma* carries **four** primary senses, not mutually exclusive:

| # | sense | where it lives | gloss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **the Buddha's Teaching** (*pariyatti-dhamma*) | the Nikāyas; the second of the Three Jewels | doctrine + path + enlightenment — *"Dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi"* |
| 2 | **natural / cosmic law** (*niyāma-dhamma*) | the Dhammapada's opening; [paṭicca-samuppāda](#paticcasamuppada) | the impersonal lawfulness of conditioned existence |
| 3 | **mental phenomena / objects** (*dhammāyatana*, the sixth sense-base) | Abhidhamma psychology; the six-*āyatana* schema | any cognizable mental object, including past, future or imaginary ones |
| 4 | **constituent elements of reality** | the *Dhammasaṅgaṇī* | the irreducible momentary events — **82** in Theravāda (citta, cetasika, rūpa, nibbāna) — into which "things" are analysed |

**Senses 3 and 4 are what make [§3.1](#anatta) work**, and it is worth seeing the connection before reading the argument rather than after. If everything that appears to be "a person" resolves without remainder into **momentary, conditioned *dhamma*s** — none of them permanent, none self-owning — then there is no *attā* left over to find. The *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*'s argument runs over the five aggregates; the Abhidhamma's contribution is to push the same analysis down to the level of elementary events and make it exhaustive.

⚠ **The whole Abhidhamma enterprise is named after sense 4.** [§4](#theravada) and the *abhidharma* material below are, in a precise sense, the working-out of one meaning of this word.

### 3.0.1 Three collisions the map must teach {#dhamma-collisions}

Because the root is shared, *dhamma*/*dharma* generates the corpus's densest cluster of false equivalences. Three are worth holding apart explicitly:

| term | tradition | what it means | relation to Pali *dhamma* |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***dharma*** | Hindu (Vedic / Mīmāṃsā / Gītā) | cosmic moral order; social duty; righteousness | **same root, overlapping cosmic-law sense (2)** — but Hindu dharma is *permanent, normative and duty-centred*, not a transient phenomenal element |
| ***dharma-dravya*** | Jain | **the medium of motion**, one of the six *dravya*s | **only the name is shared.** A physical/quasi-physical substance — neither teaching, nor mental phenomenon, nor law |
| ***dhamma*** sense 4 | Theravāda Abhidhamma | irreducible momentary event | the sense that does the anattā work, and the one the other two traditions have no counterpart for |

The Jain case is the sharpest and is drawn in the graph as an explicit **NOT-equivalent** edge: [Ch 09](../jain/09-jain-cosmos.md) treats *dharma-dravya* as one of the six substances of the Jain cosmos, and a reader who moves between these chapters on the strength of the shared word will get both wrong.

> **The general lesson.** A shared etymology guarantees a shared *history*, not a shared *referent*. √*dhṛ* tells you these words were once the same word. It tells you nothing about whether the Buddhist analysing a moment of consciousness and the Jain describing what lets a body move are talking about anything remotely alike. Ch 28 anatomises the same failure with modern-science vocabulary; this is the intra-Indian version, and it is easier to fall for because the terms really are cognate.

### 3.1 Anattā — no permanent self {#anatta}

*Anattā* (not-self) is Buddhism's most distinctive and most debated doctrine. SN 22.59 (*Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*), directly fetched (two independent translations: Thanissaro Bhikkhu + Ñāṇamoli Bhikkhu):

> *"Form is not self... If form were self, then form would not lead to affliction."*
> The same argument applies to feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness.

The argument structure: *"If X were self, X would be under self's control; but X is not under our control (it changes, leads to suffering); therefore X is not self."* Applied to all five aggregates (*khandhas*), the conclusion: there is no permanent, unchanging self anywhere in experience.

**The contested reading (corpus: anatta-buddhist.md, status: contested)**:

| reading | proponent | what it says |
|---|---|---|
| Ontological no-self | Bodhi 2000; most Theravāda commentators | there is *no self whatsoever* — anattā is a metaphysical claim |
| Methodological not-self | Thanissaro Bhikkhu | the teaching is a *practice tool*: "don't cling to X as self"; does not assert that self cannot exist |

The methodological reading is supported by the grammatical form (adjective *anattā*, not noun *anattā-n*) and by the pragmatic framing in SN 22.59. The ontological reading is the dominant commentary tradition. Both are documented in [anatta-buddhist.md](../../concepts/anatta-buddhist.md); neither is adopted as the settled position.

> ⚠️ **Anattā is the direct denial of Jain *jīva***
>
> The Jain *jīva* is a real, permanent, individual conscious substance. The Theravāda anattā doctrine denies exactly this: there is no permanent, individual conscious substance. Not a surface tension — a direct ontological contradiction. The standard cross-tradition comparison table:
>
> | question | Jain | Theravāda Buddhist |
> |---|---|---|
> | Is there a permanent conscious self? | Yes — the individual *jīva* | No — anattā |
> | What cycles in *saṃsāra*? | The individual *jīva* | A causal stream (no fixed "what") |
> | What is liberated? | The individual *jīva* (now omniscient, at *siddha-śilā*) | The cessation of the causal stream; no "who" is liberated |

### 3.2 Anicca — impermanence {#anicca}

*Anicca* (Pali; Sanskrit *anitya*) — impermanence — is the first of the Three Marks of Existence (*tilakkhaṇa*). Dhammapada 277:

> ***"sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā"*** — "All conditioned phenomena are impermanent."

The three marks together: *anicca* (impermanent) + *dukkha* (unsatisfying) + *anattā* (not-self). Each entails the others: because things are impermanent, clinging to them produces suffering; because there is no permanent self, the notion of "mine" is a superimposition.

**Cross-tradition: the permanence triangle**

| tradition | position on permanence |
|---|---|
| Theravāda | all conditioned phenomena are impermanent; the unconditioned (nibbāna) is beyond the anicca/nicca distinction |
| Jain *sat* | every *dravya* is permanent (the dravayta itself); what changes is the *paryāya* (mode). Permanence-in-change is the Jain middle ground. |
| Advaita | only *nirguṇa* Brahman is truly permanent; the world's apparent change is māyā. Permanence is the ground, change is appearance. |

## 4. The Theravāda school and the arhat ideal {#theravada}

*→ concept files: [theravada.md](../../concepts/theravada.md) · [arhat.md](../../concepts/arhat.md) · [skandha-buddhist.md](../../concepts/skandha-buddhist.md) · [abhidharma.md](../../concepts/abhidharma.md)*

**Theravāda** ("teaching of the elders") is the oldest surviving Buddhist school, based on the Pali *Tipiṭaka* (three baskets: Vinaya / Sutta / Abhidhamma). Its characteristic feature: the **arhat ideal** — the goal of the Theravāda path is to become an arhat (one who has extinguished all *āsavas* = taints/outflows).

SN 22.110 (directly fetched, Access to Insight):
Four stages of the path: Stream-enterer → Once-returner → Non-returner → Arhat.
Ten fetters (*saṃyojana*) are progressively cut: the first three at stream-entry, nine by non-return, all ten by arahantship.

**The Abhidharma project**: Theravāda's philosophical arm is the Abhidharma — a systematic analysis of all mental and physical phenomena into **82 ultimate *dhammas*** (irreducible factors of experience). This is the Buddhist "minimal ontology": not substances but *events*; not souls but *processes*.

The Abhidharmakośa (Vasubandhu, ~4th–5th c.) is the most comprehensive treatment, listing **75 dharmas** (Sarvāstivāda count). The key philosophical move: replace the question "what *substances* exist?" with "what *events* actually occur in experience?" — this eliminates the ātman by analysing experience down to impermanent dharmas with no permanent substrate.

## 5. The Mahāyāna revolution — *śūnyatā* and *bodhicitta* {#mahayana}

*→ concept files: [sunyata.md](../../concepts/sunyata.md) · [bodhicitta.md](../../concepts/bodhicitta.md) · [bodhisattva.md](../../concepts/bodhisattva.md)*

Mahāyāna ("great vehicle," vs Theravāda's "lesser vehicle" *hīnayāna* — a polemical term now avoided) introduces two conceptual revolutions:

**1. Śūnyatā (emptiness):** All phenomena — including the *dhammas* of Abhidharma — are empty of *svabhāva* (own-nature / self-existence). Even the dharmas are not ultimately real atomic events; they are empty of intrinsic nature. MMK 24.18 (Nāgārjuna):

> ***"yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaḥ śūnyatāṃ tāṃ pracakṣmahe"***
> "Whatever is dependently co-arisen, that we call emptiness."

Emptiness is not nihilism — śūnyatā is itself empty (*śūnyatā-śūnyatā*). It is the flip side of dependent arising: to be dependently arisen is precisely to lack intrinsic being. See [sunyata.md](../../concepts/sunyata.md).

**2. Bodhicitta and the bodhisattva vow:** Mahāyāna's second revolution is ethical: the ideal practitioner is not an arhat seeking personal liberation but a **bodhisattva** — one who vows to attain Buddhahood for the benefit of *all* sentient beings. This requires *bodhicitta* — the awakening mind, the aspiration for liberation of all. The bodhisattva delays parinirvāṇa indefinitely out of compassion.

Śāntideva (*Bodhicaryāvatāra*): *"When bodhicitta has arisen, he is straightway hailed son of the Sugatas, to be revered in the worlds."*

> ⚠️ **Bodhisattva ideal ≠ arhat ideal ≠ Jain *siddha***
>
> | | goal | scope | mechanism |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Theravāda arhat | personal cessation of *āsavas* | self | Noble Eightfold Path |
> | Mahāyāna bodhisattva | Buddhahood for all beings | all sentient beings | bodhicitta + six pāramitās |
> | Jain *siddha* | individual omniscience + liberation | self | ratnatraya (effort alone) |
>
> The bodhisattva and the Jain *siddha* are as different as their traditions allow: one remains in *saṃsāra* by vow; the other escapes *saṃsāra* by effort and resides at the universe's summit forever.

## 6. The two Mahāyāna schools {#mahayana-schools}

### 6.1 Madhyamaka — the middle-way school {#madhyamaka}

*→ concept files: [madhyamaka.md](../../concepts/madhyamaka.md) · [dvisatya.md](../../concepts/dvisatya.md) · [catuskoti.md](../../concepts/catuskoti.md) · [prasanga-nagarjuna.md](../../concepts/prasanga-nagarjuna.md)*

**Nāgārjuna** (~150–250 CE), *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK): all phenomena are *śūnya* (empty) of *svabhāva*. The method is **prasaṅga** — drawing out the contradictions of any positive metaphysical claim. Nāgārjuna does not assert "emptiness is the ultimate truth" but rather reduces all positions to absurdity: he has *no thesis* of his own (*VV 29*: "If I had a proposition, I would have that defect").

**Two truths (*dvisatya*):** MMK 24.8:

| truth | Sanskrit | content | role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional | *saṃvṛti-satya* | ordinary phenomenal world — persons, tables, suffering | the level at which the Eightfold Path and ethical life operate |
| Ultimate | *paramārtha-satya* | śūnyatā — the empty nature of all dharmas | the level at which no fixed entities exist |

The two truths are *not* a hierarchy where the conventional is "merely practical" — they are co-dependent. Understanding śūnyatā via the conventional is the only access route; the conventional is *real* in its domain.

> ⚠️ **Madhyamaka two truths ≠ Advaita two truths ≠ Jain two standpoints**
>
> All three traditions have a "two-level" analysis. But the ontological commitments differ dramatically:
> - **Madhyamaka** (*saṃvṛti/paramārtha*): both levels empty of intrinsic being; ultimate is emptiness, not a positive absolute
> - **Advaita** (*vyāvahārika/pāramārthika*): conventional is real but reducible to nirguṇa Brahman; Brahman is the positive ultimate
> - **Jain** (*vyavahāra/niścaya*): conventional (empirical descriptions of the soul) and absolute (pure-soul standpoint) are *both real* — Jain does not privilege niścaya over vyavahāra
>
> Three two-level schemes; three incompatible ontologies.

### 6.2 Yogācāra — consciousness-only {#yogacara}

*→ concept files: [yogacara.md](../../concepts/yogacara.md) · [vijnaptimatrata.md](../../concepts/vijnaptimatrata.md) · [alaya-vijnana.md](../../concepts/alaya-vijnana.md)*

**Asaṅga** (~4th–5th c. CE) and his brother **Vasubandhu** develop Yogācāra: *vijñaptimātratā* ("consciousness-only" or "mere representation") — all apparent external objects are *vijñaptis* (representations/cognitions), not independently real mind-external objects. The phenomenal world is constructed within consciousness.

**The *ālaya-vijñāna*** (storehouse consciousness) is Yogācāra's solution to rebirth without a permanent self:
- The ālaya stores **seeds (*bījas*)** of past actions and experiences.
- These seeds ripen into new experiences, maintaining karmic continuity across lives *without* positing a fixed ātman.
- The ālaya is itself impermanent (a stream, not a substance) — consistent with anattā.

**Vijñaptimātratā disputed (corpus: vijnaptimatrata.md, status: contested)**:

| reading | proponent | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphysical idealism | standard Western reading | mind-external objects do not exist |
| Phenomenological | Kellner + Taber 2014 | external objects cannot be *known directly* (epistemological claim only) |

The dispute matters: if Yogācāra is metaphysical idealism, it contradicts Madhyamaka (which holds that *all* phenomena are empty, including consciousness). If it is epistemological, the contradiction dissolves. Śāntarakṣita's synthesis (~8th c.) accepted Yogācāra at the conventional level, Madhyamaka at the ultimate.

> ⚠️ **Vijñaptimātratā ≠ Advaita's Brahman-as-consciousness**
>
> Both say "reality is consciousness." But:
> - **Yogācāra**: consciousness is impermanent (stream of *vijñaptis*/seeds); no single absolute; multiple minds
> - **Advaita**: Brahman is *one* eternal unchanging consciousness; the appearance of multiple minds is māyā
>
> One tradition denies a single absolute; the other requires it.

## 7. Buddha-nature — *tathāgatagarbha* {#tathagatagarha}

*→ concept file: [tathagata-garbha.md](../../concepts/tathagata-garbha.md)*

The *Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra* (3rd–4th c. CE) introduces a surprising claim: all sentient beings contain a *tathāgatagarbha* — "womb/embryo of the Tathāgata (Buddha)." Every being is, in some sense, already a potential Buddha.

**Two readings:**

| reading | content | who holds it |
|---|---|---|
| Madhyamaka interpretation | tathāgatagarbha = a *metaphor* for the potential to realise emptiness; not a positive entity | Madhyamaka commentators |
| Yogācāra interpretation | tathāgatagarbha = identified with the *ālaya-vijñāna*; a positive seed of Buddhahood | Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra |

> ⚠️ **Tathāgatagarbha ≠ Hindu *ātman***
>
> The tathāgatagarbha concept was attacked by non-Buddhists as smuggling the ātman back under a Buddhist name. The Buddhist tradition's responses:
> - It is a *skillful means* (upāya) — a teaching device for those who fear emptiness, not an ontological assertion of a self.
> - Even if "Buddha-nature" is a positive principle, it lacks the defining features of ātman: it is not *permanent, pleasurable, self, and pure* in the Hindu Sāṃkhya sense. See [tathagata-garbha.md](../../concepts/tathagata-garbha.md).

## 8. Cross-tradition: Buddhism, Jain, and Vedānta {#cross-tradition}

| question | Theravāda | Madhyamaka | Yogācāra | Jain | Advaita |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| What ultimately exists? | 82 impermanent *dhammas* | Nothing with intrinsic being; dependent arising only | Streams of consciousness-only | Six eternal substances (jīva/pudgala etc.) | One nirguṇa Brahman |
| Is there a self? | No (*anattā*) | No (and not-not-self either) | No fixed self; consciousness streams | Yes — individual *jīva* | Ultimate: No (ātman=Brahman); conventional: yes |
| Is the world real? | Conventionally real | Conventionally real, ultimately empty | Constructed within consciousness | Fully real (six dravyas) | Ultimately reducible to Brahman; appears through māyā |
| What is liberation? | Nibbāna — cessation of craving | Realising śūnyatā; no separate nirvāṇa-entity | Transformation of ālaya; āśraya-parāvṛtti | Individual *jīva* at *siddha-śilā*; omniscient | Realising ātman=Brahman; *jīvanmukti* |
| Creator-God? | No | No | No | No | No personal creator in Advaita (but Saguṇa Brahman in practice) |

The most decisive cross-tradition contrast in the corpus: **Jain realism vs Buddhist śūnyatā**.

Jain: the world is a real plurality of *dravyas* with real properties. Change is real (*paryāya*) but so is permanence (*dravya*). The soul is real and individual.

Buddhist (Madhyamaka): nothing has intrinsic being. Even the Buddhist teaching of dependent arising is itself empty. If you say "the dravyas are real substances," you are treating phenomena as having intrinsic being, which is precisely the target of MMK's critique.

These two positions are **not merely different emphases** — they are direct contradictions on what it means for something to be "real."
