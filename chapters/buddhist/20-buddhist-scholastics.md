# Chapter 20 — Buddhist Scholastics: The Figures, the Texts, and the Six-Century Argument

**Concepts covered:** nāgārjuna · mūlamadhyamakakārikā · prasaṅga · śāntideva · vasubandhu · abhidharmakośa · asaṅga · trisvabhāva · pramāṇasamuccaya · dharmakīrti · pramāṇavārttika · śāntarakṣita · kamalaśīla · apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa (nirvāṇa-mahāyāna) · dharmottara (primary-covered in Ch. 05)
**Confidence:** medium throughout — but this is the **best-sourced cluster in the Hindu/Buddhist half of the corpus**, and the reason is worth stating: five of these files rest on the **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy** or the **Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (peer-reviewed) rather than on general encyclopedias, and several quote verses directly (MMK 24.18; MA 1, 61–62, 91, 92; *Vigrahavyāvartanī* 29 in paraphrase; *Trisvabhāvanirdeśa* 1–3, 20–21, 27–28 in Garfield's translation). **No Sanskrit critical edition was fetched**, and for two texts there is no Sanskrit to fetch (see the transmission note in §6.1 and §5.3).
**Nothing here is `contested`** as a file status — but two live scholarly disputes are recorded inside converged files and are treated as findings: the **"two Vasubandhus"** question (§4.1) and **Dharmakīrti's dates** (§6.2).

## How to use this chapter

Chapter 12 gave the **doctrine**: the four noble truths, dependent origination, anattā, anicca, śūnyatā, the two Mahāyāna schools, buddha-nature. Chapter 05 gave the **epistemology**: pramāṇa, the two-vs-four dispute, vyāpti, apoha, hetu-vidyā and its transmission. Chapter 06 gave the **logic comparanda**: catuṣkoṭi against many-valued and paraconsistent formalisms.

This chapter gives the **people and the books** — who actually wrote these positions down, in what order, against whom, and what each text physically is. It runs roughly **150 CE to 800 CE**: from Nāgārjuna to the Samye debate, six centuries in which Indian Buddhism produced its systematic philosophy and then exported it.

**Remit note.** Madhyamaka and Yogācāra as *doctrines* are Ch. 12 §6 and are not re-derived. The two-pramāṇa scheme, apoha, and trairūpya as *epistemology* are Ch. 05 §§4, 6, 9 — summarised here only where a person or text needs them. **Dharmottara is primary-covered in Ch. 05**; he appears here as the terminal node of the logic lineage, not as a fresh treatment.

**The organising question.** Ch. 12 presents Madhyamaka and Yogācāra as the two wings of Mahāyāna philosophy, which is correct doxographically and slightly misleading historically. What the figures in this chapter actually show is a **single running argument with a specific shape**: each generation accepts its predecessor's negative result and then asks what can still be said. Nāgārjuna says nothing can be asserted; Vasubandhu and Asaṅga say the *appearing* can be analysed; Dignāga and Dharmakīrti say the analysis needs a logic; Śāntarakṣita says all three are true at different levels and builds a ladder. §11 traces that arc. It ends, not accidentally, in Tibet.

## 1. Two wings, two root-authors, one structural pattern {#two-wings}

Before the individual figures, the shape:

| | **Madhyamaka** | **Yogācāra** |
|---|---|---|
| root-author(s) | **Nāgārjuna** (~150–250 CE) | **Asaṅga** (~300–370) + **Vasubandhu** (~380–415) — half-brothers |
| founding text | *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* — ~450 verses, 27 chapters | *Yogācārabhūmi* (Asaṅga, encyclopedic); *Viṃśatikā* / *Triṃśikā* / *Trisvabhāvanirdeśa* (Vasubandhu, concise) |
| central move | everything is **empty of *svabhāva*** because dependently arisen | experience is **appearance-only** (*vijñaptimātra*), analysable into **three natures** |
| method | **prasaṅga** — reductio inside the opponent's own premises, asserting no thesis | positive systematic analysis of how cognition constructs its object |
| self-description | "I assert no thesis" | a *complete* scholastic architecture, meditation-grounded |

The corpus stores `asanga structurally-parallel-to nagarjuna` and `vasubandhu structurally-parallel-to nagarjuna` on exactly this basis — *"the two great post-canonical Mahāyāna systematizers,"* each a root-author whose verse treatises define a wing.

But notice the asymmetry the table exposes, because it will matter at every later stage. **Madhyamaka's founding gesture is a refusal to build; Yogācāra's is a building.** One school's foundational text is 27 chapters of demolition that explicitly disclaims a positive thesis. The other's is an encyclopedia. Everything from §7 onward is the tradition trying to hold both.

## 2. Nāgārjuna {#nagarjuna}

*→ concept file: [nagarjuna.md](../../concepts/nagarjuna.md)*

**Nāgārjuna** (~150–250 CE), founder of Madhyamaka, is called "the most important Buddhist philosopher after the historical Buddha himself" and, in Tibetan and East Asian traditions, **"the second Buddha."**

**The one reliable biographical anchor** is worth isolating from the hagiography around it: his **correspondence with King Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi** (r. ~166–196 CE). He was likely born into a Brahmin family in South India (the Andhra region). The later Tibetan and Chinese biographies incorporate extensive legendary material — including the celebrated story of his retrieving the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras from the nāgas — and the IEP treats them as historically unreliable. So: a datable royal correspondence, a plausible region, and then a great deal of story.

**The corpus of works is itself sorted by attestation**, which is the right discipline and one this chapter repeats:

| status | works |
|---|---|
| **well-attested**, Buddhist-focused | *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, *Śūnyatāsaptatī*, *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā*, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa* |
| **disputed** — no Sanskrit original, attributed via Tibetan/Chinese, heavy redaction | *Ratnāvalī*, *Suhṛllekha* |

The pattern will recur (Asaṅga's Maitreya-attributed texts, §5.1; the two-Vasubandhus question, §4.1): **a great name attracts attributions**, and the corpus's habit of separating well-attested from disputed is doing real work rather than pedantry.

### 2.1 The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā {#mmk}

*→ concept file: [mulamadhyamakakarika.md](../../concepts/mulamadhyamakakarika.md)*

**~450 verses (*kārikās*) in 27 chapters.** Each chapter takes a fundamental Buddhist or Brahmanical category — causation (*pratyaya*), motion, the senses, the aggregates, time, the self, action, suffering, the Buddha, the four noble truths, **nirvāṇa** — and shows that it has no ***svabhāva***, no inherent existence or own-being.

**The dedication negates eight extremes** before the argument even starts — the *aṣṭa-niṣedha*: no cessation, no arising; no annihilation, no permanence; no coming, no going; no difference, no identity. The concept file's technical description matters: this is a ***non-affirming negation***. Denying "arising" does not commit you to "non-arising"; it withdraws the question. That distinction is the whole grammar of the text, and missing it produces the standard misreading of Madhyamaka as nihilism.

**MMK 24.18 is the pivotal verse**, and one of the two things in this chapter quoted directly:

> "Whatever is dependently co-arisen, that is explained to be emptiness; that, being a dependent designation, is itself the middle way."

In one verse it **identifies four things**: emptiness (*śūnyatā*), dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), dependent designation (*upādāya prajñapti*), and the middle way (*madhyamā pratipad*). That identification is the doctrinal heart of the school, and it is also the school's defence against the nihilism charge: emptiness *is* dependent arising, so to be empty is precisely **to exist dependently**, not to fail to exist.

**MMK 25.19 is the most radical claim in the text:** *"There is not the slightest distinction between saṃsāra and nirvāṇa."* Not a mystical flourish — a conclusion. If both are empty of fixed essence, the sharp ontological boundary the Abhidharma scholastics had reified between the conditioned and the unconditioned dissolves. §10 shows what the Mahāyāna later built on that sentence.

**Two truths (MMK 24.8–10)** carry the weight: **conventional truth (*saṃvṛti-satya*)** — how things appear within our conceptual frameworks — and **ultimate truth (*paramārtha-satya*)** — how they stand under analysis. And the safeguard against turning emptiness into a new absolute: ***emptiness is itself empty***. It is not a substance, not a ground, not a final thing; only the absence of svabhāva.

> ⚠️ **MMK ∥ Abhidharma — and NOT equivalent.** Both are systematic analyses decomposing experience into categories. But **Abhidharma grants its ultimate dharmas their own svabhāva** — they are the irreducible reals (Ch. 12 §4; §4.2 below). **The MMK's whole point is to empty those dharmas of svabhāva.** The MMK is, in large part, a refutation of Abhidharma realism — which means the text's principal opponent is not a Brahmanical school but **other Buddhists**. That is the single most useful fact for reading it.

### 2.2 Prasaṅga — and the claim to hold no thesis {#prasanga}

*→ concept file: [prasanga-nagarjuna.md](../../concepts/prasanga-nagarjuna.md)*

***Prasaṅga*** ("[unwanted] consequence"; *prasaṅgāpādana*, "fastening the consequence") is a reductio that draws absurdity out of an opponent's **own** premises. The Mādhyamika grants the essentialist's assumptions **provisionally**, shows they collapse into contradiction or infinite regress, and **stops there** — endorsing no positive alternative.

**The "no thesis" claim is the method's philosophical core, and it was forced out of Nāgārjuna by a good objection.** Naiyāyika logicians pressed the obvious dilemma: "everything is empty" must itself be either a true thesis (hence non-empty, hence self-refuting) or false. *Vigrahavyāvartanī* 29, in the sources' paraphrase:

> *"If my statement [about emptiness] were a philosophical thesis, then it would indeed be flawed; but I assert no thesis, and so the flaw is not mine."*

He casts himself as a ***vaitaṇḍika*** — a debater who refutes rival positions while advancing none of his own. And this dovetails exactly with emptiness being itself empty: **claiming emptiness is not claiming that a thing exists**, so there is no thesis to turn on him. Whether that reply succeeds is one of the genuinely live questions in the philosophy of Madhyamaka; the corpus records the reply and the objection without adjudicating.

**The Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika split** is a dispute about whether prasaṅga is *sufficient*, and it is the defining intra-Madhyamaka division:

| position | figure | claim |
|---|---|---|
| bare prasaṅga suffices | **Buddhapālita** | refute from the opponent's premises; add nothing |
| autonomous inference is needed | **Bhāvaviveka** (c. 500–578) | Madhyamaka must offer *svatantra-anumāna* — independent inferences with premises both parties accept |
| prasaṅga only | **Candrakīrti** (the *Prasannapadā*) | the Mādhyamika and the essentialist hold *"two irreconcilable points of view"*; there is **no shared object** to ground an autonomous syllogism, and accepting conventional characteristics "according to their own marks" smuggles in a subtle grasping at inherent existence |

Tsongkhapa's later gloss states the Prāsaṅgika principle cleanly: debate must be *"founded on what the parties accept as valid,"* so one refutes opponents *"in terms of what they accept,"* never one's own commitments. Hold onto this: **Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla are Svātantrikas** (§7), and their entire method depends on Bhāvaviveka having been right.

> ⚠️ **Three non-equivalences the corpus draws on prasaṅga, all instructive:**
>
> **Prasaṅga ≠ Nyāya *tarka* (Ch. 05 §7.3; Ch. 16).** Same form exactly — assume a position, derive absurdity, reject it. **Opposite aim:** Nyāya *tarka* uses reductio to **confirm a surviving positive thesis** (it removes doubt about vyāpti); prasaṅga refutes all positions and confirms none. This is the cleanest "identical method, opposite purpose" pair in the corpus.
>
> **Prasaṅga ≠ Nyāya *anumāna*.** It is not a five-member syllogism, it asserts no *pakṣa* of its own, and it grants premises only *ad hominem* — where anumāna commits its arguer to the conclusion.
>
> **Prasaṅga ≠ Pyrrhonism (Ch. 14).** The parallel is real and striking: the "no thesis" stance mirrors the Pyrrhonist's *aphasia*, non-assertion — refuting all positions without affirming a counter-position. It fails on **purpose**: prasaṅga serves a determinate end (demonstrating śūnyatā within a soteriology, on the way to liberation); Pyrrhonist suspension is a **content-free therapy** aimed only at *ataraxia*. Two identical-looking silences, one of which is going somewhere.

## 3. Śāntideva — Madhyamaka as an ethics {#santideva}

*→ concept file: [santideva.md](../../concepts/santideva.md)*

**Śāntideva** (~685–763 CE), a monk at **Nālandā** and a follower of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka, wrote the ***Bodhicaryāvatāra*** (BCA, "Way of the Bodhisattva") — **the most widely read Mahāyāna ethical-philosophical text in the Tibetan tradition** — and the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* ("Compendium of Training"). A third work, the *Sūtrasamuccaya*, is lost.

**The BCA:** ten chapters, ~1,000 Sanskrit verses, tracing the progressive development of ***bodhicitta*** — the awakening-mind dedicated to liberating all sentient beings (Ch. 12 §5) — through the six *pāramitās*: generosity, ethics, patience, effort, meditation, wisdom.

**Two chapters carry the philosophy.** **Chapter 9 (wisdom, *prajñā*)** presents a Madhyamaka argument for śūnyatā that became **the standard exposition in Tibetan scholasticism** — which is to say the way most Buddhists have actually encountered Madhyamaka for a thousand years is through Śāntideva, not through the MMK. And **Chapter 6 (patience, *kṣānti*)** does something more unusual: it **grounds ethics directly in metaphysics**. Its analysis of anger runs from *no self* to *no one to be angry at* — anattā deployed not as a doctrine to be believed but as a technique for dismantling a specific emotion. This is the clearest case in the corpus of a metaphysical thesis doing immediate practical work.

**The biography is explicitly sorted into legend and history** by the concept file's sources, and the sorting is characteristic of this chapter's material:

- **pious legend** (so classified by the Encyclopedia of Buddhism): son of a king; renounced the throne after visions of Mañjuśrī; appeared indolent at Nālandā — "eating, sleeping, using the toilet" — until, invited to recite in order to humiliate him, he delivered the entire BCA extemporaneously.
- **historical core**: a Nālandā scholar, in the Madhyamaka tradition, 8th century.

**108 commentaries** were reportedly written on the BCA in India alone, and it remains a living commentary object — Paṭrul Rinpoche's *Words of My Perfect Teacher*, the Dalai Lama's repeated public teachings. Compare §6.1's Pramāṇasamuccaya, whose Sanskrit is lost entirely. **Two texts of roughly comparable stature, opposite fates**, and the difference is that one of them is used in practice rather than only in the curriculum.

## 4. Vasubandhu — a career in two halves {#vasubandhu}

*→ concept file: [vasubandhu.md](../../concepts/vasubandhu.md)*

**Vasubandhu** (fl. ~4th–5th c. CE; SEP narrows to **~380–415 CE**, teaching Candragupta II's family; by tradition from Gandhāra/Puruṣapura) is one of the most influential systematic philosophers of Indian Buddhism — and the corpus's most interesting case of **a thinker who changed his mind in public**. Counted among the Seventeen Nālandā Masters and revered as a patriarch in East Asian schools.

**Phase one — Abhidharma, and a scandal.** He composed the **Abhidharmakośa-kārikā**, a verse compendium of orthodox **Sarvāstivāda-Vaibhāṣika** Abhidharma — and then wrote his own prose ***bhāṣya*** (auto-commentary) that **critiques that very system** from the more economical **Sautrāntika** standpoint, notably on momentariness and unperceived matter.

The concept file's phrase for this is exact: *"the unusual fact of a root-text defending what its own commentary attacks."* His Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika colleagues were, per SEP, **"livid."** And the result was not marginalisation but canonisation: the double stance made the *Kośa* simultaneously the standard textbook and a provocation, and therefore **the indispensable summa of Abhidharma debate** for both Tibetan and East Asian Buddhism. **Saṃghabhadra** wrote a lengthy defence of Sarvāstivāda orthodoxy in reply; **Yaśomitra** wrote the sub-commentary (*Sphuṭārthā*). A text that argues with itself generated a literature.

**Phase two — Yogācāra.** Converted to the Mahāyāna by tradition under his elder half-brother **Asaṅga**, he produced the concise treatises that **set the standard** for the school: the ***Viṃśatikā*** (Twenty Verses) and ***Triṃśikā*** (Thirty Verses) establishing ***vijñaptimātra***; the ***Trisvabhāva-nirdeśa*** on the three natures (§5.2); and, in the *Triṃśikā* and *Karmasiddhiprakaraṇa*, the account of the ***ālaya-vijñāna***, the storehouse-consciousness that carries karmic continuity without a self (Ch. 12 §6.2).

He also wrote the ***Vāda-vidhi***, cited as an early landmark of Indian formal logic — **the methodological ground his successor Dignāga built on** (§6.1). So the same man stands at the head of the Abhidharma summa, the Yogācāra treatises, and the logic tradition. Which is precisely why the next question arises.

### 4.1 Two open questions, both left open {#vasubandhu-questions}

| question | state of the debate |
|---|---|
| **"Two Vasubandhus"** — Frauwallner's 1950s thesis that one Sautrāntika and one Yogācārin author were conflated | **No consensus.** The theory "holds sway in some areas," but the trend (Lusthaus and others) is toward **one author whose thought evolved** rather than two people. Wikipedia calls it "largely abandoned"; SEP says "no consensus." The corpus records both characterisations rather than picking. |
| **What does *vijñaptimātra* mean?** | Contested between an **idealist** reading (only mind exists) and a **phenomenological** one. SEP stresses that "appearance-only does **not** mean the non-existence of dharmas" — only their lack of independent nature; Vasubandhu distinguishes conventional appearance, mental causation, and an ineffable ultimate. |

The second is the more consequential, and it is worth being blunt about the stakes. **"Yogācāra is Buddhist idealism" is the standard textbook gloss, and it may be wrong** — or at least it is a live scholarly dispute presented as settled fact in most introductions. The phenomenological reading takes vijñaptimātra as a claim about the *structure of experience* (there is no cognition of an object that is not a cognition), not a metaphysical claim that minds are all that exist. Ch. 12 §6.2 states the doctrine; this chapter records that its interpretation is open.

### 4.2 The Abhidharmakośa {#abhidharmakosa}

*→ concept file: [abhidharmakosa.md](../../concepts/abhidharmakosa.md)*

Two layers, and the relation between them is the point:

- the ***kārikā*** — root text: **eight chapters, ~600 verses**, systematically summarising **Vaibhāṣika** (Kashmiri Sarvāstivāda) Abhidharma;
- the ***bhāṣya*** — Vasubandhu's own prose auto-commentary, expounding the verses while repeatedly **critiquing them from the Sautrāntika side** (and sometimes defending Vaibhāṣika against Sautrāntika).

**The 75 dharmas.** The Kośa gives the classic Sarvāstivāda catalogue of 75 dharma-types (the count Ch. 12 §4 uses), in five groups: matter (*rūpa*), mind (*citta*), mental factors (*caitta*), factors dissociated from mind (*cittaviprayukta-saṃskāra*), and the unconditioned (*asaṃskṛta*).

**The eight chapters:** (1) elements/*dhātu*, (2) faculties/*indriya*, (3) world-cosmology with dependent origination, (4) **karma**, (5) defilements/*kleśa*, (6) the path and the noble persons, (7) knowledges/*jñāna*, (8) meditative attainments/*samāpatti*.

**And a ninth.** The Bhāṣya adds a **ninth prose section with no corresponding verses** — the *Pudgala-pratiṣedha*, a sustained refutation of the "person" (*pudgala*) as a real entity, directed at the **Pudgalavāda (Vātsīputrīya)** Buddhists who held that something person-like is neither identical to nor different from the aggregates. It is a *locus classicus* of the Buddhist no-self argument (Ch. 12 §3.1). Note where it sits: **in the commentary, not the root text.** The most philosophically famous section of the work is formally an appendix.

**Transmission:** the Kośa became "the main source of Abhidharma for later Mahāyāna Buddhists," surviving in Sanskrit and in **Chinese** (Paramārtha, 564–567; Xuanzang, 651–654) and **Tibetan**, and it remains a core Tibetan monastic-curriculum text.

> **A cross-tradition parallel the corpus draws and this chapter likes:** `abhidharmakosa structurally-parallel-to tattvartha-sutra`. Both are **roughly contemporary (4th–5th c.)** authoritative verse-compendia — *kārikā* and *sūtra* — that **compress an entire tradition's metaphysics into one root text plus commentary**, and both became the text through which their tradition is taught. Buddhist Sarvāstivāda and Jain, doing the same genre-move in the same centuries. (The Tattvārtha Sūtra's own recension-and-commentary structure is worked out in full in the Jain translation reading-room.)

## 5. Asaṅga, and the three natures {#asanga}

### 5.1 Asaṅga — the systematic architect {#asanga-person}

*→ concept file: [asanga.md](../../concepts/asanga.md)*

**Asaṅga** (fl. ~4th c., traditionally **~300–370 CE**; by tradition born in Puruṣapura/Gandhāra) is, with Vasubandhu, one of the two founders of **Yogācāra** — also called **Vijñānavāda** ("consciousness-doctrine") or **Cittamātra** ("mind-only"). The concept file's division of labour between the brothers is a useful one: **where Vasubandhu is the school's sharpest dialectician, Asaṅga is its systematic architect** — the encyclopedic builder of a meditation-grounded scholastic framework.

Three principal works: the ***Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*** (the encyclopedic framework), the ***Mahāyāna-saṃgraha*** (Compendium of the Great Vehicle — one of the foundational systematic expositions of the *ālaya-vijñāna*), and the ***Abhidharma-samuccaya***, which **recasts Abhidharma categories on Mahāyāna foundations**. That last is worth pausing on: Yogācāra did not abandon the Abhidharma project, it rebuilt it — which is exactly what one would expect from the school whose other founder had written the Abhidharma summa.

And note the direction of influence between the brothers, which the corpus stores as `vasubandhu historically-influenced-by asanga`: **Asaṅga, already a Mahāyānist, converted Vasubandhu** from Sarvāstivāda-Sautrāntika Abhidharma. The elder brother turned the younger, and the younger then wrote the treatises the school is known by.

**The Maitreya question — flagged, not resolved.** Asaṅga is said to have ascended to **Tuṣita Heaven** to receive teachings from the bodhisattva **Maitreya** (or from a human teacher, "Maitreyanātha"). A set of foundational verse-texts — the **"Five Treatises of Maitreya"**, including the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* and *Madhyāntavibhāga* — is attributed **sometimes to Maitreya *through* Asaṅga, sometimes to Asaṅga himself**, and the **Chinese and Tibetan traditions disagree about the division**. Modern scholarship is split on whether Maitreyanātha was a historical teacher or a visionary/literary device.

That is a precise instance of Ch. 19 §7's finding, arriving from the Buddhist side: **an attribution can be a claim about the source and authority of a teaching rather than about who held the pen** — and when two receiving traditions divide the same corpus differently, the honest record is the disagreement.

### 5.2 Trisvabhāva — the three natures {#trisvabhava}

*→ concept file: [trisvabhava.md](../../concepts/trisvabhava.md)*

Yogācāra's analytical core: three *svabhāvas* that together constitute **every moment of experience**. Not three kinds of thing — three aspects of one occurrence.

| nature | what it is |
|---|---|
| ***parikalpita*** (imaginary, constructed) | the **superimposed duality** of subject and object — a grasping subject and a substantive object that is never actually there |
| ***paratantra*** (dependent, other-dependent) | the **causal stream of representations** (*vijñaptis*) arising by dependent origination — what genuinely exists, though not as it appears |
| ***pariniṣpanna*** (consummate, perfected) | the paratantra **seen correctly** — the emptiness of the parikalpita overlay *within* the dependent stream |

**Liberation** is recognising parikalpita as unfounded within paratantra; what remains is pariniṣpanna.

**The magician's elephant** (*Trisvabhāvanirdeśa* 27–28) makes it concrete, and it is the clearest single illustration in this chapter. A magician conjures an elephant by mantra. Three things are true at once: the **elephant does not exist** (*parikalpita*); the **visual percept does exist** (*paratantra*); and the **non-existence of the elephant in the percept** is the consummate (*pariniṣpanna*). Note what pariniṣpanna is not — it is not a fourth thing behind the percept, and it is not the percept's disappearance. It is a *fact about* the percept, correctly registered.

**Stanzas 20–21 secure the crucial point:** paratantra and pariniṣpanna are ***not different in characteristic***. They are **the same stream, misread versus correctly seen** — not two ontological levels. Yogācāra is therefore not proposing a hidden real world behind the apparent one, which is precisely the misreading its vocabulary invites.

> ⚠️ **Trisvabhāva ∥ śūnyatā — and NOT equivalent. This is the deepest doctrinal dispute inside Mahāyāna, and §7 exists because of it.** Pariniṣpanna (the emptiness of the imaginary within the dependent) genuinely parallels Madhyamaka śūnyatā. But **Madhyamaka śūnyatā applies to all three natures, including paratantra** — the dependent stream is empty too. **Yogācāra holds paratantra as a genuine substrate.** So: is the stream of representations itself empty of svabhāva, or is it the one thing that is really there? Madhyamaka says the first; Yogācāra, on the substantial reading, says the second. Śāntarakṣita's entire system (§7) is a proposal for how to answer *both* yes.
>
> ⚠️ **Trisvabhāva ∥ Advaita *māyā* (Ch. 11 §3.1) — and NOT equivalent.** Parikalpita as superimposition genuinely parallels *adhyāsa*/māyā, and both traditions distinguish correct from incorrect apprehension of an underlying substrate. **Opposite ontologies underneath:** Yogācāra's substrate is the ***vijñapti*-stream — many momentary representations**; Advaita's is **Brahman — one eternal consciousness**. Momentary plurality against eternal unity, with the same superimposition-structure laid over each.

*Transmission note:* the **Sanskrit of the *Trisvabhāvanirdeśa* is lost.** The corpus works from **Garfield's translation directly from Tibetan** (*Asian Philosophy* 7.2, 1997), with the doctrine independently grounded in canonical sources that predate the treatise — *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* ch. 6 and *Madhyāntavibhāga* chs. 1 and 3. The concept file is candid that both signals remain **inside** the Yogācāra tradition, and that the Madhyamaka critique of the doctrine was not independently verified — which is a real limit on this section and the reason the §7 dispute above is stated rather than assessed.

## 6. The logicians {#logicians}

Ch. 05 §§4, 6, 9 works out what these two men argued. This section is about **what they wrote and what happened to it** — and both texts illustrate transmission problems that shape what can be known.

### 6.1 Dignāga's Pramāṇasamuccaya — a founding text that survives in translation {#pramana-samuccaya}

*→ concept file: [pramana-samuccaya.md](../../concepts/pramana-samuccaya.md)*

The ***Pramāṇasamuccaya*** ("Compendium of Valid Cognition") of **Dignāga** (c. 480–540 CE) is the **founding text of Buddhist epistemology** (*pramāṇavāda*) — the work that "laid the groundwork for the development of deductive logic in India." Dignāga was born near Kāñcīpuram and **trained under Vasubandhu**, which closes a circuit worth noticing: the logic tradition descends directly from the author of the *Vāda-vidhi* (§4). Root verses with **his own auto-commentary** (*Pramāṇasamuccayavṛtti*) — the same kārikā-plus-auto-bhāṣya form Vasubandhu used, now standard.

**Six chapters:** (1) perception; (2–4) inference and argumentation (*anumāna*, *parārthānumāna*); (5) language — the **apoha** theory; (6) fallacies and points of defeat, a direct critique of the Nyāya debate-categories.

The doctrine in one line each (Ch. 05 has the full treatment): **only two pramāṇas**, because there are exactly **two kinds of object** — the *svalakṣaṇa*, the unique bare particular grasped by perception, and the *sāmānyalakṣaṇa*, the constructed universal grasped by inference. **Perception is non-conceptual** — *kalpanāpoḍha*, "free from all conceptual construction, including name and class concepts." Inference runs on the **trairūpya**, the three characteristics of a valid middle term. And **apoha**: "a word indicates an object merely through the exclusion of other objects" — meaning by exclusion, dispensing with the Nyāya's real universals (*jāti*).

> **The transmission ceiling, which the corpus flags as a limit on confidence rather than a piece of trivia:** **the Sanskrit original is largely lost.** The text is reconstructed from **two Tibetan translations** (Vasudhararakṣita's and Kanakavarman's) and from **Jinendrabuddhi's Ṭīkā** — an ongoing scholarly project. So the founding text of Indian Buddhist logic is available to us as a **scholarly reconstruction from translations**, and every claim about its exact wording inherits that. Set this beside Śāntideva's 108 Indian commentaries (§3): survival in Indian Buddhism tracked institutional continuity, and the institutions that carried the logic curriculum did not survive in India.

> ⚠️ **Pramāṇasamuccaya ∥ Nyāyasūtra — and NOT equivalent (Ch. 05, Ch. 16).** Both are foundational *pramāṇa* root-texts building a system on an enumeration of knowledge-sources. But the Pramāṇasamuccaya admits **two** pramāṇas and replaces real universals with apoha-exclusion; the Nyāyasūtra admits **four** (adding *upamāna* and *śabda*) and is a **realist** about universals. Dignāga's text is in large part a **polemic against exactly the Nyāya scheme** — which is why its sixth chapter is an attack on Nyāya's debate categories.

### 6.2 Dharmakīrti and the Pramāṇavārttika {#dharmakirti}

*→ concept files: [dharmakirti.md](../../concepts/dharmakirti.md) · [pramanavarttika.md](../../concepts/pramanavarttika.md)*

**Dharmakīrti** (fl. ~6th–7th c., **Nālandā**) is, with Dignāga, co-founder of the Buddhist **Epistemological School** and its principal weapon in debate against Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and Jain rivals. His **seven treatises** became the core epistemology curriculum of Tibetan monastic education.

**His dates are genuinely contested — and the corpus treats that as a finding rather than a gap**, which is the right call given that the disagreement is 50+ years wide and turns on substantive claims about who influenced whom:

| scholar | dates | basis |
|---|---|---|
| Frauwallner | 600–660 CE | classic reference dating |
| Krasser | mid-6th c. | links with Bhāviveka and Kumārila |
| Balcerowicz | c. 550–610 CE | influence on the Jain **Samantabhadra** |

Balcerowicz's basis is worth flagging for this project specifically: it dates Dharmakīrti by his influence on **Samantabhadra** — the author of the Svayambhūstotra and the Āptamīmāṃsā, both units of this repository's Jain translation reading-room. The chronology of Buddhist logic and the chronology of Jain philosophical poetry are **entangled evidence**, not separate fields.

**The *Pramāṇavārttika*** ("Commentary on Valid Cognition") is "Buddhism's most influential epistemological text" — formally a *vārttika* on Dignāga's Pramāṇasamuccaya, in fact a vast elaboration and defence. **~2,000 verse-stanzas in four chapters:** *pramāṇasiddhi* (establishing valid cognition — and the Buddha as an authority), *pratyakṣa* (perception), *svārthānumāna* (inference-for-oneself, with his own auto-commentary), and *parārthānumāna* (inference-for-others).

*A textual caution the concept file records:* **the four-chapter ordering differs across manuscripts and traditions** — Wikipedia and SEP present them in different sequences. The content is stable; the canonical order is itself a scholarly question, and the corpus leaves it unresolved.

**Three doctrinal moves, each a refinement rather than a departure:**

**(1) *Arthakriyā* — causal efficacy as the criterion of the real.** *"Whatever has causal powers really exists."* Only the momentary *svalakṣaṇa* has *arthakriyā* and is therefore real; universals are "convenient fictions." This is a thoroughgoing nominalism with a **test** attached, which is what makes it more than a slogan.

**(2) Momentariness proved.** The *sattvānumāna*, "inference from existence": whatever exists is momentary, **because nothing produces new effects while itself remaining unchanged.** The corpus calls this *"the logician's version of impermanence"* — anicca (Ch. 12 §3.2) given a formal epistemological proof rather than a meditative warrant.

**(3) Vyāpti grounded in real relations — and this is the load-bearing one.** Dharmakīrti grounds pervasion in **causation or essential identity**, *not* in repeated co-observation. That directly answers **the Cārvāka challenge** (Ch. 17 §5): induction can never secure a universal, so inference is a leap in the dark. Dharmakīrti's reply is that a genuine vyāpti is not an inductive generalisation at all but a **real relation** between the terms. It is the most successful answer to the vyāpti problem that Ch. 05 §6.2 records, and it comes from the school with the fewest pramāṇas.

He likewise **re-grounds apoha causally** — "blue things cause the judgment 'this is blue'" because blue particulars, not red ones, initiate the relevant perceptual-conceptual chain — replacing Dignāga's double-negation account with a causal one. And the apoha argument was **weaponised against Brahmanical universals**, *jāti* — the word that also names caste.

**Two metaphysical registers, held deliberately.** Most of Dharmakīrti's arguments accept external objects (**Sautrāntika** realism); by the perception chapter he holds that "everything cognizable is internal" (**Yogācāra** idealism). The concept file notes the striking part: the shift leaves **the epistemology, nominalism, and causal theory largely unaffected.** An epistemology robust across two incompatible ontologies is a real achievement, and it is also the direct precedent for §7's ladder.

**Dharmottara** (~740–800, Kashmir) closes the lineage: Dignāga → Dharmakīrti → Dharmottara → the Tibetan *tshad ma* tradition. **He is primary-covered in Ch. 05 §9**; the two contributions to register here are his definition of *pramāṇa* as cognition **"capable of verification"** — adding an epistemic-success criterion Dharmakīrti had not foregrounded — and *niścaya-pratyaya*, the ascertaining cognition bridging raw perception and propositional understanding, which **Prajñākaragupta then rejected**. The tradition kept arguing after its founders.

## 7. Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla — the synthesis, and its export {#synthesis}

### 7.1 Śāntarakṣita — three streams into one ladder {#santaraksita}

*→ concept file: [santaraksita.md](../../concepts/santaraksita.md)*

**Śāntarakṣita** (725–788 CE) is the major synthesizer, and what he fused is exactly the three streams this chapter has been tracking separately: Nāgārjuna's **Madhyamaka** (§2), Asaṅga's **Yogācāra** (§5), and the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti **pramāṇa** tradition (§6). Later Tibetan doxography names the result **Yogācāra-Svātantrika-Madhyamaka**.

Two principal works: the ***Madhyamakālaṃkāra*** ("Ornament of the Middle Way," with his own *vṛtti*) and the encyclopedic ***Tattvasaṃgraha*** (~3,600 verses), which analyses and refutes **every major Indian school**, Buddhist and non-Buddhist.

**The defining move — a two-storey ontology graded by the two truths.** He accepts Yogācāra's mind-only (*cittamātra*) and reflexive awareness (*svasaṃvedana*) **only at the level of conventional truth**, then applies Madhyamaka emptiness to consciousness *itself* at the ultimate level. **MA 92 states the program exactly:**

> "By relying on the Mind-Only system, know that external entities do not exist. And by relying on this Middle Way system, know that no self exists at all, **even in that [mind]**."

So mind-only is a ***better* analysis of the conventional** — it dissolves external objects, which no realism manages — but it is not the last word, because the mind is itself empty of intrinsic nature. That is a direct answer to the §5.2 dispute: paratantra is the right conventional story *and* it is empty.

**The "ladder" — a sliding scale of analysis.** The *Madhyamakālaṃkāra* leads the reader up successive standpoints: **Sautrāntika external realism → Yogācāra mind-only (which refutes external objects) → Madhyamaka (which refutes the ultimate reality of mind too).** Each lower view is provisionally adopted, then transcended — "shifting provisionalities." The concept file's caution is the important one: **this is soteriological pedagogy, not eclecticism.** He is not splitting differences; he is claiming that the schools are **rungs**, and that a reader who has not stood on the lower ones cannot make sense of the higher.

**The core argument — *neither-one-nor-many* (*ekānekasvabhāva*).** Anything with intrinsic nature must be either truly singular or truly plural (the alternatives are jointly exhaustive); nothing can be either; therefore nothing has intrinsic nature. **MA 1:** "These entities … have no inherent nature at all because in reality they have neither a singular nor manifold nature, **like a reflection**."

And note the method: this is an **independent (*svatantra*) positive inference** using Dharmakīrti's logical machinery — which is **exactly what Prāsaṅgika rejects** (§2.2). Śāntarakṣita is the great Svātantrika, and his synthesis is only available to someone who thinks Bhāvaviveka won that argument. He also defends Dignāga's apoha against **Kumārila** (Ch. 17 §3) by distinguishing three negation-types — one non-implicative, two implicative — so the synthesis fights on the Mīmāṃsā front too.

> ⚠️ **Śāntarakṣita is not a Yogācārin, despite the school-label.** He accepts mind-only **only conventionally**; pure Yogācāra holds the *vijñapti*-stream as **ultimately** real. They split precisely at the ultimate level, and the corpus draws the non-equivalence so the label does not mislead.
>
> ⚠️ **Ekānekasvabhāva ≠ Jain anekāntavāda (Ch. 02) — a shared-vocabulary trap worth its own line.** Both deploy the *eka*/*aneka* ("one"/"many") pair. **Opposite conclusions:** *ekānekasvabhāva* is a **destructive** argument concluding that **nothing has intrinsic nature**; *anekāntavāda* is a **constructive** claim that **many partial standpoints are jointly true** of a real, multi-faceted object. "Neither one nor many" is not "non-one-sidedness," and the shared *aneka*-vocabulary masks a flat disagreement about whether anything has determinate nature at all.

### 7.2 Kamalaśīla and the Samye debate {#kamalasila}

*→ concept file: [kamalasila.md](../../concepts/kamalasila.md)*

**Kamalaśīla** (c. 740–795 CE), a Nālandā monk-scholar and **Śāntarakṣita's direct student**, continued and defended the same Yogācāra-Svātantrika-Madhyamaka line. His works both inherit and elaborate his teacher's: the three ***Bhāvanākrama*** ("Stages of Meditation"), enormously influential in Tibet; the ***Madhyamakāloka***, his Madhyamaka magnum opus; and the ***Tattvasaṃgrahapañjikā***, his commentary on Śāntarakṣita's own *Tattvasaṃgraha*.

**The Samye (Lhasa) debate, c. 792–794 CE**, hosted by the Tibetan king **Trisong Detsen**, set **Kamalaśīla the gradualist** against **Moheyan (Heshang Mohoyen), the Chinese Chan "subitist."**

Kamalaśīla's thesis: practitioners must **gradually cultivate the causes and conditions** that make awakening possible. Enlightenment comes through the staged cultivation of **insight (*vipaśyanā*) united with calm-abiding (*śamatha*)**, of **wisdom (*prajñā*) together with method (*upāya* — compassion, bodhicitta)**, and of the pāramitās and merit — *not* through a single non-conceptual leap of "no-thought," as Moheyan held. The *Bhāvanākrama* is the textual form of that refutation.

> **The outcome is itself contested, and the corpus reports rather than asserts it.** **Tibetan sources report Kamalaśīla's victory; Chinese sources claim Moheyan won.** The event is said to have steered Tibetan Buddhism decisively toward the late-Indian gradualist model — which is a claim about consequences that the Tibetan-source version of the outcome would predict, so the two are not independent evidence. What is not in doubt is the *result on the ground*: Tibetan Buddhism inherited the Indian scholastic gradualist curriculum, and it is the curriculum this entire chapter describes.

> ⚠️ **Two gradualist parallels the corpus draws and immediately qualifies:**
> **Bhāvanākrama ∥ Jain guṇasthāna (Ch. 03 §9)** — both are staged ladders to liberation. **Incompatible ontologies and goals:** Kamalaśīla cultivates *insight into emptiness* plus compassion **in a being with no permanent self**; the guṇasthāna ascent is the progressive **karma-shedding of an eternal individual soul**.
> **Bhāvanākrama ∥ Patañjala Yoga (Ch. 15)** — both are explicitly staged meditative paths culminating in a liberative absorption (śamatha+vipaśyanā vs the eight *aṅgas*). **Opposite verdicts on whether there is a self to liberate:** the Bhāvanākrama aims at the realisation of emptiness and no-self; Yoga aims at *kaivalya*, the **isolation of an eternal puruṣa**, under Īśvara.

## 8. Where the argument was going — apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa {#apratisthita}

*→ concept file: [nirvana-mahayana.md](../../concepts/nirvana-mahayana.md)*

**Apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa** ("non-abiding" or "non-localized" cessation) is the Mahāyāna conception of Buddhahood, and it is the doctrinal cash-value of MMK 25.19 (§2.1). A liberation that is **neither static withdrawal nor continued saṃsāric bondage**: the Buddha abides in neither extreme, because once the saṃsāra/nirvāṇa distinction dissolves there is no third place to go and no reason to leave.

| | Theravāda / "Hīnayāna" | Mahāyāna |
|---|---|---|
| goal | **arhat** — personal liberation | **Buddhahood**, for the benefit of all |
| state at parinirvāṇa | nirvāṇa **without residue** (*anupādā-parinibbāna*); the aggregates cease entirely | **apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa**; untainted aggregates persist (Form Bodies + Wisdom Dharmakāya) |
| relation to beings | ends; no further return | Buddhas **manifest** (*nirmāṇakāya*, *saṃbhogakāya*) and return to saṃsāra to help |

**The Mahāyāna critique of the arhat ideal turns on this**: a being who has completed the path but declines final withdrawal **out of compassion** is *more* advanced, not less complete. Arhatship is recast as an **intermediate** stage, after which arhats "will go on to the bodhisattva path" (per the *Abhisamayālaṃkāra*). Ch. 12 §5 gives the doctrinal argument; what this chapter adds is that the position was **available** only after the MMK collapsed the saṃsāra/nirvāṇa boundary.

**The Diamond Sūtra formula**, quoted in the Platform Sūtra, states the practical version: *"Responding to the non-abiding, yet generating the mind"* (應無所住，而生其心) — generating bodhicitta **without fixing on any object, including liberation itself.**

> ⚠️ **Apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa ≠ Theravāda nibbāna (Ch. 12 §2.4).** Both are the final liberated state of their systems, and the shared word conceals the difference. **Theravāda nibbāna is the permanent cessation of the five aggregates. Mahāyāna apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa preserves untainted aggregates and enables return to saṃsāra.** Buswell and Lopez's formulation is the precise one: it "enables them to freely return to samsara to help sentient beings, **while still remaining in nirvāṇa and being a buddha**." And it is emphatically **not** re-entanglement — the non-abidance *is* the freedom from fixing on either pole.

## 9. Six centuries, in order {#chronology}

| dates | figure | what changed |
|---|---|---|
| ~150–250 | **Nāgārjuna** | MMK: everything is empty of svabhāva; prasaṅga; **no thesis of one's own** |
| ~300–370 | **Asaṅga** | Yogācāra's systematic framework; ālaya-vijñāna; Abhidharma rebuilt on Mahāyāna foundations |
| ~380–415 | **Vasubandhu** | the Abhidharma summa **and** its Sautrāntika critique; then the Yogācāra treatises and trisvabhāva; the *Vāda-vidhi* |
| c. 480–540 | **Dignāga** (student of Vasubandhu) | Pramāṇasamuccaya: **two pramāṇas**, non-conceptual perception, apoha, trairūpya |
| c. 500–578 | **Bhāvaviveka** | autonomous inference (*svatantra*) is required — the Svātantrika turn |
| (c. 6th c.) | **Buddhapālita**, then **Candrakīrti** | bare prasaṅga suffices; the *Prasannapadā* and the **Prāsaṅgika** position |
| c. 550–660 (**contested**) | **Dharmakīrti** | *arthakriyā* as the test of the real; momentariness proved; **vyāpti grounded in real relations** |
| ~685–763 | **Śāntideva** | the BCA: Madhyamaka as a **bodhisattva ethics**; metaphysics doing emotional work |
| 725–788 | **Śāntarakṣita** | the **ladder**: Sautrāntika → Yogācāra → Madhyamaka, graded by the two truths |
| c. 740–795 | **Kamalaśīla** | the *Bhāvanākrama*; the **gradualist** case, argued at Samye c. 792–794 |
| ~740–800 | **Dharmottara** | *niścaya-pratyaya*; pramāṇa as **verification-capable**; the Kashmiri school; export to Tibet |

**Three things the sequence shows that the doxographic two-wing picture (§1) hides.**

**First: the school labels lag the arguments.** "Prāsaṅgika" and "Svātantrika" are **later Tibetan doxographic categories** applied retrospectively to a dispute among Indians who did not use them. So is "Yogācāra-Svātantrika-Madhyamaka." The concept files say so explicitly, and this chapter repeats it: the names are a **Tibetan filing system** for an Indian argument, and reading the Indian figures as members of pre-existing schools inverts the actual order of events.

**Second: the running exchange is Buddhist-internal.** Nāgārjuna's principal target is **Abhidharma** realism; Vasubandhu's *bhāṣya* attacks **his own root text's** Vaibhāṣika; the Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika split is Madhyamaka against Madhyamaka; Śāntarakṣita's ladder adjudicates Yogācāra against Madhyamaka; Prajñākaragupta rejects Dharmottara. The Brahmanical opponents are real and constant — Nyāya realism, Kumārila's Mīmāṃsā, the Sāṃkhya debate that cost Vasubandhu's teacher a match (his lost *Paramārthasaptati* was a reply to Vindhyavāsin) — but **the sharpest arguments are between Buddhists.**

**Third: the direction of travel is from negation to construction and back.** Nāgārjuna refuses to build; Asaṅga and Vasubandhu build; Dignāga and Dharmakīrti give the building a logic; and Śāntarakṣita re-applies the original negation **to the whole structure** while keeping it as a conventional ladder. That is not drift. It is a tradition working out how much can be said after you have proved that nothing can be asserted — and the answer it reaches, six centuries in, is *"a great deal, provisionally, arranged in order of decreasing error."*

## 10. Cross-tradition: this chapter's flags in one place {#flags}

| flag | why the resemblance is tempting | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **MMK ≠ Abhidharma** | both systematically decompose experience into categories | Abhidharma grants its ultimate dharmas *svabhāva*; the MMK exists to empty them |
| **Prasaṅga ≠ Nyāya *tarka*** | identical reductio form | *tarka* confirms a surviving positive thesis; prasaṅga confirms none |
| **Prasaṅga ≠ Nyāya *anumāna*** | both are formal argument | prasaṅga has no *pakṣa* and grants premises only *ad hominem* |
| **Prasaṅga ≠ Pyrrhonism** | both refuse to assert any thesis | prasaṅga serves a soteriology; Pyrrhonist *epochē* is content-free therapy for *ataraxia* |
| **Trisvabhāva ≠ śūnyatā** | pariniṣpanna genuinely parallels emptiness | Madhyamaka empties **paratantra too**; Yogācāra keeps it as substrate — the deepest Mahāyāna dispute |
| **Trisvabhāva ≠ Advaita *māyā*** | superimposition on a substrate, in both | substrate is a **momentary vijñapti-stream** vs **one eternal Brahman** |
| **Śāntarakṣita ≠ Yogācāra** | the school-label says "Yogācāra-…" | he accepts mind-only **only conventionally**; pure Yogācāra makes it ultimate |
| **Ekānekasvabhāva ≠ anekāntavāda** | shared *eka*/*aneka* vocabulary | destructive "nothing has intrinsic nature" vs constructive "many standpoints are jointly true" |
| **Bhāvanākrama ≠ guṇasthāna** | both are staged ladders to liberation | insight into no-self vs karma-shedding by an **eternal jīva** |
| **Bhāvanākrama ≠ Patañjala Yoga** | both stage meditation toward absorption | emptiness/no-self vs *kaivalya*, the isolation of an **eternal puruṣa** |
| **Apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa ≠ Theravāda nibbāna** | both are the final liberated state | aggregates **cease** vs untainted aggregates **persist**, enabling return |
| **Pramāṇasamuccaya ≠ Nyāyasūtra** | both are pramāṇa root-texts | two pramāṇas + apoha nominalism vs four + realism about universals |
| **Abhidharmakośa ∥ Tattvārtha Sūtra** *(a parallel, not a flag)* | — | contemporary verse-compendia compressing a whole tradition into root-text-plus-commentary; the parallel **holds** |
