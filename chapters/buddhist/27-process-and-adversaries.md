## How to use this chapter

Chapter 12 laid out the Buddhist family doctrine-by-doctrine, and Chapter 05 laid out the pramāṇa debate school-by-school. This chapter takes four nodes that fell between them and finds that three of the four are **the same commitment worked out at three different depths**:

| depth | the claim | concept |
|---|---|---|
| **the person** | there is no self behind experience; the "person" *is* a conditioned psycho-physical process | **nāmarūpa** (§2) |
| **the object** | there are no universals out there; only momentary particulars are real, and perception touches only those | **pratyakṣa** (§3) |
| **the tradition** | and here is the single Sanskrit text-plus-commentary pair through which that epistemology survived at all | **Nyāyabindu / Dharmottara's ṭīkā** (§5) |

Then two adversaries, each instructive in a different way. **Pratyabhijñā**, the Kashmiri Śaiva school that inverted every one of those claims while sharing a city and a technical vocabulary with the Buddhist logicians (§6). The chapter's methodological payoff is §8, and it is one this corpus has now hit three times from three directions: **Gosāla survives only in the writings of schools that despised him.** Ch 25 found the same for Vindhyavāsin, Ch 24 for the Chaldean Oracles. Three traditions, one structural problem.

## 1. One commitment, three depths {#commitment}

Buddhist thought has a signature move, and this chapter is a chance to watch it operate at three scales rather than state it once abstractly.

The move is **replacing a thing with a process**. Where another system posits an entity that persists and undergoes change, Buddhism substitutes a sequence of conditioned events with nothing underneath. *Anattā* (no-self) is the most famous instance, but it is an instance, not the whole thesis — and the interesting fact is how far the same move travels.

- Applied to the **person**, it yields nāmarūpa: not a soul with a body but a psycho-physical process, conditioned and conditioning (§2).
- Applied to the **object of knowledge**, it yields *svalakṣaṇa*: not enduring things with shareable properties but momentary particulars, with universals demoted to mind-imposed constructs (§3).
- Applied to **cognition itself**, it yields a perception that must be stripped of every concept to count as perception at all (§3.4).

Watch for the pattern in each section: something that looked like a *thing* is re-described as a *happening*, and then whatever depended on the thing's persistence has to be rebuilt.

And keep one question live, because §6 and §7 both turn on it: **what does a rival have to hold instead?** The Kashmiri Śaivas answer with a self-luminous continuous consciousness (§6). The Ājīvikas answer with a fixed cosmic schedule (§7). Neither is a variant of the Buddhist view; both are what you get when you refuse the substitution at a different point.

## 2. Nāmarūpa — the person as a conditioned process {#namarupa}

*→ concept file: [namarupa.md](../../concepts/namarupa.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium** (both signals secondary; DN 15 unfetched)*

***Nāmarūpa*** — "name-and-form" — is **link 4 in the twelve-*nidāna* chain of *paṭiccasamuppāda*** (Ch 12): the psycho-physical organism seen as a conditioned process. It arises in dependence on *vijñāna* (consciousness, link 3) and itself conditions the *ṣaḍāyatana* (the six sense-bases, link 5):

> … → **vijñāna** (3) → **nāmarūpa** (4) → **ṣaḍāyatana** (5) → …

Its two halves:

| component | what it covers |
|---|---|
| ***nāma*** ("name" / mental) | feeling (*vedanā*), perception (*saṃjñā*), volition (*cetanā*), contact (*sparśa*), attention (*manasikāra*) — the four formless *khandhas* minus *vijñāna* |
| ***rūpa*** ("form" / physical) | the four material elements (earth, water, fire, wind) and derived material forms |

Together they constitute **the entire "person," seen as a conditioned interdependent process** — and this is what *vijñāna* is said to "descend into" at conception to produce a new life-stream.

Note immediately what has been done to the mental/physical distinction. *Nāma* is not "mind" as a substance opposed to matter; it is a **list of functions** — feeling, perceiving, willing, contacting, attending. The person is not soul-plus-body. It is five mental operations plus four elements, running.

### 2.1 The loop at DN 15 — why the "chain" is not simply a chain

In the *Mahānidāna Sutta* (Dīgha Nikāya 15), the Buddha describes something the linear diagram cannot show: **mutual conditionality.** Vijñāna conditions nāmarūpa — *and* nāmarūpa conditions vijñāna. Neither is prior. The image the tradition gives is precise and worth memorising:

> **Two sheaves of reeds leaning against each other.** Remove one and the other falls.

This matters more than it may look. The twelve links are usually drawn as an arrow diagram, which invites the reading that link 3 causes link 4 the way a first domino topples a second. DN 15 blocks that reading at exactly the point where it would do most damage — the joint between consciousness and the organism. **Consciousness does not produce the psycho-physical process, and the process does not produce consciousness.** They stand up only against each other.

And that is the structural reason no self is needed here. A linear chain invites the question "what started it?", which invites a first term, which is a short walk from a substrate. A mutually-supporting pair does not: there is nothing for a self to be *underneath*, because neither member is more basic than the other.

### 2.2 Two analyses of one apparent person

Nāmarūpa is often described as a synonym for the five *khandhas*, and it is more useful to say exactly how they relate:

| | **five khandhas** | **nāmarūpa** |
|---|---|---|
| what kind of analysis | **structural** — what a person is composed of | **causal-process** — how that composition arises and conditions |
| where it appears | the standard analysis of "person" | link 4 of the twelve *nidāna*s |
| the mapping | *rūpa* = khandha 1; *nāma* = khandhas 2–5 **minus vijñāna**, which is link 3 | — |

**The same apparent person, from two angles.** The khandha analysis takes it apart; the nidāna analysis puts the parts in a causal sequence. And the arithmetic detail — vijñāna pulled out of *nāma* and given its own link — is exactly what makes the DN 15 loop expressible: you cannot state the mutual conditioning of consciousness and organism if consciousness is already inside the organism's definition.

### 2.3 The flag the corpus stores

> ⚠️ ***Nāma* is not *jīva*.** Both are "the mental side of a person" and both stand opposite something physical, which makes the equation almost automatic. But **nāma is the mental component of a conditioned process** — a set of functions with nothing behind them — while the Jain **jīva is an eternal individual substance** (Ch 03, Ch 09), a real *dravya* that changes modes (*paryāya*) while persisting. Sliding one onto the other **imports substance-realism into a process-analysis**, which is precisely the thing the analysis exists to refuse.

Set the two side by side and the disagreement is total: Jainism analyses the person as *jīva* + *pudgala*, a genuine substance entangled with matter (Ch 26); Buddhism analyses it as nāmarūpa, a process with no substrate. Both are anti-materialist, both are soteriologies of release, and they disagree about whether there is anything to release.

## 3. Pratyakṣa — perception with the concepts taken out {#pratyaksha}

*→ concept file: [pratyaksha-buddhist.md](../../concepts/pratyaksha-buddhist.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium** (the Sanskrit definitions are quoted via a secondary essay, not from critical editions)*

Now the same commitment applied to knowledge. In the **Dignāga–Dharmakīrti** tradition (Ch 05, Ch 20), *pratyakṣa* — "before the eyes" (*prati* + *akṣa*) — is the first and more basic of the **two** pramāṇas, and it is defined by exclusion.

**Dignāga**, *Pramāṇasamuccaya* 3 (~5th c.):

> *pratyakṣam kalpanāpoḍham nāmajātyādyasaṃyutam*
> "Perception is free from conceptual construction (*kalpanāpoḍha*) — not associated with names, class-characters, or generic features."

**Dharmakīrti**, *Nyāyabindu* 1.4 (~7th c.), adding one word:

> *tatra kalpanāpoḍham abhrāntaṃ pratyakṣam*
> "Perception is [cognition that is] free from conceptual construction **and non-erroneous** (*abhrānta*)."

### 3.1 Why *abhrānta* had to be added

Dignāga's definition has a hole, and Dharmakīrti's addition patches it with a nice example. Stand on a moving ship and look at the shore: the trees appear to move. That cognition is **entirely free of conceptual construction** — you are not inferring anything, not applying a class-name, just seeing — and it is **wrong**. Non-conceptuality alone therefore cannot be sufficient for valid perception, since it admits cognitions that misrepresent.

So both conditions are necessary: **nirvikalpaka *and* abhrānta**. Note what kind of repair this is. Dharmakīrti does not weaken the non-conceptuality requirement; he adds a second, independent one. The system's austerity is preserved and a second gate is installed beside the first.

### 3.2 The four types

*Nyāyabindu* 1.7 — *taccaturvidham*, "and it is fourfold":

| type | Sanskrit | object |
|---|---|---|
| sense-perception | *indriya-pratyakṣa* | external *svalakṣaṇa*s, via the sense-faculties |
| mental perception | *mānasa-pratyakṣa* | follows immediately on a sense-cognition; the same momentary object |
| self-cognition | ***svasaṃvedana*** | consciousness apprehending its own occurrence, reflexively |
| yogic apprehension | *yogijñāna* | direct intuition of (e.g.) the Four Noble Truths, after meditative cultivation; clear and trustworthy |

> **All four remain *nirvikalpaka*.** The requirement is **not** relaxed for yogic perception — which is a striking piece of discipline. The obvious move for a religious epistemology would be to let the meditative adept's cognition carry content that ordinary perception cannot. Dharmakīrti does not make it: the yogin's apprehension is *more vivid and more reliable*, not *more conceptual*.

Two entries deserve marking for later. **Svasaṃvedana** — reflexive self-awareness — is the item the Śaivas will pick up and turn around (§6.2). And **yogijñāna** is where the Jain comparison breaks (§4).

### 3.3 Why only the non-conceptual counts — the ontology underneath

The definition looks arbitrarily strict until you see what it rests on.

The only ultimately real (*paramārthasat*) object is the ***svalakṣaṇa***: the **unique momentary particular**. Svalakṣaṇas are

- **causally efficacious** (*arthakriyāśakti*) — they *do* something, which is Dharmakīrti's criterion of the real;
- **momentary** — they last an instant;
- **inexpressible** — no word attaches to them, because words attach to types and a svalakṣaṇa is not a type.

By contrast, *sāmānyalakṣaṇa*s — general characteristics, universals — are **mind-imposed constructs**, not features of objects. This is the *apoha* theory (Ch 05): a word like "cow" does not pick out a real universal cowhood but operates by **exclusion** — marking off not-non-cows.

Now the definition follows. To perceive a svalakṣaṇa is to contact reality as it is. The moment the mind adds conceptual framing — *"this is fire," "this is one of those"* — it has applied a construct, which means it has left the momentary particular and moved to the constructed general. And that move is *inference*, not perception.

> ⚠️ **Conceptual perception is not a degraded form of perception. It is a different cognitive act.** This is the point most often softened in summaries, and softening it destroys the position. Dignāga is not saying that seeing-as-a-cow is a blurry or second-rate perceiving. He is saying it belongs to the *other* pramāṇa. The two-pramāṇa system is exhaustive precisely because there are two kinds of real object — particular and constructed-general — and one cognition for each.

Notice the §1 pattern completing itself: the enduring *thing* with shareable properties has been replaced by a momentary *event* plus a construct the mind supplies, and the theory of perception has been rebuilt around the replacement.

## 4. Three traditions, one word {#three-pratyaksha}

*Pratyakṣa* is used by Buddhists, Naiyāyikas, and Jains alike, and it does not mean the same thing in any two of them. The corpus stores this as a set of typed non-equivalences; here is the whole picture at once.

| | **Buddhist** (Dignāga/Dharmakīrti) | **Nyāya** (Ch 16) | **Jain** (Ch 07) |
|---|---|---|---|
| is *savikalpaka* (conceptual) perception valid pratyakṣa? | **No** — excluded by definition | **Yes** — a legitimate subtype | not divided this way |
| object of perception | ***svalakṣaṇa*** — momentary, non-linguistic particular | a particular **with** universal features (realism) | *kevala-jñāna* perceives all substances in all modes |
| is omniscience a kind of pratyakṣa? | *yogijñāna* approaches it; **full omniscience is not claimed** | **No** | **Yes** — *kevala-jñāna* is perfect pratyakṣa |
| role of the senses | sense-perception is the most basic type | primary, the paradigm case | **sensory cognition is *parokṣa*** — indirect! |
| are universals real? | **No** — nominalism, *apoha* | **Yes** — realism | **Yes** — realism about *sāmānya* |

Two of these rows are worth stopping on.

**The Nyāya break is ontological, not terminological.** Nyāya holds that *savikalpaka-pratyakṣa* — determinate, conceptual perception, "this is a cow" — is fully valid perception. Dignāga denies it. That looks like a boundary dispute about how to use a word, and it is not: it is **realism about universals against nominalism**. If cowhood is a real feature that the cow really has, then perceiving the cow *as* a cow is perceiving something that is there, and Nyāya is right. If cowhood is a construct assembled by exclusion, then that same cognition has added something not in the object, and Dignāga is right. The disagreement about perception *is* the disagreement about universals.

**The Jain inversion is almost total.** In Jain epistemology, *pratyakṣa* means **unmediated** — knowledge the soul has directly, without instruments — which *includes* clairvoyance, telepathy, and omniscience. And since the senses are instruments, **sensory cognition is *parokṣa*, indirect.** So the same word marks the *sensory floor* of the Buddhist hierarchy and the *supersensory ceiling* of the Jain one. A reader who carries the Buddhist sense of *pratyakṣa* into a Jain text will get the epistemology exactly upside down.

> ⚠️ **Shared technical vocabulary between rival schools is the most dangerous kind of shared vocabulary**, because it looks like agreement about a subject-matter and is often disagreement so deep that the parties cannot even fix a common referent. This corpus's honesty layer exists mostly for cases like this one — three `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent` edges around a single word.

## 5. The *Nyāyabindu* and its ṭīkā — how the tradition survived {#nyayabindu}

*→ concept file: [dharmottara-nyayabindu.md](../../concepts/dharmottara-nyayabindu.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium** (neither text independently fetched)*

The ***Nyāyabindu*** ("Drop of Logic," c. 635–650 CE) is **Dharmakīrti's concise teaching manual** — a brief, sūtra-like statement of his epistemology in three chapters, built as an accessible entry to the much larger *Pramāṇavārttika*:

| chapter | topic | content |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Pratyakṣa** | perception | the four types; *kalpanāpoḍham abhrāntam* (NB 1.4) — §3 above |
| **II. Svārthānumāna** | inference **for oneself** | the qualities of the middle term (*hetu*); three types of valid reason; *trairūpya* |
| **III. Parārthānumāna** | inference **for others** | the three-member syllogism; *hetvābhāsa* (fallacies of the middle term); *pakṣābhāsa* |

The NB/PV relation is a nice instance of a general pattern: the *Pramāṇavārttika* is the magnum opus (~2000 verses, four chapters); the *Nyāyabindu* covers the same ground in a fraction of the length. **The NB was the preferred teaching text; the PV supplied the depth.** They are paired, not rival, and the corpus stores `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent: pramanavarttika` to keep the compact manual from being cited as if it were the full argument.

### 5.1 Dharmottara's ṭīkā, and three things in it

**Dharmottara** (c. 740–800 CE) wrote the *Nyāyabinduṭīkā*, **the only surviving Sanskrit commentary** on the NB. Three contributions:

**A working definition of *pramāṇa*.** Valid cognition is that which is **"capable of verification"** — cognition that does not disagree with the objective reality it represents. Note how pragmatic that is: not "corresponds to" in the abstract, but *checkable*, which fits a system whose criterion of the real is causal efficacy (§3.3).

***Niścaya-pratyaya*** — ascertaining cognition — and the problem it solves. §3 left a genuine gap: if perception is strictly non-conceptual and momentary, **how does it ever guide action?** You cannot act on an inexpressible instant. Dharmottara inserts a three-stage model:

> **raw perception → ascertaining cognition (*niścaya*) → action**

The middle stage is where a bare non-conceptual contact becomes something usable. This is a real piece of philosophical repair — and the corpus records that it was **subsequently challenged by Prajñākaragupta**, so it is a move in an ongoing argument, not a settled fix.

**A Yogācāra re-reading.** Dharmottara interprets Dharmakīrti through a **mind-only** lens: the objects of cognition are ultimately internal, which makes external-object realism (the Sautrāntika reading) a *conventional* rather than an *ultimate* claim. So the tradition's principal surviving Sanskrit commentary is also an interpretive intervention — the reader who comes to Dharmakīrti through Dharmottara comes through a Yogācāra door. Chapter 25 §10 made the general version of this point; here is a concrete case of a commentary shaping the text it transmits.

### 5.2 The text-critical fact that shaped a millennium of reception

> The NB survives in Sanskrit — unlike most of Dharmottara's other works, which survive only in Tibetan — and **the ṭīkā survives in Sanskrit too.** That makes the NB/ṭīkā pair **the best-preserved complete text-plus-commentary unit of the entire *hetu-vidyā* tradition in Sanskrit.**

And that accident of survival is why the pair holds its privileged position in Kashmiri intellectual culture and in Tibetan monastic curricula. Note the direction of causation, which is easy to get backwards: it is not only that the NB was important and therefore preserved, but that it was **preserved and therefore available to be central**. What a later tradition can build on is constrained by what happens to have come through.

This is Ch 24 §10's Simplicius finding in an Indian register. There, two-thirds of the verbatim Presocratic fragments survive because one late commentator quoted at length. Here, an entire epistemological tradition is read in Sanskrit through the one manual-plus-commentary that made it. **Transmission is not a neutral pipe.** It selects, and the selection shapes what the tradition afterwards looks like.

## 6. The adversary in the same city — Pratyabhijñā {#pratyabhijna}

The Dignāga–Dharmakīrti school flourished in medieval Kashmir at the same time as the Śaiva **Pratyabhijñā** ("Recognition") school — **Utpaladeva** (c. 925–975) and **Abhinavagupta** (c. 975–1025) (Ch 18). They were not neighbours who ignored each other. They were **explicit philosophical adversaries**, and Abhinavagupta's *Īśvarapratyabhijñāvivṛtivimarśinī* engages Buddhist epistemology in direct polemic.

The disagreement is total, and lines up point-for-point against §§2–3:

| issue | **Buddhist** (NB / Dharmottara) | **Kashmir Śaivism** (Pratyabhijñā) |
|---|---|---|
| **self** | no enduring self; cognition is a momentary series of svalakṣaṇas | the self is **self-luminous, continuous, universal Śiva-consciousness (*cit*)**; "recognition" *presupposes* a self that re-recognises |
| **perception** | non-conceptual, momentary, pre-linguistic | the perceiving subject is **already self-aware** |
| **universals** | nominal constructs (*apoha*) | **real** — grounded in Śiva's creative freedom (*svātantrya*) |
| **consciousness** | multiple, momentary, causally linked; no over-arching mind | **one** self-luminous Śiva-consciousness; multiplicity apparent, unity real |

### 6.1 The name is the argument

*Pratyabhijñā* means **recognition** — and recognition is a well-chosen banner, because it is exactly the cognitive act the Buddhist analysis has the most trouble with. To *re*-cognise is to encounter something now and identify it as what was encountered before. That requires (i) something persisting to be re-identified, and (ii) someone persisting to do the re-identifying. A metaphysics of momentary particulars apprehended by a momentary series supplies neither.

So the Śaiva school did not choose an arbitrary label. It named itself after the datum it took to refute its rivals.

### 6.2 Co-optation — using an opponent's tools against him

The sharper move is that **the Śaivas explicitly took up Dharmakīrti's own *apoha* theory and his *svasaṃvedana* as entry points** — deploying Dharmakīrti's arguments in support of positions Dharmakīrti would reject.

*Svasaṃvedana* is the perfect vehicle for it. It is the Buddhists' own third type of perception (§3.2): consciousness apprehending its own occurrence, reflexively. The Buddhist means it as a momentary event self-illuminating in an instant. The Śaiva reads the same reflexive self-luminosity as **evidence of continuous self-aware consciousness** — if awareness is intrinsically self-revealing, why should it wink out between instants? The datum is granted by both sides; only its extension in time is in dispute.

> ⚠️ **This section is a record of an argument, not a structural parallel.** The corpus stores `structurally-parallel-to: shaivism` on the NB node with a note naming it the tradition's **primary Kashmiri philosophical adversary** — the parallelism is that two developed systems addressed the same questions with the same technical vocabulary in the same place. It is emphatically **not** a claim that the doctrines converge. On every row of the table they are opposed, and the co-optation of *svasaṃvedana* is a hostile act, not a shared insight.

That distinction matters for how this corpus reads *any* pair of neighbouring systems. Shared vocabulary plus shared problems plus close contact produces *engagement*, and engagement can be total disagreement conducted in a common language. Reading closeness as convergence is the §0 error in its social-historical form.

## 7. Makkhali Gosāla — the same words, no efficacy {#gosala}

*→ concept file: [makkhali-gosala.md](../../concepts/makkhali-gosala.md) — `converged`, confidence **medium** (see §8 for why it is not higher)*

**Makkhali Gosāla** (6th–5th c. BCE; A. L. Basham dates his death to **484 BCE**) founded and led the **Ājīvika** school — one of the heterodox (*nāstika*) **śramaṇa** movements that flourished alongside early Buddhism and Jainism in the Gangetic plain. He is the historical anchor of an otherwise sparsely-documented tradition, and the principal exponent of ***niyati-vāda***.

### 7.1 Niyati — determinism without remainder

Gosāla held that **everything that has happened, is happening, or will happen is entirely preordained** by *niyati*, the fixed inner order of things. Not "mostly determined," not "determined except for the will." Everything.

And the claim's real bite is where it lands:

> **Liberation itself is preordained.** Every soul transmigrates through a fixed, immense number of rebirths and is freed **on a predetermined schedule, regardless of conduct or effort.** Ascetic practice, ethics, and striving change nothing.

Take that seriously for a moment before evaluating it. This is not a licence for indulgence — the Ājīvikas were, by all accounts, ascetics — nor is it despair. It is the claim that the cosmos has an order so complete that the appearance of efficacy in one's own striving is an artefact of standing inside the process. Every soul *does* reach liberation. It simply does not reach it *because of* anything it did.

### 7.2 Keeping the word, discarding the mechanism

The relation to its neighbours is precise, and it is the reason this node earns a place in this chapter:

> **Gosāla retains the *word* karma and strips it of all efficacy.**

Which makes his position the exact inverse of the Jain one. Chapter 26 laid out Jain karma as a closed physical loop that ascetic effort intervenes in at two points — *saṃvara* stopping the influx, *nirjarā* burning off what is already bonded. Gosāla keeps the vocabulary of that world and removes the intervention. The corpus accordingly stores `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent: karma` and `often-conflated-with-NOT-equivalent: moksha` on his node: superficially the same two concepts, in a system where neither does what its name implies.

Three śramaṇa options, one milieu:

| | **Jain** (Ch 03, Ch 26) | **Buddhist** (Ch 12) | **Ājīvika** (here) |
|---|---|---|---|
| what karma is | **fine matter** bonded to the jīva | **volitional action** conditioning future experience | a word retained; **no causal power** |
| is intention required? | **no** — activity alone attracts matter | **yes** — intention is definitional | the question does not arise |
| does effort affect liberation? | **yes** — it *is* the mechanism | **yes** — the path is the method | **no** — liberation is scheduled |
| what liberation is | the jīva disentangled from matter | the ending of craving and conditioned arising | arrival at the end of a fixed series of rebirths |

> ⚠️ **"All three śramaṇa schools taught karma and rebirth" is true and nearly empty.** They share the vocabulary and the general picture of transmigration, and they disagree about the one thing that determines what a religion *is for*: whether anything you do matters. Gosāla's is the case that shows how much of a doctrine can be preserved verbatim while its entire function is removed.

### 7.3 The Mahāvīra connection

Per the Jain *Bhagavatī Sūtra* (*Viyāhapannatti*), Gosāla was for about **six years the companion** — and, by the Jain account, the **disciple** — of **Mahāvīra**, before a bitter doctrinal split. The corpus stores the inbound edge from `mahavira`: the two shared the same śramaṇa milieu before parting.

Read that alongside §7.2 and something interesting appears: the *closest* doctrinal relations in this milieu are also the most hostile. Gosāla and Mahāvīra shared a practice, a vocabulary, and years of company, and ended as the sharpest possible opponents on the efficacy of exactly the practice they shared. Compare §6: the Buddhist logicians and the Pratyabhijñā Śaivas shared a city, a vocabulary, and a problem-set, and were adversaries. **Proximity generates polemic, not agreement** — a pattern this corpus should expect rather than be surprised by.

Note also the framing to be careful with: the "disciple" claim is *by Jain account*, in a Jain text, about a rival's founder. §8.

## 8. Figures who survive only in their opponents' books {#hostile}

Here is the finding, and it is now the third instance the corpus has recorded from three unrelated traditions.

> **There is no surviving Ājīvika scripture.** Everything known about Makkhali Gosāla comes from the Jain *Bhagavatī Sūtra* and the Buddhist *Sāmaññaphala Sutta* (Dīgha Nikāya 2) with Buddhaghosa's commentary — **the polemics of two rival schools.**

And the corpus's independence check makes it worse rather than better. The node's two web signals are **the same encyclopedia's linked articles** (the "Makkhali Gosala" and "Ajivika" pages), and *both ultimately derive from the same two hostile texts.* So the confidence is held at **medium** — not because the reports are inconsistent, but because consistency among descendants of a single hostile source is not confirmation. This is the same test that capped Ch 25's `vyasa-yogabhasya` at low and Ch 26's `karma-vargana` at low.

Three instances now:

| case | who survives | through whom | the interest of the channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chaldean Oracles** (Ch 24 §4.4) | a revealed pagan text | **Proclus, Damascius, Psellos** — the school that treated it as scripture | the preservers are the authorisers; the text survives as they read it |
| **Vindhyavāsin** (Ch 25 §8) | a Sāṃkhya reformer | **Paramārtha's *Life of Vasubandhu*** — the Buddhists he defeated | his victory is the setup for their champion's revenge |
| **Makkhali Gosāla** (here) | an entire school's founder | **Jain and Buddhist polemic** | both schools' central doctrine is the one he denied |

The three channels have *different* interests — reverence, rivalry, refutation — and the corpus's obligation is the same in all three: **name the channel and size the claim to it.**

What that means in practice, applied here. Some things a hostile source is *good* evidence for, and some it is not:

| reasonably safe | to be held loosely |
|---|---|
| **that the doctrine existed** and was worth attacking — opponents do not invent a rival's position out of nothing when their audience knows the rival | the doctrine's **most damning formulations** — "we are powerless; suffering is pre-destined" is how a critic states a fatalism, not necessarily how a fatalist does |
| the **broad shape**: niyati, effort-independence, the fixed schedule — attested by two mutually independent hostile traditions, which is a genuine cross-check | the **biographical relations** — "Gosāla was Mahāvīra's disciple" is a claim by a rival's scripture about status, and status claims are what such texts are for |
| that the Ājīvikas were a **serious force** — they would not be attacked at this length otherwise | the **internal rationale**: what the Ājīvikas thought their ascetic practice was *for*, given that it changed nothing, is exactly what a hostile source has no reason to transmit |

That last row is the real loss. A tradition of ascetics who believed asceticism achieved nothing must have had an account of why they practised anyway — and that account is precisely what has not come down, because it is the part a critic would gain nothing by reporting. **What hostile transmission destroys first is a position's best version of itself.**

## 9. Why this matters {#why}

**Because it shows how far one move goes.** The substitution of process for substance is stated once in Ch 12 as *anattā*. Watching it applied to the person (§2), then to the object of knowledge (§3), then defended against a school that refused it (§6), shows that it is not a doctrine about souls but a general policy about what may be posited — and that the price is real: having removed enduring objects, Dharmakīrti's successors had to invent *niścaya-pratyaya* just to explain how anyone acts (§5.1).

**Because §4 is the corpus's cleanest case of a word that means opposite things.** *Pratyakṣa* marks the sensory floor for the Buddhist and the supersensory ceiling for the Jain, and is a battleground about universals between the Buddhist and the Naiyāyika. Three schools, one word, three incompatible referents — and none of them is being careless. That is what the typed honesty layer is for, and it is worth seeing a case where all three edges are needed at once.

**Because proximity generates polemic.** The two adversary sections are the chapter's most transferable lesson. Gosāla and Mahāvīra spent six years together; the Buddhist logicians and the Pratyabhijñā Śaivas shared a city and a technical vocabulary. Both pairs ended in total opposition, and in both cases the opponent's own tools were turned against him. A comparative method that treats shared vocabulary and close contact as evidence of convergence will get these two cases exactly backwards.

**And because §8 is a discipline, not a lament.** The honest response to a source with an interest is neither to discard it nor to trust it. It is to sort what it is good evidence for from what it is not — and to notice, specifically, that the thing hostile transmission destroys first is a position's strongest version. That is worth remembering wherever this corpus works from polemic, which is more often than it looks.

## 10. Flags {#flags}

| flag | why the confusion is tempting | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **nāma ≠ jīva** | both are "the mental side" opposite something physical | nāma is a **list of functions** in a process with no substrate; the jīva is an **eternal individual substance**. Equating them imports substance-realism into a process-analysis |
| **the 12 nidānas ≠ a simple causal chain** | the arrow diagram invites domino-reading | **DN 15 makes vijñāna and nāmarūpa mutually conditioning** — two sheaves of reeds. Neither is prior, which is precisely why no substrate is needed |
| **nāmarūpa ≠ the five khandhas restated** | the coverage is nearly the same | one is a **structural** analysis, the other a **causal-process** one; and *vijñāna* is pulled **out** of nāma into link 3 — the difference that makes the DN 15 loop expressible |
| **Buddhist pratyakṣa ≠ Nyāya pratyakṣa** | same word, same slot as "first pramāṇa" | Nyāya's *savikalpaka* (conceptual) perception is fully valid; the Buddhist excludes all conceptual content. The dispute **is** realism vs nominalism about universals |
| **Buddhist pratyakṣa ≠ Jain pratyakṣa** | same word, same role of naming direct knowledge | Jain *pratyakṣa* = **unmediated**, and so *includes omniscience* while **sensory cognition is *parokṣa***. The word marks the sensory **floor** in one system and the supersensory **ceiling** in the other |
| **savikalpaka ≠ blurry perception** | it sounds like a lesser degree of the same act | it is a **different cognitive act** — inference, not perception. Softening this collapses the two-pramāṇa system |
| **yogic perception ≠ conceptually enriched perception** | a religious epistemology usually privileges the adept | *yogijñāna* is still **nirvikalpaka**; it is more vivid and reliable, not more conceptual |
| **the Nyāyabindu ≠ the Pramāṇavārttika** | both Dharmakīrti, same system | the NB is the **compact teaching manual**; the PV is the ~2000-verse magnum opus. Citing the manual as the full argument misrepresents both |
| **Dharmottara's ṭīkā ≠ a transparent window on Dharmakīrti** | it is the only surviving Sanskrit commentary | it re-reads him through **Yogācāra**, demoting external-object realism to a conventional claim. The standard portal is an interpretation |
| **preserved ≠ central on the merits** | the NB/ṭīkā pair is canonical in Kashmir and Tibet | it is the **best-preserved Sanskrit text+commentary unit** of the tradition — availability shaped centrality, not only the reverse |
| **Pratyabhijñā ∥ Buddhist logic ≠ doctrinal convergence** | shared city, shared vocabulary, shared problems, and a stored `structurally-parallel-to` edge | **opposed on every row** — self, perception, universals, consciousness. The parallel is *engagement*; the co-optation of *svasaṃvedana* is a hostile act |
| **Ājīvika karma ≠ karma** | the word is retained | **stripped of all efficacy** — the exact inverse of the Jain effort-driven mechanism |
| **Ājīvika liberation ≠ mokṣa** | it is called liberation and does arrive | it arrives **on a fixed schedule regardless of conduct**; nothing you do brings it nearer |
| **"all three śramaṇa schools taught karma and rebirth" ≠ informative** | it is true | they disagree about **whether anything you do matters**, which is the question that determines what a soteriology is for |
| **two encyclopedia articles ≠ two sources** | they are separately titled and separately written | the same encyclopedia's linked pages, both descending from **the same two hostile texts**; consistency among descendants is not confirmation |
| **a hostile source's formulation ≠ the doctrine's own** | it is the only formulation we have | "we are powerless; suffering is pre-destined" is how a **critic** states a fatalism. What hostile transmission destroys first is a position's best version of itself |
